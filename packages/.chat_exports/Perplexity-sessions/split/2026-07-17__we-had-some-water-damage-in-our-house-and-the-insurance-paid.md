## Human

We had some water damage in our house, and the insurance paid for replacing all the hardwood. They moved our washing machine to do their work, and broke the buttons on the washing machine. They attempted to repair the buttons, but now they're squishy, and they're not 100% reliable. The washing machine is 20 years old. The insurance company offered $250, for us to buy our own washing machine, and when we complained they offered us $500, but in under Canadian law, or Alberta law, what is their requirement to compensate us?

## Assistant

Under Alberta law, their obligation is not to “buy you a new washer,” but to **put you back, as nearly as money can do it, in the position you were in before they damaged it**. That usually means paying the *actual cash value* (depreciated value) of a 20‑year‑old washer that still worked properly, or the reasonable cost to restore it to its prior functionality, whichever is less, not whatever arbitrary low amount they feel like.[1]

Below is how that plays out in practice, and how you can push back.

***

## Legal baseline: “Indemnity,” not a windfall

Alberta’s Insurance Act requires property policies to be contracts of **indemnity**, meaning you should be compensated for your loss but not better off than before.[1]

- In plain terms: before the contractor moved it, you had a 20‑year‑old washer with fully working, reliable buttons. After their work, you have a cosmetically and functionally degraded machine with unreliable, “squishy” controls. The **loss** is the value difference between those two states.[1]
- For scheduled personal property (buildings, contents, appliances), insurers normally apply a **replacement‑cost** or **actual cash value (ACV)** basis depending on your policy wording. Replacement cost ignores depreciation but requires you to actually replace the item; ACV is replacement cost minus depreciation. The Insurance Act allows this structure; the exact rule for you is in the policy, not in a stand‑alone statute about washers.[1]

FACT: There is **no Alberta or Canada statute** that says “if a 20‑year‑old appliance is damaged, you must pay X% of a new one.” The law instead enforces the general indemnity principle plus whatever method of valuation your policy sets out.[1]
CONSENSUS: In practice, insurers treat a 20‑year‑old washer as having low but non‑zero ACV if it’s still fully functional.

***

## Who is responsible: insurer vs. contractor

Even though the physical damage was caused by the contractor or mitigation company, they were acting for the insurer.

- Under ordinary negligence principles, **whoever damaged the washer is liable for the loss**, but your contract claim is against your insurer under your home policy; the insurer can subrogate against the contractor if it wants.[1]
- That means you do **not** have to chase the contractor personally as long as the damage occurred in the course of an insured repair job; your dispute is about whether your insurer is meeting its contractual indemnity obligation.

FACT: The key document here is your **home insurance policy wording** (and any applicable endorsements), not a special Alberta appliance statute.[1]

***

## How the amount should be determined

From a legal/claims handling perspective, “what do they owe?” usually follows this logic:

1. **Identify pre‑loss condition and value**  
   - A 20‑year‑old machine normally has low market value, but if it was working well, it still has some **usable value to you**.  
   - The relevant measure is typically:  
     - ACV = cost of a similar used washer of like age and condition, or  
     - replacement cost of a comparable modern machine minus depreciation, per policy terms.[1]

2. **Cost to repair vs. cost to replace**  
   - If repair can **restore full, reliable function and roughly the same utility** as pre‑loss, the insurer can usually choose to repair instead of replace, provided the repair is competent.[1]
   - But what you describe (squishy, not 100% reliable) is **not a restoration to pre‑loss condition**; that’s a *failed repair*.  
   - At that point, they should either:  
     - redo the repair properly at their expense; or  
     - compensate you for the washer’s ACV (or replacement cost, if that’s what your policy promises).

3. **Age and depreciation**  
   - From a pure valuation standpoint, many insurers would argue that the ACV of a 20‑year‑old washer is modest. Some internal depreciation schedules would put it near zero after 10–15 years.  
   - But the legal test is not “how old is it?”; it is **“what is the value of what you actually lost?”** A working, reliable washer has higher value than a non‑functional relic, regardless of age.

SPECULATION (medium confidence): For a 20‑year‑old unit, many adjusters would peg ACV somewhere in the low hundreds, often below the cost of a basic new unit, which is likely what is driving their $250–$500 offers. The exact figure is not prescribed by law; it is a negotiation informed by the policy and market values.

***

## Is $250 or $500 “enough” under Alberta law?

Legally, the standard is not a fixed dollar amount; it is whether the insurer has **reasonably indemnified** your loss under the contract.

You can assess that in a structured way:

- **Step 1 – Confirm your loss measure from the policy**  
  - Look at the “Basis of Claim Payment” or “Loss Settlement” section of your home policy. This will say if contents/appliances are on:  
    - Replacement cost; or  
    - ACV; or  
    - Replacement cost only if you actually replace, with ACV paid first then topped up after you submit proof of replacement.[1]

- **Step 2 – Get objective numbers**  
  - Find the cost of a **basic new washer** comparable in capacity and features to your old one (not a premium model).  
  - Check local used listings (Kijiji, Facebook Marketplace) for similar 15–20‑year‑old working washers to estimate **market value** of a functioning equivalent.  
  - If, say, used working units go for $200–$300, and a basic new one is $700–$900, then:  
    - ACV might reasonably be argued around that used‑market number;  
    - Replacement‑cost entitlement, if your policy gives it, could be up to the reasonable cost of a new basic model, minus any deductible structure.

- **Step 3 – Compare to their offer**  
  - If your policy is ACV‑only, and used functional equivalents are around $200–$300, then **$250 may be within a defendable range**, though you can argue up based on actual market data.  
  - If your policy is **replacement cost on contents**, and you intend to or already have replaced the washer, you can reasonably argue that **they should cover the full cost of a comparable new unit (subject to policy limits and deductible)** once you provide proof of purchase, with ACV paid upfront and the holdback released after replacement.[1]

FACT: Alberta law does not force them to pay the full price of a high‑end new machine, but if your policy is on replacement‑cost terms for contents, they normally must cover the reasonable cost of a comparable replacement once you actually replace.[1]

***

## How to push back effectively

Given your description, the strongest angle is **“not restored to pre‑loss condition” plus “policy basis of settlement”**, not “there’s a statute that says you must pay $X.”

Concrete steps:

1. **Pull and read your policy wording**  
   - Focus on:  
     - “Basis of Claim Payment” / “Loss Settlement”  
     - Any “Replacement Cost” endorsement for contents  
     - Definitions of “Actual Cash Value” and “Depreciation”  
   - This will give you your **contractual leverage**.

2. **Document the defect clearly**  
   - Take photos/videos showing:  
     - The squishy buttons;  
     - Any intermittent / unreliable operation (e.g., presses not registering).  
   - Make brief notes of what worked *before* and what does not work *now*.

3. **Get at least one independent repair estimate**  
   - Ask an appliance technician for a written opinion:  
     - Whether the control panel is properly repairable to reliable function;  
     - Estimated cost to repair correctly;  
     - Whether, given age and parts availability, replacement is more reasonable than repair.  
   - If the tech states that reliable repair is impractical or uneconomical, that strengthens the case that **replacement (on an ACV or replacement‑cost basis) is the appropriate measure of indemnity**.

4. **Escalate within the insurer**  
   - Write to the adjuster and, if needed, the supervisor or internal complaints contact, referencing:  
     - Your policy clause on loss settlement;  
     - The fact that their contractor’s repair **did not restore full function**, so their obligation remains unsatisfied;  
     - Any data on used washer values and repair estimates.  
   - Example framing:  
     > “Before your contractor moved it, we had a fully functional washer with reliable controls. After the work and attempted repair, the controls are degraded and unreliable. Your contractor’s repair has not restored the appliance to its pre‑loss condition. Under the indemnity principle and the [‘Loss Settlement’ / ‘Replacement Cost’] clause of my policy, I am entitled to be put back in my pre‑loss position, either by proper repair or by payment of the fair [ACV / replacement cost] of a comparable washer. Based on market data and the attached repair opinion, $250 / $500 does not reflect that value.”

5. **Use the insurer’s complaint process and regulators if needed**

   - All insurers in Alberta must have a **formal complaint escalation process** leading to an internal ombuds office.  
   - If you remain unsatisfied after that, you can bring the matter to the **General Insurance OmbudService (GIO)** or complain to the **Alberta Insurance Council** or **Alberta Superintendent of Insurance**, who oversee market conduct (fair claims handling), though they do not set individual dollar amounts.[1]
   - For relatively small disputes, **Provincial Court (Civil Division)** small claims is also an option, where a judge would look at your evidence of value and the policy.

FACT: Oversight bodies focus on whether the insurer applied the contract fairly and transparently; they will not re‑write dollar numbers from scratch but can pressure the insurer to justify or revise an unreasonable offer.[1]

***

## How to think about “fair” in your case

Based on what you’ve said and typical practice:

- If your policy is **ACV on contents**, the legally required compensation is likely **around the fair market value of a functioning 20‑year‑old washer of similar type in your area**, which you can evidence via used listings and any expert opinion.  
- If your policy is **replacement cost on contents**, and you replace the washer with a reasonable, broadly comparable new model, the insurer’s legal obligation is usually to cover **that full reasonable cost (up to policy limits), less deductible**, once you supply proof, even though your old unit was 20 years old.[1]
- A **failed repair** does not fulfill their obligation; either they must repair properly or compensate you consistent with the policy’s settlement terms.
