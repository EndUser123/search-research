## UNDRIP and the 2019 law

In 2019, B.C.’s NDP government passed legislation to implement the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), publicly insisting it would not create a veto over provincial laws or private property rights.  Ministers at the time said it would not create new rights or allow courts to strike down B.C. laws or reclassify private land as Aboriginal title.[1]

## The recent court decision

On Dec. 5, the B.C. Court of Appeal struck down the Mineral Tenure Act on the basis that it conflicted with UNDRIP, and held that any B.C. law or government action could be overturned if it does not align with the declaration.  The court treated UNDRIP, once incorporated into B.C. law, as the interpretive standard through which all provincial legislation must be read.[1]

## Eby’s role and political backlash

Premier David Eby, who had championed UNDRIP, now says the courts have gone too far and that amendments are needed because UNDRIP was intended as a guiding framework, not a superior law placing courts “in the driver’s seat.”  Critics point to a 2021 amendment Eby pushed through the Interpretation Act requiring every B.C. law to be interpreted as consistent with UNDRIP, which legal analysts had already warned could have unpredictable effects.[1]

## Related Indigenous rights case

Separately from UNDRIP, an August B.C. Supreme Court ruling placed a large area of privately owned land in Richmond under Cowichan Tribes jurisdiction and held that Aboriginal title can override existing guarantees of private land titles.  This decision has compounded public anxiety about land rights and long‑term certainty for property owners and investors.[1]

## Assistant

Yes. In Gitxaala v. British Columbia, the B.C. Court of Appeal held that UNDRIP now has “immediate legal effect” in the province through the Declaration Act and the Interpretation Act, meaning B.C. statutes are presumed to be interpreted consistently with UNDRIP, and it found the old free‑entry mineral claim system inconsistent because it allowed staking without prior consultation.  This dramatically raises the legal bar for new mining and infrastructure projects, since permitting, land‑use planning, and other regulatory decisions must now be measured against UNDRIP standards, which increases consultation obligations, litigation risk, and uncertainty for investors even as the province is actively courting critical‑minerals development.  In response, Premier David Eby’s government has announced plans to amend the Declaration Act to clarify that aligning provincial laws with UNDRIP should be primarily a political and negotiated task rather than one driven by courts, even as the B.C. Human Rights Commissioner and Indigenous organizations welcome the rulings as a concrete enforcement of the reconciliation framework.[1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8][9]

At the same time, the Cowichan Tribes v. Canada decision recognized Aboriginal title over roughly 800 acres in Richmond, including lands held by governments and private fee‑simple owners, and found that some Crown grants and municipal interests are defective or unjustified infringements of that title.  The court suspended parts of its declarations to give governments time to negotiate, but the ruling still signals that Aboriginal title can overlap and, in some respects, be “prior and senior” to existing property interests, forcing the province to reconcile fee‑simple titles and public infrastructure with Indigenous ownership in that specific area.  This has amplified concerns among municipalities, landowners, and developers about long‑term security of title and the potential for similar claims elsewhere, even though legal commentators stress the decision is highly fact‑specific and does not automatically unsettle private property across the province.[10][11][12][13][14]

Together, Gitxaala and Cowichan mark a decisive shift in B.C. toward legally enforceable Indigenous rights rather than purely political commitments, with UNDRIP acting as a minimum standard for interpreting laws and Aboriginal title being recognized even in heavily developed urban settings.  Indigenous nations and rights advocates view this as long‑overdue implementation of constitutional and international obligations, while industry groups warn of a chill on investment and opposition parties press the government to either roll back or tightly circumscribe the reach of the Declaration Act.  The policy challenge for B.C. is now how to redesign its mineral tenure, land‑use, and consultation regimes to reduce legal uncertainty without retreating from commitments to shared decision‑making and the honour of the Crown.[3][4][5][15][12][7][9][16][1]
