This should be done with a qualified employment lawyer licensed where your son lives (Alberta) and where he will work (New York / Florida law in this case), because those systems interact in non‑obvious ways.[1][2]

Below is a practical framework you can use to review this package with counsel.

## 1. Map the document set

Start by listing what each document covers and how they fit together, so you know what you are actually agreeing to:  
- Offer Letter: role (Quant Research Analyst), location (New York), compensation (base, bonus, signing bonus, guarantees), at‑will status, notice requirement, Florida governing law and Miami courts, and conditions precedent (immigration, background check, signing of IP/NC etc.).[2]
- IP Protection and Restrictive Covenant Agreement: confidentiality, IP ownership, non‑disparagement, non‑solicit of clients and employees, mandatory mediation/arbitration in Miami under AAA rules, Florida law, and definitions (Cause, Competitive Activity, Competitive Enterprise, Confidential Information, etc.).[1]
- Agreement Not to Compete + Appendix A: post‑employment non‑compete duration, restricted period payments, and mechanics of notice/employer elections.[3][4]
- Benefits summary: health, disability, time off, parental leave, 401(k) and other benefits; these are generally not contractual guarantees and can be changed by the employer.[5]
- Relocation / early‑career policy: relocation allowance, immigration support, tax assistance, repayment obligations if he leaves or is terminated for cause within 12 months.[6]

Have counsel confirm whether anything else (e.g., incentive plan documents) will be provided later that could add further restrictions or clawbacks.

## 2. Role, pay, guarantees, and clawbacks

With a lawyer or accountant, go line‑by‑line through the economic terms:  
- Base salary, bonus / incentive awards, and 2026 guarantee (USD 350k total comp target, with USD 250k base and USD 100k guaranteed incentive, prorated for partial year).[2]
- Signing bonus and any “Hiring Awards”: when they pay, over what period they are “earned,” and circumstances where he must repay them (resignation or termination for Cause within the repayment period). These clawbacks can be very significant and interact with immigration risk.[2]
- Early separation payment: how much he would receive if terminated without Cause before the 2026 incentive is actually paid, the timing of the two installments, and the conditions (signing a release; compliance with all covenants).[2]
- Tax and cross‑border issues: how US income, bonuses, and equity/UMI awards will be taxed given he is Canadian, and whether there’s any tax gross‑up beyond relocation.[6][2]

For a Canadian citizen moving to New York, have cross‑border tax counsel model: after‑tax cash flow in years 1–2, impact of Canadian residency ties, and the potential tax consequences if he leaves the US earlier than expected.

## 3. Immigration and relocation risks

Because this is “American employment” for a Canadian moving to the US, immigration and relocation clauses deserve special scrutiny:  
- Immigration contingency: the offer is expressly contingent on obtaining proper work authorization in the US and satisfying registrations/licensing; relocation benefits can be withdrawn if visas/work permits are refused.[6][2]
- Relocation policy: scope of covered expenses (visa costs, travel, household goods, temporary housing, relocation allowance of about USD 10,000 net, tax gross‑up rules), and his obligation to repay all relocation benefits if he resigns or is terminated for Cause within 12 months.[6]
- Family status: rules for classifying dependents for immigration and benefits purposes; some countries’ (and the US’s) limits on recognizing partners, and consequences for partner work authorization.[6]

Ask immigration counsel: which visa path is expected (e.g., TN, E‑3, H‑1B, etc.), contingency if that category is unavailable or delayed, and what happens to the employment/relocation/bonus obligations if immigration fails or is withdrawn mid‑employment.

## 4. At‑will employment, notice, and Cause

Understand the practical job‑security picture:  
- At‑will employment: the firm can terminate employment at any time, with or without Cause; your son can also resign, but must give one‑month written notice, which the firm can waive (with no pay for waived portion).[2]
- Definition of Cause: located in Exhibit A of the IP Agreement; it is quite broad (includes misconduct, policy breaches, certain performance failures, inaccurate disclosures, regulatory issues, and more), and losing guaranteed or earned payments often hinges on whether the firm asserts Cause.[1][2]
- Interplay with non‑compete and payments: termination for Cause can affect eligibility for non‑compete restricted period payments, hiring award retention, early separation payments, and potentially immigration/relocation.[3][1][2]

Have counsel stress‑test the Cause definition: ask for practical scenarios (e.g., weak performance vs. misconduct, inadvertent policy breach) and how often Cause is invoked in practice, and whether any language could be softened.

## 5. Non‑compete and non‑solicit obligations

This is one of the most important sections to analyze in depth, especially for someone early in career and mobile across countries:  
- Non‑compete duration and elections: post‑employment “Restricted Period” of up to 15 months, at Citadel’s election within 10 days of employment ending; this can expand to a 24‑month maximum once his total annual compensation reaches USD 1,000,000, and that higher cap never comes back down.[4]
- Restricted period payments: schedule of monthly payments tied to length of the Restricted Period (fractions of salary for months 1–6, fixed dollar amounts beyond that), and conditions under which payments stop or must be repaid (e.g., breach of the agreement or other contracts).[4][3]
- Scope of “Competitive Activity” and “Competitive Enterprise”: defined broadly to cover not only direct trading roles but any capacity where his work is similar to his Citadel role or could reasonably draw on Citadel’s confidential information, including certain investments or planning a competitive venture.[1]
- Client and employee non‑solicit: 12‑month client non‑solicit and 24‑month employee non‑solicit post‑employment, covering anyone he had “material contact” with or confidential information about, and all Citadel employees defined in the agreement.[1]

With a lawyer licensed in New York and familiar with Florida‑law non‑competes, ask:  
- How enforceable is this under New York law where he will physically work, versus under Florida law as chosen in the contract?  
- How would this be viewed if he returns to Canada and works for a Canadian or US‑based competitor from Calgary or Toronto?  
- Are there realistic modifications (e.g., narrowing geography, scope, or length) that the firm has historically accepted for early‑career hires?

## 6. Arbitration, Florida law, and forum

The dispute‑resolution and choice‑of‑law framework is unusually important because he will live and work in New York, is Canadian, and the firm has selected Florida law and Miami forums:  
- Mandatory mediation and binding arbitration: nearly all disputes related to employment must go first to confidential mediation, then confidential arbitration in Miami under AAA Commercial Rules, with a waiver of jury trial.[1]
- Fee‑shifting and escrow: the claimant may have to escrow up to 50% of his highest annual total compensation from the past three years to secure potential fee awards, and the “prevailing party” is entitled to reasonable legal fees and costs.[1]
- Florida governing law and Miami courts: even where court proceedings are allowed (e.g., injunctive relief), both sides consent to exclusive jurisdiction and venue in Miami state and federal courts, and waive arguments that Florida is an inconvenient forum.[3][2][1]

Ask counsel:  
- How would these clauses interact with New York employment protections and any mandatory Canadian law if he ever litigated from Canada?  
- Are there any mandatory rights in New York or federal US law that cannot be waived (e.g., certain collective/class claims, specific statutory rights)?  
- What practical leverage does an individual associate have if a fee‑shifting clause and escrow requirement apply?

## 7. IP ownership, confidentiality, and generative AI

For a quantitative researcher, the IP and confidentiality terms are crucial for future mobility and side projects:  
- Work‑for‑hire and assignment: all IP created during employment that relates to Citadel’s current or anticipated business is owned exclusively by the employer; exceptions for inventions developed entirely on one’s own time without company resources are narrowed if they relate to Citadel’s business or stem from employment work.[1]
- Confidential information scope: includes strategies, models, data, tools, compensation information, client identities, recruiting strategies, and “Citadel Generative AI Materials” (inputs/outputs using generative AI connected to business activities).[1]
- Post‑employment restrictions: ongoing confidentiality, return/destruction of all materials, obligations to notify future employers of restrictions, and cooperation duties in legal/regulatory matters.[1]

Your son should discuss with counsel and, potentially, with the firm:  
- Whether any pre‑existing personal projects, open‑source work, or IP need to be disclosed and carved out.  
- How generative‑AI‑related work at Citadel might affect his ability to later work on similar tools or prompts at another employer or on independent research.

## 8. Benefits, time off, leave, and disability

Benefits are generous for a US employer but are generally not guaranteed and may differ from Canadian expectations:  
- Health, dental, vision, and other medical programs; mental‑health support; fertility/adoption benefits; and travel/fitness benefits.[5]
- Vacation and time off structure (e.g., 3 vs. 4 weeks based on experience, NYSE holidays, rollover rules, sick leave, and parental leave structure).[5]
- Disability insurance, life insurance, and 401(k) with discretionary employer match.[5]

With a Canadian lens, it helps to:  
- Compare these to his current or anticipated Canadian benefits and public coverage.  
- Confirm waiting periods, pre‑existing condition exclusions, and whether coverage starts on day one.  

## 9. Canada–US interaction and planning questions

Finally, frame explicit cross‑border planning questions for your Canadian‑side and US‑side advisors:  
- Residency and tax: When will he cease to be tax‑resident in Canada, and will he maintain significant residential ties? What are the implications for Canadian departure tax and treaty relief?  
- Provincial employment standards vs. US law: If he later returns to Alberta and a dispute arises, could any mandatory Alberta employment protections or non‑compete rules come into play?  
- Mobility: If he wants to move back to Canada and work in the same industry, how do the Florida‑law non‑compete and non‑solicits apply, practically and legally, when he is physically in Canada?  
- Enforcement risk and negotiation: Are there standard modifications that can be negotiated pre‑signing for Canadian hires (e.g., explicit carve‑outs for future employment in Canada, narrowing of arbitration cost‑shifting, or limits on non‑compete length for early‑career employees)?
