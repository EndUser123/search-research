## Human

I have adhd, I don't know what type.  I'm 56 years old.  Why do I get so very tired in the afternoon around 2pm?

## Assistant

Most likely you’re hitting a mix of “normal human circadian slump” plus ADHD‑related factors (sleep quality, mental overexertion, dopamine/cortisol rhythm, blood sugar), and at 56 any other medical issues (thyroid, anemia, apnea, meds) can amplify that. It’s very common for people with ADHD to feel disproportionately wiped out in the early afternoon, but it’s still worth checking for non‑ADHD causes as well.[1][2][3][4][5][6]

I’ll break this into: what’s “normal,” what ADHD adds on top, what else to rule out, and what you can experiment with.

***

## The “normal” 2 p.m. crash

Most people without ADHD have a natural drop in alertness in the early afternoon.

- Your circadian rhythm has a built‑in dip in the early afternoon, which shows up as sleepiness and lower energy even if you slept fine.[4]
- If you’ve been up since 6–7 a.m., by 2 p.m. you’ve already done 7–8 hours of decision‑making, social interaction, and mental work, so some fatigue is expected.  
- A high‑carb or heavy lunch (sandwiches, pasta, sweets, sugary drinks) can spike blood sugar and then drop it 1–3 hours later, which feels like brain fog and sleepiness.[4]

So part of what you’re feeling is probably a real biological slump that everyone has; the question is why it’s **so** strong for you.

***

## How ADHD makes that slump much worse

ADHD doesn’t list “fatigue” as a core symptom, but in practice many adults with ADHD report chronic tiredness and daytime sleepiness. Several mechanisms stack together:[2][5][1]

1. **Sleep quality and ADHD brains**  
   - Adults with ADHD have higher rates of insomnia, delayed sleep phase (can’t fall asleep until very late), restless legs, and sleep apnea.[3][1]
   - Studies show adults with ADHD are objectively sleepier during the day and this daytime sleepiness directly impairs focus and performance.[3]
   - If you consistently get less or more fragmented sleep than you think (e.g., apnea, frequent micro‑awakenings), the “normal” 2 p.m. dip becomes a brick wall.  

2. **Dopamine and alertness**  
   - People with ADHD tend to have lower baseline dopamine activity, which is involved in motivation and feeling awake.[5]
   - Dopamine also helps regulate sleep–wake cycles, so lower dopamine can mean both worse sleep and more trouble staying alert during the day.[5]
   - Around mid‑afternoon your cortisol naturally drops; when you combine that with already‑low dopamine, the brain can suddenly feel like someone turned the lights off.[7]

3. **Mental effort and “executive fatigue”**  
   - ADHD makes planning, prioritizing, switching tasks, and resisting distraction much more effortful; your brain is burning more mental energy to achieve the same output.[6][2]
   - By 2 p.m., you may have done 6+ hours of “executive function heavy lifting,” even if it doesn’t look like extreme productivity from the outside, which leaves you drained.  
   - Hyperfocus (if you get it) can make this worse: you may skip breaks, ignore hunger/thirst, and then hit a wall when the focus state collapses.[1][2]

4. **Emotion and stress load**  
   - Anxiety and depression are common with ADHD and both significantly increase perceived fatigue.[1][5]
   - Minor stressors accumulate through the morning; with ADHD‑style emotional sensitivity, the nervous system can be more fried by early afternoon than you’d expect.[2]

5. **If you’re on ADHD meds** (ignore this if unmedicated)  
   - Stimulant meds often peak in the morning and taper off around midday or early afternoon, especially immediate‑release or a single morning dose.[8][7]
   - The natural circadian drop can line up with medication wear‑off, leading to a sharp “cliff” in energy and mood rather than a gentle dip.[8][7]
   - Higher productivity while medicated can also mean you simply used more energy earlier in the day, so the crash feels larger.[8]

Even without knowing your ADHD type, these mechanisms can all apply; the fatigue is more about sleep, regulation, and load than about subtype labels.

***

## Other things to actively rule out at 56

At your age it’s important not to attribute everything to ADHD. Several common conditions can cause or worsen afternoon exhaustion:

- **Sleep apnea**: Very common in middle‑aged adults, especially with snoring or waking unrefreshed; strongly associated with daytime sleepiness.[3][4]
- **Thyroid issues, anemia, diabetes, low B12, low testosterone**: All can show up as afternoon fatigue and brain fog.[4]
- **Medications**: Antihistamines, some blood‑pressure meds, certain antidepressants, and others can cause daytime tiredness as a side effect.[4]
- **Chronic pain or inflammatory conditions**: Subtle but persistent pain or inflammation also drains energy reserves over the day.[4]

From a medical perspective, “suddenly very tired at 2 p.m. every day” is common, but **if it is severe, persistent, or worsening**, a primary‑care visit with basic labs and a sleep discussion is warranted. That’s especially true given your ADHD and age.

***

## Concrete experiments you can try

These are low‑risk, ADHD‑friendly tweaks you can run as experiments over 1–2 weeks each, while still planning to discuss with a clinician.

### 1. Map the pattern

- For 7–10 days, note: wake time, bedtime, estimated hours slept, when you take any meds/caffeine, what/when you eat, and time/intensity of the crash (0–10 scale).  
- Patterns often jump out: late‑night screen use → short sleep → worse crashes; heavy lunch → sharper slump; no breaks in the morning → bigger 2 p.m. crash.  

### 2. Sleep hygiene tailored to ADHD

Because ADHD brains often drift late and fight sleep, aim for **realistic** improvements, not perfection.[5][1][3]

- Fixed **wake time** every day is more powerful than obsessing over bedtime; the brain gradually adjusts.  
- Cut stimulating screens or “deep dive” tasks 60–90 minutes before target sleep, swap to low‑effort, low‑brightness stuff.  
- If snoring, waking choking/gasping, or feeling unrefreshed despite long sleep, push hard for a sleep apnea evaluation.  

### 3. Adjust food and caffeine around noon

- Test a **lighter, higher‑protein lunch** with some fat and fiber and fewer fast carbs (e.g., chicken + salad + nuts instead of big sandwich + fries). Blood sugar swings soften and so does the crash.[7][4]
- Time caffeine so your *last* dose is late morning, not mid‑afternoon, to avoid disrupting night sleep.  
- Try a small protein‑heavy snack at ~1:30 p.m. (Greek yogurt, nuts, boiled eggs, protein shake) and see if that blunts the 2–3 p.m. dip.  

### 4. Change how you use your mornings

- Assume you have a **finite mental battery**; schedule your most demanding or important work for the morning while energy and dopamine support are higher.[6][7]
- Push routine admin, email, low‑stakes tasks to after the slump, when you’re naturally less sharp.  
- Insert 5–10 minute movement breaks every 60–90 minutes in the morning (walk, stretch, stairs); this can delay and soften the afternoon crash.[4]

### 5. If you’re on ADHD meds

If you are medicated (you didn’t say):

- Track exactly when doses are taken and when you crash; show that timeline to your prescriber.  
- Sometimes shifting timing, changing formulation (e.g., XR vs IR, or adding a small booster dose), or addressing sleep improves the afternoon dramatically.[7][8]
- Do not adjust doses on your own; use what you notice as data for your clinician.  

***

## When to bring this to a doctor

I’d treat a 2 p.m. crash as “needs medical input” if:

- You’re fighting sleep hard, or briefly dozing at your desk or in meetings.  
- You wake unrefreshed more than a few times per week despite adequate time in bed.  
- Fatigue is new, significantly worse than a year or two ago, or associated with weight change, low mood, or other new symptoms.  

In that visit, I’d explicitly mention ADHD, the time of day of the crash, and ask about: sleep apnea screening, thyroid panel, CBC (for anemia), fasting glucose/A1c, B12, and a medication side‑effect review, so they don’t just shrug it off.

## How Vyvanse usually behaves vs what you feel

Vyvanse (lisdexamfetamine) is a long‑acting prodrug: your body slowly converts it to dextroamphetamine, which typically gives 10–14 hours of therapeutic effect in adults from a **single morning dose**.[4][1]

But in real life:

- Some adults get only 6–8 good hours, others get 12–14; metabolism and individual sensitivity make a big difference.[1]
- Onset is slow: effects typically start ~1.5 hours after taking it and peak around 3.5–6 hours.[5][6][1]
- The FDA label and most guidelines treat Vyvanse as a **once‑daily, morning** medication, not something you keep stacking all day.[7][3]

So if you take 30 mg in the morning, the drug is still very much in your system at 2 p.m.—but your *subjective* focus and energy may have dipped compared with the morning peak. A 20 mg Vyvanse at noon will:[3][1]

- Take 1–2 hours to even start working.  
- Peak more like 3–5 p.m., and still be active into the evening.[5][1]

That delay is one reason you don’t feel it helping much **at** the 2 p.m. crash.

***

## Why the noon Vyvanse might not help your 2 p.m. crash

Putting this together with ADHD and your age, a few likely factors:

1. **Timing mismatch**  
   - You crash about 2 p.m. The noon Vyvanse is barely starting to do anything by then; its peak help arrives closer to late afternoon.[5][1]
   - Meanwhile the 30 mg morning dose is leaving its peak window, your circadian rhythm is naturally dipping, and your brain has done a full morning of work.  

2. **Stacking long‑acting on long‑acting**  
   - Vyvanse has a long half‑life (active d‑amphetamine ~10–13 hours).[1]
   - Adding another 20 mg at noon doesn’t give a quick “edge”; it extends and raises the overall curve, which you might experience more as “wired but tired” or just no obvious benefit.  

3. **Your crash might be more about sleep / load than med level**  
   - If your morning is cognitively intense, you may simply be **depleted** by early afternoon; more stimulant doesn’t always fix that, it can even make the drained feeling more obvious.  
   - Any sleep issues (even mild apnea, restless sleep, late nights) can turn the normal circadian dip into a wall, regardless of stimulant levels.[8][3]

4. **Age and comorbidities matter**  
   - At 56, things like blood pressure meds, subtle thyroid issues, mild anemia, or glucose regulation can all interact with stimulants and your energy curve.[9][3]
   - In that case, tweaking Vyvanse helps less than addressing the underlying medical factor.  

So the fact that the noon 20 mg doesn’t help much is not a failure on your part; it may simply be the wrong tool/shape for the problem.

I’ll keep this focused on realistic, medically standard adjustments—**nothing here is a DIY change; it’s fodder for a detailed conversation with your clinician.**[2][10][11][3]

### 1. Re‑think the noon Vyvanse

Given how Vyvanse works, possibilities include:

- **Move the main dose later**:  
  - Example: instead of 30 mg at 6–7 a.m., take it a bit later (if your schedule allows) so peak coverage overlaps your 2 p.m. block better.  
  - Trade‑off: you may lose some very early‑morning sharpness, and if taken too late it can hurt sleep.  

- **Change the split** rather than adding a big noon dose:  
  - Some doctors use a slightly lower morning Vyvanse plus a small **short‑acting amphetamine or methylphenidate booster** around 11–12, which kicks in faster and is gone by evening.[10][11][2]
  - Example pattern your prescriber might consider:  
    - Vyvanse 30 mg in the morning  
    - 5–10 mg of an immediate‑release amphetamine at 11–12 as a booster instead of 20 mg Vyvanse.  
  - Because the booster has faster onset, it can actually touch the 2 p.m. slump.  

- **Increase the morning Vyvanse instead of adding noon dose**:  
  - For some people, going from 30 → 40 mg once daily gives more stable all‑day coverage and less perceived “crash” without needing a second dose.[3][1]
  - But this depends heavily on your side effects, blood pressure, anxiety, and sleep.  

### 2. Check whether this is rebound vs just tired

Two different afternoon patterns:

- **Rebound/wear‑off**: irritability, mood dip, racing or scattered thoughts, craving carbs or sugar, sometimes a sharp contrast between “on” and “off.”[2][3]
- **Plain fatigue**: you’re just exhausted, possibly sleepy, with low motivation but not necessarily more impulsive or emotional.  

If what you feel at 2 p.m. is more “my brain is sand” than “med crash mood storm,” then:

- The core problem may be sleep quality, workload pacing, lunch, or another medical factor.  
- Med tweaks help some, but they won’t fix chronic under‑sleep or untreated apnea, etc.

I have a very very messy room, and even when I really try to do stuff that I don't want to do, I can't do it.  What part of adhd is this called?

## Assistant

The pattern you’re describing is usually called **executive dysfunction**, and more specifically problems with **task initiation** and organization, not laziness or lack of willpower. In ADHD terms, it’s your executive functions (planning, starting, sequencing, prioritizing) getting overwhelmed, so your brain “freezes” instead of letting you just clean the room.[1][2][3][4][5][6]

***

## The ADHD “part” this fits under

Clinically they don’t list “messy room” as a symptom, but it’s a downstream effect of core executive‑function issues. The main pieces are:[2][3][5]

- **Executive dysfunction**: the umbrella term for trouble planning, organizing, starting, and finishing tasks.[5][2]
- **Task initiation problems**: difficulty beginning a task even when you *want* to and know exactly what to do.[4][6][7]
- **Working‑memory limits**: holding multiple steps in mind (“pick clothes up, sort, fold, put away…”) is hard, so the whole thing feels massive and vague.[3][1]
- **Interest‑based nervous system**: boring, non‑urgent tasks (like cleaning) don’t generate enough dopamine for your brain to “turn on,” so you hit what researchers call **task paralysis**, not regular procrastination.[6][7]

So when you say “even when I really try to do stuff I don’t want to do, I can’t do it,” that’s classic **ADHD task paralysis / task initiation failure** inside executive dysfunction, not a moral failing.[7][4][6]

***

## Why a messy room is such a strong signal in ADHD

Cleaning a messy room looks simple from the outside, but it’s actually a perfect storm for ADHD brains:

- The instruction “clean your room” is **vague and multi‑step**, which is hard for weak executive functioning.[8][3]
- You have to constantly **decide what to do next** (trash, laundry, surfaces, sorting), which burns a lot of executive capacity.[3][5]
- There’s very little **immediate reward** compared to the effort, so dopamine doesn’t show up to help.[1][6]
- You see the whole mess at once, feel overwhelmed, and your brain protects itself by freezing or escaping into something more stimulating.[4][6][7]

That’s why many ADHD resources explicitly link messiness and clutter with **executive dysfunction + task initiation difficulty**, not with intelligence, effort, or character.[2][5][1]

***

## A couple of naming options you can use

If you want language to bring to a doctor or therapist, you can say things like:

- “I have **severe executive dysfunction around home tasks**, especially cleaning and organizing.”[5][2]
- “I experience **task paralysis** with boring or overwhelming chores even when I’m motivated to do them.”[6][7]
- “I struggle with **task initiation and planning**, not with understanding what needs doing.”[9][4]

Those phrases line up with how the ADHD literature and executive‑function coaches talk about this problem.[7][2][4][6]

## Human

what medications help with executive dysfunction?

## Assistant

There isn’t one “executive dysfunction pill,” but several ADHD medications have decent evidence that they improve executive functions like starting tasks, organizing, and working memory for many people.  The main groups are stimulants (which you’re already on with Vyvanse) and non‑stimulants like atomoxetine or guanfacine; meds help the brain’s capacity, but they usually need to be paired with behavioral strategies to really change day‑to‑day functioning.[1][2][3][4][5][6]

I’ll keep this concise and focused on the “what helps” question.

***

## 1. Stimulant medications

Stimulants are first‑line for ADHD and have the strongest overall evidence for improving core symptoms and many aspects of executive function.  They mainly work by increasing dopamine and norepinephrine in frontal brain circuits involved in attention, inhibition, and working memory.[7][2][3][4][6]

Common stimulant classes:

- **Methylphenidate‑based** (Ritalin, Concerta, etc.)  
  - Meta‑analyses show chronic methylphenidate use improves several executive domains (attention, inhibition, working memory, processing speed) versus placebo.[3][4]
  - It’s often the most prescribed stimulant group worldwide and is considered a standard option alongside amphetamines.[4]

- **Amphetamine‑based** (Adderall, Dexedrine, Vyvanse/lisdexamfetamine)  
  - Amphetamine stimulants also show small to medium positive effects on executive function in adults.[6][3][4]
  - A review of adult ADHD treatments found lisdexamfetamine and other amphetamines improved executive measures compared with placebo.[3][6]

In practice: these meds can make it **easier to hold steps in mind, resist distractions, and actually get started**, which indirectly reduces the “I can’t make myself do it” wall.  But they don’t usually eliminate every executive problem, especially under stress or in boring tasks.[2][1][4][6]

***

## 2. Non‑stimulant ADHD medications

Non‑stimulants are options when stimulants aren’t enough, aren’t tolerated, or you need extra coverage; they also show real (though often slightly smaller) benefits on executive function.[8][5][1][4][6][3]

Key ones:

- **Atomoxetine (Strattera)**  
  - Selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor; used as a non‑stimulant ADHD med.[5][8][4]
  - A meta‑analysis found atomoxetine and methylphenidate produced **comparable improvements** in executive functions like attention, inhibition, and working memory, with no significant difference in overall effect size.[4][3]
  - Another review of adult ADHD treatments highlighted atomoxetine as having some of the strongest evidence for improving executive deficits in adults.[6]

- **Guanfacine (Intuniv) and clonidine (Kapvay)** – alpha‑2 agonists  
  - Often used when stimulants are not tolerated, or as add‑ons; they modulate noradrenergic signalling in prefrontal cortex.[8][5][3]
  - Evidence suggests they can help attention and inhibition and can reduce impulsivity and emotional reactivity, which are part of executive control.[5][3][6]
  - They may be especially helpful when anxiety, hyperarousal, or sleep issues are prominent.[8][5]

Overall: non‑stimulants typically have a slower onset (weeks), and effect sizes for executive function are in the small‑to‑moderate range but similar to stimulants in some analyses.[3][4][6]

***

## 3. Antidepressants sometimes used off‑label

Some antidepressants that affect norepinephrine and dopamine can help ADHD‑related executive issues in some adults, especially when depression or anxiety are also present.[2][5][6][8]

- **Bupropion (Wellbutrin)**  
  - Norepinephrine–dopamine reuptake inhibitor; sometimes used off‑label for ADHD in adults, particularly when depression is comorbid or stimulants aren’t suitable.[2][8]
  - Evidence suggests modest improvements in attention and impulse control, which can support executive functioning.[6][2]

These are not first‑line for pure ADHD, but they can be valuable in the right clinical picture.[8][2][6]

***

## 4. What medication can and cannot do for executive dysfunction

From the literature and clinical reviews:

- **Can do:**  
  - Improve attention, inhibition, and working memory, making it *possible* to plan, sequence steps, and start tasks more reliably.[1][4][2][3][6]
  - Reduce the frequency and intensity of complete “task paralysis” episodes, especially when they’re driven by distractibility and working‑memory overload.[1][2][3][6]

- **Cannot do (by itself):**  
  - Teach you systems for organizing, breaking tasks down, or prioritizing; that’s where coaching, CBT, or structured tools come in.[5][2][6]
  - Completely eliminate executive dysfunction in every context; even on optimal meds, people with ADHD often still benefit from environmental supports and routines.[2][5][6]

A review aimed at clinicians concluded that current adult ADHD treatments (stimulant and non‑stimulant) **do** show positive effects on executive function, but the best results come when medication is combined with behavioral strategies like CBT or executive‑function coaching.[5][6][2]

***

## How this fits your situation

Given you’re already on Vyvanse:

- The fact that you still feel intense task paralysis around things like your room doesn’t mean meds “aren’t working”; it often means you’re hitting the remaining 20–40% of executive dysfunction that meds only partially touch.[6][2][5]
- Sometimes adding or switching to a different stimulant, or adding a non‑stimulant like atomoxetine or guanfacine, can shift things further—but the gains tend to be incremental, not magical.[3][4][8][5][6]

Are silent migranes with aura a know side effect with these drugs?

## Assistant

**Headaches are a known side effect of Vyvanse and the other ADHD meds we discussed, but silent migraines with aura (visual disturbances without headache pain) are not explicitly listed in official prescribing information or large clinical trials as a common or recognized side effect.**[1][2][3][4][5][6][7]

That said, there are **anecdotal reports and some indirect links** worth noting, so it’s worth tracking and discussing with your doctor.

***

## What the official data says

- **Vyvanse (lisdexamfetamine):**  
  - Headaches are a recognized side effect (reported in ~1–10% of users in trials), often mild and in the back of the head, sometimes as rebound when the dose wears off.[8][5][6][1]
  - No mention of aura, visual disturbances, or “silent migraines” in the FDA label, clinical trial data, or standard side‑effect lists.[2][6][1]
  - A review of ADHD meds and headaches notes mixed evidence but doesn’t single out aura phenomena.[9]

- **Atomoxetine (Strattera):**  
  - Headaches are a possible side effect, but again, no specific mention of aura or silent migraines.[3][10][7]

- **Guanfacine (Intuniv):**  
  - Headaches can occur, but no reports of aura or migraine‑like visual symptoms in standard lists.[4][11]

- **Stimulants in general (methylphenidate, amphetamines):**  
  - Headaches are common; some patients report migraines worsening or starting after starting stimulants, but this is more about tension/rebound headaches than classic migraines with aura.[12][13][8][9]

Bottom line from primary sources: silent migraines with aura aren’t flagged as a “known” side effect for these drugs.

***

## Anecdotal reports and indirect links

- **User forums and case reports:**  
  - Some people on Reddit and ADHD communities report **worsening migraines or new visual aura** after starting Vyvanse or other stimulants, sometimes describing “silent” or atypical migraines.[14]
  - These are not controlled studies, so it’s hard to separate drug effect from coincidence, stress, dehydration, or pre‑existing migraine tendency.  

- **Stimulants and migraine biology:**  
  - Stimulants raise dopamine and norepinephrine, which can sometimes **trigger vascular changes** or tension that mimic migraine aura in susceptible people.[15][13][12]
  - Paradoxically, some research suggests stimulants might **help migraines** in ADHD patients by stabilizing dopamine (migraines involve dopamine fluctuations), but this doesn’t rule out triggering in others.[15]
  - ADHD itself is linked to higher migraine rates (possibly shared genetics or dopamine issues), so meds might unmask or worsen a pre‑existing tendency.[12][15]

**Speculation (low confidence):** Your silent auras *could* be linked to Vyvanse (vasoconstriction, rebound, or dopamine effects), especially if they started or worsened after your dose changes, but there’s no strong causal evidence.[13][14][8][15]
