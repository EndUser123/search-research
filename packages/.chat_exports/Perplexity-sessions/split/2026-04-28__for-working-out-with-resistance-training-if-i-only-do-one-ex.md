## Human

for working out with resistance training, if I only do one exercise, what should it be?

## Assistant

If you truly only do one resistance exercise, a deep squat pattern (ideally a front or goblet squat, loaded as heavy as you can safely manage) is the best “single move” trade‑off for strength, muscle, and long‑term function.[1][2]

## Why a squat is the best single exercise

A properly loaded squat trains a large amount of muscle mass at once: quads, glutes, hamstrings, calves, spinal erectors, and a lot of your trunk stabilizers. You also get direct practice in a fundamental human movement you use daily (sitting, standing, stairs), so strength carries over to real life unusually well.[2][1]

Squats also have a big systemic effect: because they use a lot of muscle, they create strong strength, hypertrophy, and even mild conditioning stimulus in one hit, especially if you work close to technical failure in the 6–12 rep range. Compared with things like curls or calf raises, you get far more “whole‑body benefit per minute invested.”[3][4][1][2]

## How to implement “only squats” efficiently

If time is the real bottleneck, you can get meaningful benefits with:

- Frequency: 2–3 days per week.[4][3]
- Sets: 1–3 hard sets per session, depending on recovery and how hard you push each set.[3][4]
- Reps: 6–12 reps per set with a load that brings you near failure but not into ugly breakdown (1–2 reps in reserve).[4][3]
- Progression: When all sets in a session feel clearly submaximal, add a little load, a couple reps, or an extra set.

If you are training at home with minimal kit, a heavy goblet squat (or two‑dumbbell front squat) is often the safest way to get to challenging loads without needing a rack.[1]

## Edge cases: when you might choose something else

If back or knee issues make squats problematic even with good form and progressive loading, a different “big pattern” might be smarter:

- Hip hinge variant (trap‑bar deadlift or Romanian deadlift) if knees are the main limiter but hips/back tolerate hinging well.[5]
- Push‑up if you cannot load legs much at all but want upper‑body strength and trunk stability using only bodyweight.[6][1]
- Step‑up or split squat if bilateral squats are uncomfortable but single‑leg work with support is fine.[7]

These are second‑best choices in the abstract, but might be first‑best for your specific joints and equipment.

Would you be doing this one exercise at home or in a gym with access to barbells and racks?

## Human

For lower body, if I do two exercises, what should they be, and in what ratio?

## Assistant

For two lower‑body moves, pair a squat pattern with a hip‑hinge pattern, and bias your volume slightly toward the one that matches your main goal (quads vs posterior chain). A practical starting point is about a 1:1 set ratio, then drift to 2:1 toward either squats or hinges depending on priorities.[1][2][3]

## The two exercises

Assuming no major joint limitations and some load access:

- **Exercise 1 – Squat pattern:** Back squat, front squat, or goblet squat. These hit quads, glutes, and adductors very efficiently and are a staple in almost every serious leg program.[4][2]
- **Exercise 2 – Hip hinge pattern:** Romanian deadlift (RDL) or trap‑bar deadlift. These emphasize hamstrings, glutes, and the rest of the posterior chain with complementary loading to squats.[2][1]

Together they cover essentially all major lower‑body muscle groups with just two patterns.[1][4][2]

## Set ratio and weekly dose

For minimum‑effective but still productive training, most evidence‑based hypertrophy guidance suggests roughly 9–18 sets per muscle group per week, spread over 2–3 sessions. With only two lower‑body exercises, you can implement that like this:[5][3]

- Baseline: 1:1 ratio – e.g., per week, 6–9 hard sets of squats and 6–9 hard sets of RDLs, over 2–3 days (for example, 3× squats + 3× RDLs twice per week).[3][5]
- Quad‑biased: 2:1 toward squats – e.g., 8–12 squat sets, 4–6 hinge sets weekly if you care more about quads and anterior strength.[5][3]
- Posterior‑biased: 2:1 toward hinges – flip the above if you want more hamstring/glute/posterior‑chain development.[3][1]

You can keep total weekly hard sets roughly constant while only changing the split between the two patterns.

## Practical progression and session structure

In a given session:

- Do the more important lift first (squat first if you’re quad‑biased, RDL first if posterior‑biased).  
- Run 2–4 working sets of the first lift and 1–3 of the second, in the 5–10 rep range for strength emphasis or 8–12 for more hypertrophy emphasis, taking sets close to technical failure.[5][3]
- Progress by adding load when you hit the top of the rep range on all sets with solid form, or by adding a set when recovery is clearly good.

This keeps the system simple while still letting you bias adaptation toward what you care about most.

## Human

3 lower body exercises and ratio?

## Assistant

For three lower‑body exercises, use: a squat, a hip hinge, and a unilateral (single‑leg) squat, with roughly equal volume, then bias slightly toward your priority pattern. A clean default is a 2:2:2 weekly set split, then shift to 3:2:1 depending on goals.[1][2]

## The three exercises

Pick specific movements that fit your joints and equipment:

- Squat pattern (quads + glutes): Back squat, front squat, or goblet squat.[3][2]
- Hip hinge pattern (posterior chain): Romanian deadlift or trap‑bar deadlift.[2][1]
- Unilateral squat (balance + weak‑side fix): Bulgarian split squat, split squat, or walking lunge.[4][5]

This trio is very close to the “core” three most experts and programs converge on when forced to minimize lower‑body exercise selection.[1][4][2]

## Ratio and weekly volume

Evidence‑based guidelines for hypertrophy and strength cluster around 10–20 hard sets per muscle group per week for most lifters, with a minimum effective dose at about 4 weekly sets. With only three exercises, you can cover that by distributing sets like:[6][7]

- Balanced default (no specific bias):  
  - Squat: 4–6 weekly sets  
  - Hinge: 4–6 weekly sets  
  - Unilateral: 4–6 weekly sets  
  This lands you in the 12–18 total‑set zone, which is solid for most people.[7][2]

- Quad‑biased:  
  - Squat: 6–9 sets  
  - Unilateral: 4–6 sets  
  - Hinge: 3–5 sets  
  Here your ratio is roughly Squat : Unilateral : Hinge ≈ 3 : 2 : 1.[2][1]

- Posterior‑biased:  
  - Hinge: 6–9 sets  
  - Unilateral: 4–6 sets  
  - Squat: 3–5 sets  
  Ratio ≈ Hinge : Unilateral : Squat ≈ 3 : 2 : 1.[1][2]

If you are at the “minimum‑effective” end (busy weeks), you might just do 2 sets of each exercise twice per week (total 4 sets per exercise weekly).[7]

## Session structure

Across 2 lower‑body days per week, a practical layout:

- Day A (quad‑first):  
  - Squat: 3–4 sets  
  - Unilateral: 2–3 sets  
  - Hinge: 2 sets  

- Day B (hinge‑first):  
  - Hinge: 3–4 sets  
  - Unilateral: 2–3 sets  
  - Squat: 2 sets  

Work mostly in the 6–10 rep range for strength emphasis or 8–12 for hypertrophy, taking each working set close to technical failure. Progress by filling out the top of your chosen rep range with solid form, then add small load bumps or an extra set when recovery is clearly good.[6][2][7][1]

## Human

4 lower body?  5 lower body?

## Assistant

For 4–5 lower‑body exercises, you’re no longer constrained; the key is to cover: squat, hinge, unilateral, and (optionally) hip abduction/adduction and calves. A simple way to think about ratios is “core compounds first, accessories second” rather than rigid percentages.[1][2][3][4]

## Recommended 4‑exercise lower body menu

Use 3 big compounds plus 1 accessory:

- Squat pattern (anterior chain): Back squat, front squat, or goblet squat.[2][4][1]
- Hip hinge: Romanian deadlift or conventional/trap‑bar deadlift.[4][2]
- Unilateral squat: Bulgarian split squat, split squat, or walking lunge.[1][2]
- Glute‑ or hamstring‑biased accessory *or* calf raise: Hip thrust, leg curl, or standing calf raise depending on bias.[3][4][1]

These match what most “best leg exercises” lists converge on when limited to a small set.[2][3][4][1]

### 4‑exercise ratio and sets

Think in weekly hard sets (close to failure, good form):

- Total weekly hard sets for legs: ~12–20 is a solid evidence‑based range for most lifters.[5][2]
- Example “balanced strength/hypertrophy” split (say 16 total weekly sets):  
  - Squat: 5 sets  
  - Hinge: 4 sets  
  - Unilateral: 4 sets  
  - Accessory: 3 sets  

In ratio terms that’s roughly Squat : Hinge : Unilateral : Accessory ≈ 5 : 4 : 4 : 3, i.e., about 30–35% of sets on squats, 25–30% on hinges, 25–30% on unilateral, the rest accessory.

You’d spread that across 2 sessions, e.g.:

- Day A: Squat 3–4 sets, Unilateral 2–3 sets  
- Day B: Hinge 3–4 sets, Accessory 2–3 sets  

## Recommended 5‑exercise lower body menu

Here you can add a dedicated lateral or posterior‑chain accessory and calves:

- Squat pattern: Back/front/goblet squat.[4][1][2]
- Hip hinge: Romanian or conventional/trap‑bar deadlift.[2][4]
- Unilateral squat: Bulgarian split squat or walking lunge.[1][2]
- Glute/hamstring accessory: Hip thrust or leg curl.[3][2]
- Calf or lateral pattern: Standing calf raise or lateral lunge/step‑out for frontal‑plane work.[6][4]

This lines up with “best 5 exercises for legs” style lists: squat, RDL, split squat, leg press/hip thrust, calf raise—with you swapping leg press for a unilateral.[3]

### 5‑exercise ratio and sets

Keep total weekly sets similar (e.g., 15–20) but distribute:

Example across 18 weekly hard sets:

- Squat: 5 sets  
- Hinge: 4 sets  
- Unilateral: 4 sets  
- Glute/ham accessory: 3 sets  
- Calves or lateral: 2 sets  

Ratio ≈ 5 : 4 : 4 : 3 : 2, i.e.:

- ~25–30% of sets on squats (primary driver).  
- ~20–25% on hinges.  
- ~20–25% on unilateral.  
- Remaining ~20% split between glute/ham accessory and calves/lateral work.[5][2][3]

Across 2 sessions per week, something like:

- Day A  
  - Squat: 3–4 sets  
  - Unilateral: 2–3 sets  
  - Calves or lateral: 1–2 sets  

- Day B  
  - Hinge: 3–4 sets  
  - Hip thrust/leg curl: 2–3 sets  
  - Optional light pump set on calves/unilateral if you tolerate it  

Work mostly in 6–10 reps for the big compounds and 8–15 for accessories, close to technical failure.[5][2]

## Human

What if I wanted to use body weight or banded exercises?

## Assistant

You can keep the exact same pattern logic (squat + hinge + unilateral + accessories), just swap in bodyweight/banded variants and push harder on reps, tempo, and progression. Bodyweight and bands are absolutely capable of building real lower‑body strength and size when you take sets close to failure.[1][2][3][4]

## Core movement patterns with bodyweight/bands

Use this mapping from the earlier barbell framework:

- Squat pattern  
  - Bodyweight: Deep squat, tempo squat (slow 3–5s lowering), pause squat, jump squat.[2][1]
  - Banded: Banded squats (band above knees or under feet + over shoulders), banded front squat.[3][4]

- Hip hinge pattern  
  - Bodyweight: Good mornings, single‑leg RDL, hip hinge from standing holding a dowel or just air.[1]
  - Banded: Banded RDL/hinge with band under feet and around hands or shoulders.[4][3]

- Unilateral squat / lunge  
  - Bodyweight: Reverse lunge, static split squat, Bulgarian split squat, walking lunge, Cossack squat.[5][2][1]
  - Banded: Same moves with a band adding resistance (under front foot and held at shoulders, or looped around the hips).[3][4]

- Hip extension / glutes  
  - Bodyweight: Glute bridge, hip thrust, single‑leg glute bridge.[2][1]
  - Banded: Banded glute bridge/hip thrust (band over hips or around knees), banded donkey kicks or kickbacks.[4][3]

- Calves / lateral work  
  - Bodyweight: Standing or single‑leg calf raises, wall sit, lateral lunge, step‑ups.[6][1]
  - Banded: Banded lateral walks, monster walks, banded calf raises.[3][4]

These are all widely recommended in credible home‑workout and calisthenics programs for lower‑body strength and hypertrophy.[5][6][1][2][4][3]

## Example 3‑exercise bodyweight/band template (with ratios)

If you want to stay in the “minimalist” 3‑exercise world, a strong bodyweight/band trio could be:

- Squat: Tempo or pause squats (or banded squats).  
- Unilateral: Bulgarian split squats or reverse lunges.  
- Hip extension: Glute bridge/hip thrust (bodyweight or banded).  

Weekly set ratio (balanced):  
- Squat: 4–6 sets  
- Unilateral: 4–6 sets  
- Glute bridge/hip thrust: 4–6 sets  

In “ratio” terms, that’s about 1:1:1, then bias:

- Quad‑biased: Squat : Uni : Bridge ≈ 3 : 2 : 1 (e.g., 9 : 6 : 3 sets).  
- Glute/ham‑biased: Bridge/Uni : Squat ≈ 3 : 2 (e.g., 8 : 8 : 4 with more emphasis on bridges and split squats).[1][2]

Because load is lower than barbells, you’ll usually live in higher reps (10–25) and slower tempos, but you still aim to get within a few reps of failure.[2][1]

## Example 4–5 exercise bodyweight/band template

If you move up to 4–5 lower‑body movements, this structure maps cleanly:

**Four‑exercise bodyweight/band day**

- Squat: Tempo squats or banded squats.[1][3]
- Unilateral squat: Bulgarian split squats.[2][1]
- Hip hinge: Single‑leg RDL (bodyweight or light band).[1]
- Hip extension or calves: Glute bridge/hip thrust or calf raises.[6][2][1]

Balanced weekly set split (for, say, 16 total sets):  
- Squat: 5 sets  
- Unilateral: 4 sets  
- Hinge: 3–4 sets  
- Glute bridge/calf: 3–4 sets  

**Five‑exercise bodyweight/band day**

Add a lateral/glute‑med move with a band:

- Squat: Tempo/banded squats.  
- Unilateral squat: Bulgarian split squats or walking lunges.  
- Hip hinge: Single‑leg RDL or banded RDL.  
- Hip extension: Banded hip thrust or single‑leg bridge.  
- Lateral/calf: Banded lateral walks or calf raises.[6][4][3][1]

Weekly ratios across ~18 sets might look like:  
Squat : Uni : Hinge : Hip ext : Lat/Calf ≈ 5 : 4 : 3 : 3 : 3.

## How to make bodyweight/bands “heavy enough”

The big issue at home is relative intensity, not exercise selection. You can make these brutally effective by combining:

- Higher rep ranges: Often 10–25 reps per set, especially when load is low.[2][1]
- Slow eccentrics: 3–5 seconds down on squats, lunges, and bridges to boost time under tension.[1]
- Pauses: 1–2s pauses in the bottom of squats, Bulgarians, and bridges.  
- Frequent unilateral work: Single‑leg variations effectively double load per leg even with bodyweight.[7][1]
- Shorter rest periods when load is low: 60–90 seconds between sets can keep stimulus high when you can’t load heavy.[2][1]

The key is that the last few reps of a set are genuinely hard while still clean in form.

## Human

what about horsestance?

## Assistant

Horse stance is an excellent addition for lower‑body strength, endurance, and hip mobility, but it’s not a complete replacement for dynamic squats/hinges if you care about full‑range strength and hypertrophy. It works best as a key isometric pattern in the mix, not your only leg exercise.[1][2][3]

## What horse stance actually gives you

Horse stance (wide squat, thighs near parallel, held isometrically) trains:

- Quads, glutes, and especially adductors (inner thighs) under continuous tension.[2][1]
- Hip and ankle mobility via a “loaded stretch” effect at deep ranges.[3][1][2]
- Serious strength endurance and positional stability around hips, knees, and ankles.[4][1][3]
- Postural control: stacked spine, braced trunk, upright torso.[5][1][3]

Consistent practice improves your ability to stay solid in low stances, hold good posture, and resist being pushed around—very useful in martial arts and general “braced” life tasks.[6][1][5][3]

## The hard limitation of horse stance

The main limitation: it’s an isometric at one joint angle.

- Strength gains from isometrics are highly angle‑specific: roughly at the angle you train and about ±15 degrees.[3]
- If you only train horse stance, you’ll get strong at that depth, but not necessarily through the whole range of a squat or lunge. You won’t load hip extension or knee extension dynamically the way squats, split squats, or RDLs do.[3]
- Community and coaching consensus is that horse stance is great for endurance and hip strength, but not ideal as your *only* leg work if maximal strength and hypertrophy are goals.[7][3]

So it’s more like a powerful supplement: fantastic for deep hip strength, mobility, and mental toughness; incomplete for overall leg development on its own.

## How I’d use horse stance in your minimalist framework

Given everything we already laid out (squat + hinge + unilateral, with options for bodyweight/bands), here’s how horse stance fits:

### If you want to stay ultra‑minimal

You could run something like:

- Dynamic squat or split squat (bodyweight/band or loaded).  
- Hip hinge (RDL/hip hinge or single‑leg RDL, bodyweight/band).  
- Horse stance (isometric squat).

Horse stance there is your “isometric accessory” for deep, end‑range strength and adductors. The ratio:

- Dynamic squat pattern: ~40–50% of weekly leg sets.  
- Hinge: ~30–40%.  
- Horse stance: ~20–30% (done as timed holds instead of sets/reps).  

Example: 10 total weekly “hard efforts”  
- 4–5 dynamic squat sets  
- 3–4 hinge sets  
- 2–3 horse‑stance holds (e.g., 30–90s each)

### If you’re doing 4–5 exercises (bodyweight or bands)

You can treat horse stance as either:

- Replacement for some unilateral work if you want more adductor/end‑range focus, or  
- Extra “positional” block at the end.

Example 4‑exercise bodyweight day:

- Tempo squats  
- Bulgarian split squats  
- Single‑leg RDL (bodyweight or band)  
- Horse stance holds  

Example weekly emphasis (out of ~16 hard efforts):

- Squat (dynamic): 5 sets  
- Unilateral: 4 sets  
- Hinge: 4 sets  
- Horse stance: 3 holds  

### Progressing horse stance

Evidence‑informed practice suggests treating it like progressive isometric training:

- Start at whatever depth you can hold with clean form for 10–20 seconds.[1][2]
- Build up to holds of 60–120 seconds at that depth over weeks.[8][1][3]
- Then gradually widen stance or sink deeper while maintaining the hold times.  
- Keep knees driven out, chest up, pelvis slightly tucked; if your lower back collapses, you’re too low or too fatigued.[2][1][3]

You can slot it at the end of a lower‑body session as a “finisher” or at the start as a position/hip‑opening primer.

## Where I’d *not* over‑rely on horse stance

I would not use horse stance as:

- Your only leg work if you want significant muscle growth across full range of motion. You’ll be missing dynamic loading in extension.[3]
- Your only knee/hip strengthening if you’re rehabbing and need strength across the joint’s ROM; you’ll still want controlled squats, step‑ups, and hinges.[3]

As a core lower‑body *component* though—especially if you’re into martial arts, mobility, and “iron hips”—it’s extremely worthwhile.
