## Human

Hey Perplexity, we need to replace our hardwood floor because it got damaged, but we don't want to replace it with something that will cause the house to lose value. What flooring should we use so that the house does not lose value?

## Assistant

For resale and value retention, real or engineered **hardwood** in the main living areas is still the safest and most value-preserving choice, with luxury vinyl plank (LVP) as the best “second place” if durability/budget is a bigger concern.[1][2][3]

## What buyers value most

- Hardwood (solid or engineered) is consistently cited by real estate agents as the “gold standard” for resale; homes with hardwood tend to sell faster and for higher prices than those with carpet or low-end vinyl.[3][1]
- National Association of Realtors data, referenced by USA Today, estimates hardwood can return around or even above its cost at resale (over 100% ROI in some reports), which is unusually strong for any upgrade.[1][3]
- In markets like Calgary, local flooring pros note buyers often explicitly list hardwood as a top desired feature in established neighbourhoods.[4][5]

## Best choices to avoid losing value

- Replace damaged hardwood with new **engineered hardwood** or solid hardwood in the same areas; this maintains the “real wood” signal buyers pay for and keeps finishes consistent across rooms.[2][6][1]
- If you need something tougher or more water-resistant (kitchen, entry, basement), use high-quality LVP that closely mimics wood in those spaces, while keeping real wood in the main living areas and bedrooms.[6][2][4]
- If the existing floor is structurally sound but just worn, refinishing rather than replacing often gives the best value return and keeps the original hardwood, which buyers like even more than new synthetic products.[2][1]

## Options that can hurt value

- Swapping real hardwood for cheap laminate or low-end vinyl in main living areas can downgrade the perceived quality of the home and usually does not recoup its cost at resale.[5][1][2]
- Highly personalized or trendy colors (very dark, very gray, or unusual tones) can limit appeal; neutral, mid-tone wood looks tend to show better and feel more “move-in ready” to a broader pool of buyers.[7][8][1]
- Inconsistent flooring (many different types/tones between rooms) can make the house feel chopped up and lower perceived value; buyers respond better when the main level uses one continuous wood or wood-look product.[7][1]

## Calgary-specific considerations

- Calgary’s dry winters and seasonal humidity swings mean engineered hardwood often performs better than solid hardwood (less movement and gapping) while still reading as “real wood” to buyers.[4][6]
- Local flooring and real estate guidance in Calgary still points to hardwood as outperforming vinyl for resale; vinyl is recommended more for basements, rentals, and very high-wear spaces.[5][4]

## How to choose for your house

- If your goal is “don’t lose value,” prioritize: real wood in main living/bedrooms, high-quality LVP or tile only where moisture or abuse is a big issue, and consistent, neutral mid-tone finishes throughout.[1][2][4]
- If you tell more about which rooms are affected and whether the rest of the house already has hardwood, it is possible to give a room-by-room recommendation that balances resale with cost and durability.[8][7]
