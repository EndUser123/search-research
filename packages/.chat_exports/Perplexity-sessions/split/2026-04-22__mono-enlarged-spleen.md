## Human

Mono enlarged spleen

## Assistant

Infectious mononucleosis (mono), caused by the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), frequently leads to splenomegaly—an enlarged spleen—in about 50% of cases, as the spleen works overtime filtering blood and producing immune cells during the infection. This enlargement happens because EBV triggers immune hyperplasia in the spleen, making it swell beyond its normal fist-sized dimensions in the upper left abdomen. While often asymptomatic, it increases vulnerability to rupture, a rare (0.1-0.5%) but life-threatening complication from even minor trauma.[1][2][3][4][5][6][7]
## Why Mono Causes Splenomegaly
The spleen's core job is filtering blood, removing old red blood cells, and ramping up white blood cells/platelets during infections like mono. EBV infects B-cells, sparking a massive T-cell response that congests the spleen's red pulp, causing it to expand—sometimes doubling in size. This isn't unique to mono; other viruses (HIV, CMV) or parasites can do it too, but mono is a top trigger in teens/young adults. Uncertainty: Exact size increase varies; imaging (ultrasound/CT/MRI) confirms it, as physical exams miss mild cases.[1][8][3][4][6]
## Key Risks and Complications
Rupture risk peaks in the first 3 weeks but can linger 4-5 weeks post-symptoms, with studies suggesting activity restrictions up to 31 days or until spleen normalizes via imaging. Symptoms of rupture: sudden left upper abdominal pain, shoulder pain (from diaphragm irritation), dizziness, or shock—emergency surgery often needed. Enlarged spleen also sequesters healthy blood cells, potentially causing anemia, low platelets, or clotting issues. Fact: Fatiguability persists 1-6 months even after spleen shrinks, due to ongoing EBV dormancy.[1][2][8][3][9][7][10][11]
## Diagnosis Approach
Blood tests show lymphocytosis (>50% lymphocytes, >10% atypical), positive heterophile/Monospot, or EBV antibodies. Imaging (ultrasound first-line) measures spleen length (>13cm adult splenomegaly); CT/MRI for hematoma suspicion. Assumption: Mono assumed if classic symptoms (fever, sore throat, nodes, fatigue); rule out strep/CMV/toxoplasmosis if tests negative early. Confidence: High for EBV link (95% mono cases), but spleen size doesn't always correlate with rupture risk.[3][7][12][13]
## Treatment and Management
Supportive only—no antivirals routine, as mono self-resolves in 2-4 weeks (spleen 4-8 weeks). Use acetaminophen/ibuprofen for pain/fever; hydrate; gradual rest based on energy, not strict bedrest. Avoid contact sports/heavy lifting 4-8 weeks or until imaging clears; no antibiotics unless bacterial superinfection. Speculation (low confidence): Hydroxyurea mentioned for "symptomatic" cases but likely miscontextualized—not standard for mono (viral, not myeloproliferative). Splenectomy rare, for rupture only.[2][8][6][9][12][13]
## Next Steps
See a doctor immediately for abdominal pain, especially left-sided/sharp, or if mono symptoms + fatigue >2 weeks—get bloodwork/imaging. Track symptoms: monitor for fever drop, energy return; re-image spleen at 4 weeks if enlarged initially. Avoid alcohol (liver stress) and NSAIDs if bleeding risk high. If athlete, get clearance via ultrasound before sports. Consensus: Full recovery typical, but challenge "mild" assumptions—imaging trumps symptoms for safety.[1][9][7][11]
