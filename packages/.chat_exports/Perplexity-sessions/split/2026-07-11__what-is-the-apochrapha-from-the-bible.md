## Human

What is the apochrapha from the bible

## Assistant

The Apocrypha are ancient Jewish religious writings that are closely related to the Bible but are not accepted as scripture in all Christian traditions. More precisely, they are “hidden” or non‑canonical books that some churches include in their Bible and others omit or place in a separate section.[1][2][3]

## Meaning of “Apocrypha”

- The word comes from Greek \(apokryphos\), meaning “hidden” or “things hidden away.”[4][5][3]
- In modern religious use, it refers to biblical‑style texts that lie outside an accepted canon of scripture—either excluded entirely or given secondary status.[2][3]
- In many Protestant contexts, “apocryphal” has also come to mean “of doubtful authenticity” or “not truly scriptural,” which affects how these books are viewed.[6][2]

## Which books are in the Apocrypha?

When people say “the Apocrypha of the Bible,” they usually mean the set of extra Old Testament books printed between the Testaments in older Protestant Bibles (for example, the 1611 King James Version). Different traditions count and divide them slightly differently, but the commonly listed Old Testament Apocrypha include:[7][8][5][9][3]

- Tobit  
- Judith  
- Additions to Esther  
- Wisdom of Solomon  
- Ecclesiasticus (also called Sirach or Wisdom of Ben Sira)  
- Baruch  
- Letter/Epistle of Jeremiah  
- Additions to Daniel (Song of the Three Children, Susanna, Bel and the Dragon)  
- Prayer of Azariah (often grouped with the Song of the Three Children)  
- Prayer of Manasseh  
- 1 Maccabees  
- 2 Maccabees  

Some lists also include 1 Esdras and 2 Esdras (also called 3 and 4 Ezra in some traditions) among the Apocrypha. Outside these, there is a broader world of “New Testament apocrypha” (extra gospels, acts, letters, and apocalypses) such as the Gospel of Thomas or other Nag Hammadi writings, but those are usually treated as a separate category.[8][5][3]

## How different churches treat the Apocrypha

Christian traditions disagree on whether these books are scripture, useful but non‑scriptural, or to be avoided:[5][10][2][6]

- Roman Catholic Church:  
  - Accepts most of these books as **deuterocanonical**—a secondary but genuine part of the Old Testament canon.[3][2][5]
  - They are interspersed throughout the Old Testament (e.g., Wisdom and Sirach appear alongside other wisdom books).[3]

- Eastern Orthodox churches:  
  - Generally accept an even larger set of books, including some not in the Catholic deuterocanon (for example, additional sections of Ezra/Esdras).[10][2]
  - They often use “Apocrypha” differently, sometimes reserving it for texts outside even the expanded Orthodox canon.[10]

- Most Protestant churches:  
  - Do not treat these books as part of the biblical canon, arguing they were not part of the Hebrew Bible and are not quoted as scripture in the New Testament.[1][2][6]
  - Historically, many Protestant Bibles still printed them in a separate Apocrypha section “for reading and instruction,” but not for doctrine; modern Protestant editions often omit them entirely.[1][3]

## Why they matter

Even when not treated as scripture, these writings are historically important:[2][7][6]

- They provide background on Jewish history between the Old and New Testaments, especially the Maccabean revolt and the rise of different Jewish groups.[7][2]
- They show how Jewish thought about wisdom, law, resurrection, and judgment was developing in the centuries before Jesus.[2][7]
- They influenced early Christian liturgy, theology, and preaching, and were widely read in the early church.[1][2]

In terms of epistemic status: it is a **FACT** that different Christian traditions officially classify these books differently (canonical, deuterocanonical, or apocryphal) and that the term comes from Greek “hidden things.” It is **CONSENSUS** among mainstream biblical scholars that these texts are valuable historical and religious sources but that their canonical status is tradition‑dependent. Any theological claims about their inspiration or authority beyond that depend on specific church doctrines and should be treated as **SPECULATION** from a neutral standpoint.[4][5][7][3][2]

What do they say

## Assistant

They cover a mix of history, wisdom teaching, prayers, and expansions of familiar biblical stories, mostly from the Jewish world in the few centuries before Jesus. Below is a high‑level, book‑by‑book view of “what they say” in the commonly referenced Old Testament Apocrypha.[1][2][3]

## Narrative and historical books

These read like extended Bible stories or historical chronicles.[4][2][5]

- **Tobit**: A family story about Tobit, a righteous but suffering Israelite, his son Tobias, and the angel Raphael. It combines themes of piety, almsgiving, marriage, and divine guidance; God sends Raphael to heal Tobit’s blindness and protect Tobias, stressing prayer, generosity, and faithfulness to the law.[2][5]

- **Judith**: A patriotic tale in which Judith, a devout widow, saves Israel by infiltrating the enemy camp and beheading the Assyrian general Holofernes. It emphasizes courage, trust in God, and resistance to foreign oppression, using a somewhat fictionalized setting to make its theological point.[5][2]

- **1 Maccabees**: A straight historical narrative of the Maccabean revolt against Seleucid (Greek) rule, the desecration and rededication of the Temple (origin of Hanukkah), and the political/military leadership of the Hasmonean family. It is relatively sober and “history‑style,” focusing on covenant faithfulness, armed resistance, and God’s help in defending Jewish law and worship.[3][4][2][5]

- **2 Maccabees**: Covers some of the same period as 1 Maccabees but with more theological and dramatic emphasis. It highlights martyrdom, resurrection hope, and God’s direct interventions, including visions and miraculous events, and insists that suffering Jews who die for the law will be vindicated by God.[4][2][3][5]

- **1 Esdras** (in some Apocrypha lists): Retells portions of Chronicles, Ezra, and Nehemiah, including the return from exile and temple rebuilding, with a famous “three bodyguards” contest about what is strongest (wine, the king, women, or truth), concluding that “truth” and God’s wisdom prevail.[1][2][5]

- **2 Esdras / 4 Ezra** (where included): A series of apocalyptic visions attributed to Ezra wrestling with why the wicked prosper and why Israel suffers. It explores questions of divine justice, predestination, and the fate of souls, with symbolic visions and angelic explanations similar to Daniel and Revelation.[2][3][1]

## Wisdom and ethical teaching

These are closer to Proverbs or Ecclesiastes: practical ethics, theology, and reflection on life.[3][4][2]

- **Wisdom of Solomon**: Philosophical and theological essays on wisdom, righteousness, and the fate of the just and unjust. It argues that the righteous, even if they suffer, are secure with God, while idol worship and moral corruption lead to ruin; it also reflects on God’s role in creation and history, often echoing themes later picked up by Christian writers.[5][2][3]

- **Ecclesiasticus / Sirach**: A large collection of teachings on everyday conduct—family, speech, business, friendship, anger, and worship—framed as instruction from a wise teacher, Ben Sira. It strongly emphasizes fear of the Lord, respect for parents, care for the poor, diligence, and proper use of speech, and ends with a long praise of Israel’s heroes (like a historical meditation on God’s work through key figures).[2][3][5]

- **Baruch** (often with the Letter of Jeremiah): Presented as words of the scribe Baruch during the Babylonian exile, urging confession of sin, trust in God’s mercy, and rejection of idols. The attached Letter of Jeremiah is a sustained critique of idol worship, mocking the powerlessness of statues and insisting that only the living God is worthy of trust.[3][5][2]

## Expansions to existing biblical books

These add prayers and episodes to books already in the standard Old Testament.[5][2][3]

- **Additions to Esther**: Extra sections that explicitly mention God, prayer, and religious motives, which are absent in the Hebrew Esther. They include prayers by Mordecai and Esther, and royal decrees that frame events more theologically, turning Esther from a purely political story into one of divine providence and fasting.[2][3][5]

- **Additions to Daniel** (usually printed as separate short books):  
  - *Song of the Three / Prayer of Azariah*: A prayer and hymn placed into the fiery furnace episode, where the three youths praise God for justice and mercy despite their suffering, and then celebrate God’s deliverance.[3][5][2]
  - *Susanna*: A story about a righteous woman falsely accused of adultery by corrupt elders; Daniel exposes their lies through clever cross‑examination, emphasizing God’s vindication of the innocent and the importance of judicial integrity.[5][2][3]
  - *Bel and the Dragon*: Short tales where Daniel debunks claims that a pagan idol (Bel) consumes offerings and defeats a dragon worshiped as a god, stressing monotheism and God’s protection over Daniel.[2][3][5]

## Short prayers and confessions

These are brief but theologically dense.[3][5][2]

- **Prayer of Manasseh**: A penitential prayer attributed to King Manasseh of Judah, famously wicked in the Old Testament. It is a heartfelt confession of sin, appeal to God’s mercy, and affirmation that God forgives even very great guilt when a person truly repents.[1][5][2]

Factually, it is well‑supported that these books collectively describe:  
- Jewish resistance and survival under foreign empires.  
- Ethical and wisdom teaching on daily life and piety.  
- Apocalyptic wrestling with suffering and justice.  
- Liturgical additions that make implicit theology explicit in existing stories.[4][1][5][2][3]

That is **FACT** based on textual summaries and scholarly surveys. It is **CONSENSUS** among mainstream scholars that these writings represent diverse theological voices within Second Temple Judaism rather than a single unified message. Any claim that they definitively teach or contradict specific later doctrines (for example, purgatory, intercession of saints) requires theological interpretation and falls into **SPECULATION** from a neutral, non‑confessional perspective.[1][5][2][3]

Why are they not in the bible

## Assistant

They are “not in the Bible” in many modern editions because different communities drew the boundary of *canon* differently, and Protestants in particular decided these books were useful but not inspired scripture. Roman Catholic and Orthodox Bibles *do* include most of them as scripture, while most current Protestant Bibles omit them entirely or place them in a separate section.[1][2][3][4]

## The core issue: canon and authority

- “Canon” means the official list of books regarded as divinely inspired and authoritative for faith and practice.[4][1]
- The Jewish Bible (Tanakh) settled on a set of books that does not include the Apocrypha; these extra books mostly come from Greek‑language Jewish literature of the Second Temple period and never became part of the standard Hebrew canon.[2][1][4]
- Early Christians used the Greek Septuagint, which did include these texts, leading some churches to treat them as scripture and others to treat them as secondary.[1][4]

From a neutral standpoint, it is **FACT** that canon boundaries were decided historically by communities and councils, not by a single event, and that the Jewish canon never counted these books. It is **CONSENSUS** among scholars that these books were widely read and respected, but their status varied. Treating them as definitively “inspired” or “non‑inspired” is **SPECULATION** tied to particular theological commitments.[2][4][1]

## Main Protestant reasons for excluding them

Across modern Protestant explanations, you see a fairly consistent set of arguments for not including the Apocrypha in the 66‑book Bible:[5][6][3][7][2]

- Not in the ancient Hebrew canon: Jewish sources and later Christian writers note that books written after a certain point (often linked to the Persian period) were not treated as inspired scripture by mainstream Judaism, even if they were valued historically.[3][5][2]
- Not cited as scripture by Jesus or the apostles: Protestants often point out that New Testament writers quote “Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms” as scripture but do not explicitly quote Apocrypha passages with the same formula (“it is written,” etc.).[8][3][2]
- Questioned authorship and origin: “Apocrypha” is sometimes defined in Protestant apologetics as books of uncertain authorship or obscure origin, which lowers confidence that they are prophetic or apostolic in origin.[6][7][5]
- Perceived doctrinal and historical issues: Some Protestant writers argue that certain Apocrypha passages conflict with doctrines they infer from the 66‑book canon, or contain historical details they view as less reliable than canonical narratives.[7][5][6]
- Church‑history precedent: Many Reformation‑era confessions (e.g., Westminster) explicitly state that the books commonly called Apocrypha are “not of divine inspiration” and thus “no part of the canon of Scripture.”[3][7]

These are **FACT** as reasons Protestants themselves articulate; whether they are persuasive depends on one’s theological assumptions, so their evaluative force is **SPECULATION** from a neutral perspective.

## Why Catholics and Orthodox *keep* them

On the other side, Roman Catholic and Orthodox traditions include most of these books (calling them “deuterocanonical” in Catholic language) because:[9][4][1]

- Longstanding liturgical use: They had been read in churches and used in worship and theology for centuries before the Reformation.[9][4][1]
- Septuagint tradition: The Greek Old Testament used by early Christians included these writings, so they appeared in major early biblical manuscripts (like codices) used by the church.[4][1][9]
- Conciliar decisions: The Council of Trent (1546) formally affirmed these books for the Catholic canon in response to Reformation challenges, codifying a practice that had already existed in many parts of the church.[1][2][9]

Again, it is **FACT** that these councils and liturgical practices exist and that these churches regard the books as scripture. The judgment that “therefore they are inspired” is **SPECULATION** from a neutral viewpoint.[2][9][4][1]

## Why modern Protestant Bibles often omit them entirely

Historically, many early Protestant Bibles *printed* the Apocrypha in a separate section “for reading and instruction” but not for doctrine. Over time, especially in English‑speaking contexts:[9][1]

- Confessions and statements of faith emphasized a 66‑book canon, reinforcing the idea that the Apocrypha are non‑scriptural.[7][3]
- Economic and practical pressures (printing cost, page count) plus doctrinal concerns led publishers to drop the Apocrypha from many mass‑market Protestant editions.[1][9]

So in many Protestant traditions today, Bibles simply go from Malachi to Matthew, which can make it look as if the Apocrypha “were never there,” even though they were widely printed between the Testaments in earlier centuries.[9][1]
