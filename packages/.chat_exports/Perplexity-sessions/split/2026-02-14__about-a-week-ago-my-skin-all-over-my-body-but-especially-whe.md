## Assistant

Widespread “raw” painful skin, especially around hair, for a week is not something to self‑diagnose at home and needs an in‑person medical assessment soon (same‑day or urgent care), especially because it is persistent and very painful. I’ll outline some possibilities and red‑flag symptoms, but only a clinician examining you can sort these out.[1][2]

## Why your skin might feel “raw”

Some broad categories doctors think about:

- **Skin/follicle inflammation or infection** such as folliculitis (inflamed hair follicles) typically causes tender, burning or itchy skin with small red or pus‑filled bumps centered on hairs, and can occur anywhere hair grows. Folliculitis usually produces visible spots or rash, so if your skin looks mostly normal, this is less likely.[3][4][5][6]
- **Nerve‑related pain (allodynia)** is when normally non‑painful touch (clothes, light brushing, hair movement) feels burning, raw, or intensely sore, often without much visible skin change. This can occur with conditions like shingles (sometimes even before a rash), migraines, fibromyalgia, small‑fiber neuropathy, diabetes, or post‑viral states.[7][8]
- **Widespread rash/immune reactions or infections** (for example allergic drug eruptions, viral exanthems, shingles, or serious reactions like Stevens‑Johnson syndrome) can cause painful skin, sometimes starting as tenderness or burning before an obvious rash appears, and are more concerning if you see blisters, target‑like spots, or peeling skin.[9][1]
- **Contact or irritant dermatitis** can make skin feel sore and raw where something touched you (new detergent, body wash, clothing dye), usually with visible redness or rash in the contact areas.[2]
- **Systemic illness with skin sensitivity**: some infections and inflammatory conditions can make the skin hurt to touch even if it doesn’t look bad yet, especially when combined with fever, fatigue, or body aches.[10][1][2]

Because your pain is “all over” and especially at hair‑bearing areas, nerve‑related causes (allodynia) or diffuse follicle/skin inflammation are both on the table, and you really need a clinician to narrow this down.[8][3][7]

## When this is an emergency

Go to an emergency department or call emergency services right away if ANY of these are happening:

- Trouble breathing, swallowing, or swelling of lips, tongue, or face.[1][9]
- High fever, confusion, feeling faint, very rapid heart rate, or looking/feeling seriously ill.[2][9][1]
- A rapidly spreading rash, purple/black spots, or skin that blisters, peels, or turns dusky.[9][1]
- Severe, rapidly worsening pain in a specific area, especially with warmth, redness, or swelling (could signal deep infection).[1][9]

These can indicate life‑threatening infection or severe drug or immune reactions and cannot wait.[9][1]

## When to seek urgent (same‑day) care

Even without the above red flags, your description meets criteria to be seen urgently (same day or within 24 hours):

- Pain has lasted more than a week with no improvement and is affecting comfort and function.[2]
- The skin feels very painful or raw to light touch over large areas of the body.  
- You are unsure of the cause and there may not be a clear rash.  

In Alberta, that would normally mean calling Health Link (811) for triage and either going to urgent care/ER or seeing a walk‑in/primary‑care clinic the same day, depending on what the nurse advises.[4]

## What to do right now (while arranging care)

These are general comfort measures, not a substitute for seeing a doctor:

- Use loose, soft clothing and bedding; avoid friction, heat, and tight waistbands since these can aggravate allodynia or irritated follicles.[10]
- Avoid new soaps, detergents, or topical products until you’ve been assessed, so you don’t complicate the picture.  
- If you are not allergic and a clinician has previously said it is safe for you, typical over‑the‑counter pain relievers (like acetaminophen or ibuprofen) may reduce discomfort a bit until you are seen; follow package directions and your prior medical advice.  

## What your clinician will likely check

Expect them to:

- Examine your skin and hair follicles closely for subtle rashes, bumps, or blisters.[6][3]
- Ask about new medications, infections, vaccines, travel, tick bites, or illnesses in the last month.[7][8]
- Consider blood tests and, if nerve pain is suspected, possibly refer you to neurology or dermatology for further evaluation.[8][7]

## Typical allergic reactions to Ritalin

Commonly described skin‑type reactions to oral methylphenidate include:[2][4][3][1]
- Itchy rash or hives (urticaria).  
- Nonspecific “skin eruptions” (red spots, sometimes without itch).[2]

- Swelling of face, lips, tongue, or throat.  
- Trouble breathing or swallowing.

## About the allodynia‑like pain

## 1. Home measures that may blunt allodynia short‑term

These are low‑risk comfort strategies you can try **while arranging urgent care**:

- Wear **very soft, loose clothing** (old thin cotton T‑shirts, soft pajama bottoms, no seams/waistbands if possible) and avoid anything tight, rough, or elastic.[4][5][6]
- Optimize temperature: many people with allodynia find **neutral to slightly warm** better than cold; avoid drafts, fans directly on skin, and big temp swings.[4]
- Use very **light, steady pressure** instead of light brushing where possible; steady contact can sometimes hurt less than intermittent brushing, but don’t force any pressure that spikes pain.[7][5]
- Short lukewarm showers or baths; avoid hot or very cold water and strong jets, which can trigger pain.[2][1]
- If your skin barrier feels dry or tight, apply a **fragrance‑free, bland moisturizer** (e.g., ceramide‑based cream) with a flat palm in *slow*, minimal strokes, only if the contact isn’t intolerable.[3][4]
- Gentle relaxation: diaphragmatic breathing, body‑scan relaxation, or a low‑stimulus audio track can dampen central amplification a bit, though it won’t remove the pain.[8][4]

Only use OTC pain meds (acetaminophen, ibuprofen) if they’re normally safe for you; they often help less for allodynia but may take the edge off and are reasonable short‑term while you get assessed.[3]

## 2. Could 18 mg/day gabapentin cause sudden severe allodynia?

At usual doses, **gabapentin is used to treat** neuropathic pain and allodynia by dampening abnormal nerve firing. However:[9][10][3]

- Some people report **initial worsening or increased sensitivity** when starting gabapentin, likely as a transient CNS side effect or due to wrong dosing/schedule.[11][12][10]
- Individual cases and patient reports describe gabapentin subjectively “making neuropathy worse” or causing paradoxical symptoms, even though that’s not the expected pharmacologic effect.[12][10][11]
- Very low or intermittent dosing may be subtherapeutic and simply **fail** to help, making underlying pain more noticeable rather than gabapentin truly “causing” it.[10][3]

So: yes, it is **possible** in a practical sense that 18 mg/day (which is far lower than typical therapeutic doses in mg, standard regimens start around 100–300 mg multiple times a day) could coincide with or subjectively worsen your pain, but mechanistically gabapentin is designed to reduce, not trigger, allodynia. Any new, sudden severe pain after starting or changing gabapentin should be treated as a reason to speak with a doctor promptly about whether to adjust or stop it—do not just increase or quit abruptly on your own.[9][10][3]

## 3. When neuropathic pain needs ER vs urgent care vs clinic

General guidance (not specific to one site):

**Go to the ER immediately (call 911 if needed) if neuropathic‑type pain comes with any of:**[13][14][15][16]

- Sudden weakness, paralysis, or trouble moving an arm/leg.  
- Loss of bladder or bowel control, new severe difficulty walking, or loss of balance.  
- Confusion, trouble speaking, facial droop, or vision changes.  
- Chest pain, shortness of breath, or severe headache with neck stiffness.  
- Fever, rapidly worsening pain, or new rash/blisters/skin color change suggesting infection or severe skin reaction.  

**Urgent care or same‑day assessment** is appropriate when:[16][13][3]

- Pain is **severe or sudden** and interferes with function (like your 8/10 allodynia), but you don’t have the above red‑flag neurologic or systemic signs.  
- Pain is clearly linked to a new medication or unclear cause and hasn’t improved over several days.  

**Regular clinic** is for chronic, stable neuropathic pain that is mild–moderate and not changing rapidly.[16][3]

Given your pain level (8/10 by your high scale), sudden onset, body‑wide allodynia, and recent CNS‑active med changes, you remain in the **urgent care or ER** zone rather than “home only” management, even if vitals seem okay to you.[17][13][16]
