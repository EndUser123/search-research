## Assistant

For employment income in Alberta, a typical middle‑income earner ends up with an average total income tax rate (federal + provincial, excluding GST) in roughly the mid‑teens to low‑20s percent, depending on income level.[1][2][3]

## Clarifying your question

Your message cuts off at “including get”. You might mean one of these:

- Including **GST** (5% federal sales tax on most purchases).[4]
- Including **all** personal taxes (federal + provincial + CPP/EI, etc.).[5][3]

Each of those gives a different “average rate,” so I’ll outline the main pieces, using Alberta (Calgary) as context.

## Income tax basics (2025–2026)

- Canada uses **marginal** tax brackets; your average rate is total income tax divided by your income.[6]
- 2025 federal brackets: 15% up to about 57k, 20.5% to ~115k, 26% to ~178k, 29% to ~253k, 33% above that.[3][1]
- Alberta 2025 brackets: 8% up to ~61k, then 10%, 12%, 13%, 14–15% at higher incomes.[2][3]

Because these are tiered, someone earning, say, 80k in Alberta does **not** pay 20.5% + 10% on the full 80k; only the top slices are taxed at those higher rates.[6][3]

## Approximate average rates (Alberta employment income)

Very rough ballpark for combined federal + Alberta income tax, excluding CPP/EI and GST:[1][2][3]

- 40k income: ~10–12% average tax.  
- 60k income: ~14–16% average tax.  
- 100k income: ~19–22% average tax.  
- 150k income: ~24–27% average tax.

CPP and EI contributions add a few more percentage points on earned income up to their annual maximums.[7][5]

## GST and other taxes

- **GST**: 5% on most goods and services; as a share of your income, the effective rate depends on how much you spend vs. save/invest.[4]
- Alberta has **no provincial sales tax** and no health premiums, but there are payroll contributions (CPP, EI), property tax, fuel tax, etc., which all effectively raise your “all‑in” tax burden.[4]
