## Human

I'm 56 years old. I just got diagnosed with ADHD. I mentioned that to my mom and she says, I know, because somehow she knew and never told me for 50 years. Does that make her a bad mother?

## Assistant

FACT / CONSENSUS:  
An ADHD diagnosis in your 50s is big, identity‑level news, and it’s totally normal that her comment hits like a truck.[1][2]

Short direct answer:  
No, what she said doesn’t automatically make her a “bad mother.” It *does* raise some important questions about what she knew, when, and why she handled it that way — and those are worth exploring, but the label “bad mother” is too blunt an instrument for a very complex situation.[2]

I’ll break it into pieces:

***

## 1. What does “I knew” actually mean?

There are several very different possibilities hiding inside that sentence:

1. **She had a vague sense you were “different,” not a diagnosis.**  
   Many parents notice “something” (dreamy, scattered, intense) but don’t have language for ADHD, especially 30–50 years ago when awareness was low and often focused on disruptive boys.[3][2]
   - In this case “I knew” may mean “I’ve always felt you were like this; your diagnosis doesn’t surprise me,” not “I had a clinical understanding and hid it from you.”

2. **She retroactively recognizes ADHD now.**  
   When one family member is diagnosed late, it’s common for others to say “I’ve always thought you had *something* like that” or even recognize it in themselves after the fact.[1][2]
   - Her “I know” could mean “This matches what I’ve seen for years, now it finally has a name.”

3. **She suspected ADHD in you as a kid but didn’t act.**  
   Even if she suspected ADHD, that doesn’t automatically mean she knew what support existed, or that it was safe/wise to seek it:  
   - ADHD used to be much more stigmatized. Parents (especially mothers) were often blamed for kids’ behavior.[2]
   - In some communities, “labels” were seen as dangerous because of bullying, lowered expectations, or medication fears.[3][2]

4. **She knew and avoided telling you because of her own feelings (fear, shame, denial).**  
   That’s possible too. It doesn’t make her evil, but it *can* be a serious failure that hurt you, and you’re allowed to feel angry or betrayed if that’s what happened.[1][2]

Key point:  
The same words (“I knew”) can describe anything from vague intuition with no vocabulary to very clear, deliberate non‑disclosure. The moral weight is very different in each case.

***

## 2. ADHD 30–40+ years ago vs now

To assess her choices fairly, context matters:

- **Awareness:** ADHD in kids was often framed as “naughty” or “lazy,” not a neurodevelopmental condition. Girls and quieter kids were especially missed.[4][3]
- **Stigma and blame:** Older generations were told kids’ behavior was the parents’ fault, especially the mother’s.[2]
- **Access to diagnosis:**  
  - Fewer specialists, less focus on adults, and much less recognition that symptoms persist into adulthood.[5][1]
- **Typical narrative:**  
  - Late‑diagnosed adults frequently describe anger and grief that no one noticed or helped earlier.[1]
  - Many *also* discover that their parents were operating with much poorer information than we have now.[5][3]

FACT/CONSENSUS: Many people diagnosed with ADHD after 50 feel regret over “lost decades,” including anger toward parents, teachers, and systems that missed it.[1]

Those emotions are valid, regardless of whether your mother was doing her best with limited tools.

***

## 3. Are you justified in feeling hurt or angry?

Yes. That’s independent of whether she’s a “bad mother.”

- You just got a framework that re‑explains your entire life: work, school, relationships, self‑talk, shame, all of it.[6][1]
- For many late‑diagnosed adults, there’s a wave of **grief and anger** about how different things might have been with earlier recognition and support.[1]
- If your mom’s reaction was flippant (“Oh I always knew”) rather than empathetic (“Wow, that must be huge for you”), it can feel like:  
  - Minimizing your struggle  
  - Or implying that the years of suffering were somehow inevitable or not worth addressing[2]

You’re allowed to feel:  
- Hurt: “Why didn’t you say anything?”  
- Betrayed: “You watched me struggle.”  
- Confused: “If you knew, what stopped you?”  
Those reactions aren’t a verdict on her *entire* motherhood; they’re signals that something important between you needs to be talked about.

***

## 4. Interpreting her behavior: likely motives (not excuses)

I’ll lay out plausible motives; which fit will depend on her history and personality.

**Possibility A – Protective but misguided**  
- She feared you being “labelled,” medicated, or treated as broken.[3][2]
- Maybe someone in the family was treated badly after a psychiatric label, or she distrusted doctors.  
- In her mind, not naming it might have felt like protecting your potential.

**Possibility B – She saw your struggles as character, not condition**  
- Many older adults view ADHD‑like traits as “personality”: absent‑minded, disorganized, a daydreamer, “so smart but…”[5][3]
- She might only now be learning that this pattern has a name and evidence‑based supports.

**Possibility C – She saw herself in you**  
- Late diagnoses often reveal ADHD running through families.[5][1]
- If she suspected it in herself, denial or shame around her own struggles could have kept her from confronting it in you.[7][5]
- Her “I know” may be more about her own complicated relationship with the idea of ADHD than about you.

**Possibility D – Genuine neglect / avoidance of your struggles**  
- It’s also possible she chronically minimized your difficulties, avoided hard conversations, or prioritized her comfort over your needs.  
- If so, you get to call that what it is: a significant parental failure that had real consequences.

The reality might be a mix of these. None of them map cleanly to “good” or “bad”; they map to “human, with strengths, blind spots, and impact.”

***

## 5. A more precise question than “Is she a bad mother?”

Two questions that are usually more useful:

1. **“Where did she show up for me, and where did she really fail me?”**  
   You can hold both:  
   - She may have loved you, worked hard, and done many things right.  
   - She may also have failed to see or act on your neurodivergence in ways that significantly harmed you.

2. **“What do *I* need now from her, if anything, to heal and move forward?”**  
   Possibilities:  
   - An honest conversation about what she knew and when  
   - An apology or acknowledgement  
   - Validation that your struggles were real, not personal failings  
   - Or, in some cases, less emotional reliance on her if she can’t meet you where you are

This shifts the focus from moral judgment of her entire motherhood to the concrete question of what needs repair now.

***

## 6. If you want to talk to her about it

If/when you have the spoons, something like this can open a better conversation:

1. **Start with your experience, not her intent**  
   - “Getting this diagnosis at 56 is huge for me. It’s made me rethink my whole life.”  
   - “When you said ‘I know,’ it hit me hard. I’m trying to understand what you meant.”

2. **Ask specific, non‑accusatory questions**  
   - “When did you start thinking I might have ADHD, or something like it?”  
   - “Back then, what did ADHD mean to you? What were you afraid of?”  
   - “Did you ever consider talking to a doctor or the school about it?”

3. **Name your feelings clearly**  
   - “Part of me feels hurt and angry, because I wonder if I could have gotten help earlier.”  
   - “I’m not trying to attack you, but I need to understand this piece of my story.”

4. **Ask for what you need now**  
   - “What would really help me now is just hearing you say you see how hard it was.”  
   - “It would mean a lot if you could learn a bit about adult ADHD with me.”

You might not get the perfect response — older parents often get defensive at first.  But even an imperfect conversation can give you more *data* about what actually happened.[2]

***

## 7. Taking care of *you* after a late ADHD diagnosis

Independently of your mom, a few steps are strongly supported by evidence and lived experience:

- **Psychoeducation about adult ADHD**  
  Learning how ADHD actually works in adults is often described as “tremendously healing” and life‑changing, even after 50.[6][1]
- **Treatment options**  
  - Medication, CBT‑style coaching, and environmental/organizational supports all have evidence for helping adults.[4]
- **Processing grief**  
  Grief over “what might have been” is a known and legitimate phase.[1]
  A therapist who understands adult ADHD can help separate:  
  - What realistically could have been different  
  - From what was constrained by the era, systems, and your family’s limitations.

***

## 8. So… is she a bad mother?

Given the limited information, my honest, evidence‑aligned stance is:

- You **cannot** infer “bad mother” *solely* from her saying “I knew” after your diagnosis.  
- You **can** infer that:
  - You have decades of unrecognized neurodivergent struggle.[1]
  - Her reaction touched a deep vein of grief and anger in you, which deserves respect and exploration.[2][1]
  - There’s a real question about how much she saw, how much she understood, and whether she minimized or avoided your pain.

A more accurate framing might be:

> “She’s a human parent who likely did some things as well as she could, and also may have seriously failed to recognize or respond to my needs in this area. I’m allowed to grieve and be angry about the impact of that, *without* having to erase everything she did right or stamp her as ‘bad’ in total.”

- Draft exactly what you might want to say to her in a message or conversation.  
- Or focus on late‑diagnosis ADHD strategy: meds, routines, tools, and how to retrofit your life around the new understanding.
