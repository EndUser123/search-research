## Human

Was there a cyber attack on the US water affecting 7 states?

## Assistant

Yes — a coordinated cyberattack targeted water and wastewater systems in at least seven U.S. states over the past week, and it remains an active federal investigation.[1][2]

## What happened

The campaign began in Minnesota, where more than 30 community water systems were targeted on July 26–27, prompting the state to activate its cybersecurity incident response capabilities. Related incidents then surfaced in Michigan (nine municipal systems), Wisconsin, and South Dakota (Rapid City wastewater lift stations), with sources telling ABC News that Georgia was also affected, bringing the confirmed total to at least seven states. The FBI and Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) issued a joint alert on July 30 warning of "malicious cyber actors" remotely tampering with water infrastructure nationwide.[3][4][5][1]

## How the attackers operated

Hackers targeted internet-exposed programmable logic controllers (PLCs) — the hardware that directly monitors and controls treatment processes — by cracking or changing passwords and altering network/IP configurations to lock out legitimate operators. In some cases this caused flooding, loss of water pressure, or outright shutdowns of water supply, forcing utilities to switch to manual operations and issue boil-water advisories as a precaution. One utility also reported unauthorized changes to the software automating plant functions.[6][7][4][3]

## Impact on drinking water

Despite the disruption to control systems, officials have consistently said there is no evidence that drinking water was contaminated or that public health was at risk in any affected state. Michigan officials specifically stated all systems "continued to operate safely" with "no known impacts that posed a public health concern".[4][8]

## Who's behind it

U.S. and state officials, along with intelligence sources, suspect Iranian-linked hacking groups based on the tactics used, and federal officials reportedly briefed Minnesota leaders that Iran is a potential culprit. However, no U.S. agency has formally attributed the attacks to Iran, and investigators note the hackers have not demanded ransom — suggesting the goal may be disruption rather than financial gain. Notably, President Trump has instead publicly blamed Minnesota and its governor for the state's water issues rather than pointing to Iran.[9][10][8][3]

This event is distinct from, but part of a broader pattern following, an October 2024 cyberattack on American Water, the largest U.S. water utility, which disrupted billing systems but not water operations. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) had already warned in late July of a "significant increase" in threat actors targeting water-sector PLCs and urged operators to take them off the public internet.[11][12]

## Human

Any other cyber attacks affecting public utilities or services in Canada?

## Assistant

Yes — Canada has experienced several notable cyberattacks on public utilities, though most have been less physically disruptive than the recent U.S. water incidents, and Canadian officials warn the threat is rising sharply.[1]

## Recent incidents

Nova Scotia Power suffered a major breach beginning March 19, 2025, when an employee clicked a malicious pop-up link infected with "SocGholish" malware, likely by a Russian threat actor; the compromise exposed personal data—including Social Insurance Numbers—of over 900,000 current and former customers, though the utility found no evidence its operational infrastructure was at risk. In response, Nova Scotia Power has committed to deleting SINs from its systems and undergoing an external security assessment, and the province's energy board is conducting a two-part investigation.[2][3]

In September 2023, a pro-Russian hacking group claimed responsibility for a distributed denial-of-service (DoS) attack that knocked Hydro-Québec's website and app offline; Hydro-Québec confirmed no personal data or critical systems were compromised. That same week, the group also claimed credit for taking down the Prime Minister's Office website, and leaked U.S. intelligence documents suggested Russian-backed hackers had separately gained access to Canada's natural gas distribution network — though CSE confirmed no physical damage occurred.[4][5]

## Broader pattern and warnings

Canada's Communications Security Establishment (CSE) and the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security warned in October 2025 of hacktivists actively tampering with internet-connected industrial control systems across water, energy, and agricultural facilities — citing a real incident where a water pressure disturbance disrupted service to a local community, plus false alarms triggered at an oil and gas company and a grain silo. Following that, Canada's Minister of National Defence and Minister of Public Safety issued a joint statement in November 2025 explicitly stating that malicious cyber activity against power, water, health, finance, and transportation infrastructure "are on the rise and are a real and urgent threat," attributing activity to both nation-state-linked actors and non-state groups.[6][7][1]

## Historical context

These recent events build on a longer pattern: CSE has assessed since at least 2020 that state-sponsored actors (including Russian and Chinese-linked groups) are "very likely" developing capabilities to disrupt Canada's electricity supply, and Iranian hacking groups have separately targeted industrial control systems internationally. A notable earlier case involved Telvent Canada, a Calgary-based supplier of control systems for electrical grids, municipal water systems, transit, and oil/gas pipelines, whose corporate network was breached, potentially exposing project files related to critical infrastructure clients.[8][9][10]
