# 9 Tools to DOMINATE as an ARCHITECT in 2026
I used to spend days on things that now take 
me minutes. We all know the drill. Waiting  

for renders, fighting with clunky BIM files, or 
scrolling endlessly through 3D libraries looking  

for that one specific chair. It's exhausting, and 
frankly, it burns through the budget. But in 2026,  

my workflow looks completely different. It's about 
a stack of nine specific tools that talk to each  

other. We are moving from the first sketches in 
the browser to building our own custom software  

applications for clients. Today I'm walking you 
through the exact software stack I'm using right  

now to run my architecture practice efficiently. 
First up is Rayon. For the longest time,  

we were stuck between sketching on an iPad and 
opening heavy software like Revit or AutoCAD  

just to draw a simple floor plan. Rayon bridges 
that gap. Think of it as the Figma or Google Docs  

of CAD. It runs entirely in the browser which 
changes everything because I can send a link  

to a client or a colleague and we can work on 
the drawing simultaneously. But the real power  

lies in its smart tables and styles. So you go 
from a technical black and white schematic to a  

textured shadowed presentation ready plan without 
ever touching Photoshop. It supports DWG and DXF  

import and export. So it fits perfectly into 
the traditional pipeline when you need it to.  

Once I have a layout, I need to understand the 
volume. For those very first 3D massing studies,  

I use Spacio. Before I even open the BIM tool, I'm 
in the web browser with Spacio. It's designed for  

informative design. This means as I'm pushing 
and pulling the 3D shapes of the building,  

the dashboard on the right is updating in real 
time. It calculates my floor area ratio, gross  

floor area, and even gives me immediate feedback 
on solar exposure and wind comfort. In the past,  

we design a building and then check if it met 
the zoning requirements. Spaceio flips that. You  

design with the data visible. It's the quickest 
way to validate an idea and ensure it's actually  

buildable before you commit serious hours to it. 
Once the concept is solid, I move to Autodesk  

Forma. This is the heavy hitter for site analysis. 
While Spacio is great for the building itself,  

Forma is incredible for the context. I 
can pull in high resolution terrain data,  

surrounding buildings, and road networks 
for almost anywhere in the world instantly.  

We use it to run granular analysis, microclimate 
studies, noise propagation from nearby traffic  

and operational energy predictions. But 
the reason it's in my 2026 stack is the  

integration. Forma has a direct bridge to Revit. 
I can take that validated, analyzed final sketch,  

hit one button and it populates my Revit file with 
native walls, floors and topography. It eliminates  

that painful phase of remodeling everything from 
scratch when you move to BIM. This next tool is  

what separates the modern architect from the 
traditional one. AI Studio. We are entering an  

era where architects need to be tool makers. AI 
Studio allows you to use models like Gemini 3.0 to  

build actual functional web applications without 
needing to be a fullstack developer. For example,  

I built an internal tool for a firm called the 
vacancy checker. It connects to the project  

database and visualizes which apartments 
are rented versus vacant in real time. I  

even implemented a real backend using Firebase 
and Google Cloud, so the data is persistent.  

I'm also working on a nodebased workflow manager 
that allows me to save complex AI prompt chains  

and share them with my team. It turns prompt 
engineering into a repeatable software asset.  

I'll be releasing that application soon, so keep 
an eye out. For research, I rely on Notebook LM.  

Standard AI models often hallucinate, which is a 
disaster for compliance. Notebook LM is different  

because it stays strictly grounded in the sources 
you provide. I actually tested this by uploading  

my own YouTube channel transcripts. It now answers 
questions using my specific knowledge base.

I also love the new deep search agent. I use it to 
scout topics like new Archviz AI tools. It scours  

the web, reads the articles, and synthesizes 
a report with real sources. It's like having a  

dedicated research assistant. For the day-to-day 
glue that holds this workflow together, I use  

Gemini. It's my general purpose assistant. I use 
the gems feature to create custom personas. For  

instance, I have a gem called Nanoverse 2. This 
gem is trained specifically on image prompting.  

I can upload a render I found on Pinterest or 
Arc Daily that has a specific mood I love and  

ask it to reverse engineer that style. It analyzes 
the lighting, the camera angle, and the materials  

and gives me a precise text prompt I can use 
to generate similar concepts. This works great  

because Gemini is able to analyze the input images 
perfectly. The output image is really close to  

the original image. Let's talk about dressing 
the scene. Finding specific 3D assets used to  

be a massive bottleneck. My go-to solution now is 
Hunyuan 3D. It's an open-source model that solves  

the biggest problem with AI generation, geometry. 
The massive advantage here is component splitting.  

Most AI generators give you a single fused blob 
of geometry. Hunyuan understands structure. It  

creates the mesh in a way that allows me to 
actually separate the cushion from the wooden  

legs. This is crucial for a pro workflow. I 
can import that clean geometry into 3ds Max  

and apply my own highquality V-Ray materials. 
But even if I'm in a rush, I can just use  

Hunyuan's native texturing option. It works 
perfectly fine for most background objects,  

giving me a solid asset in under a minute. For 
the final high-end imagery, I'm using Nano Banana  

Pro accessed via Google AI Studio. The range of 
applications is massive. I use it for instance  

style transfer where I can take a raw 3D viewport 
screenshot and steal the mood, lighting, and  

textures from a reference image while keeping my 
geometry perfectly intact. It's also a lifesaver  

for furniture swapping and material testing. If 
a client wants to see a different sofa or change  

the brick facade to natural stone, I can swap 
those elements out in seconds using a reference  

photo without ever reopening my 3D software. 
But it goes even deeper. It can turn messy  

hand sketches into clean CAD drawings, generate 
photorealistic 3D floor plans from 2D outlines,  

and even create artistic site plans from Google 
Maps screenshots. It even handles outpainting,  

allowing you to extend a landscape render into 
a vertical format for social media flawlessly.  

I've uploaded several detailed videos on the 
channel specifically about Nano Banana Pro where  

I deep dive into these 13 plus use cases. You 
definitely need to check those out to see the full  

potential of the prompts. Finally, to sell the 
dream, we need motion. Kling 2.6 is currently the  

leader in AI video generation for architecture. 
Static images are great, but clients buy feelings  

and cling allows me to animate my renders with 
terrifying realism. The killer feature here is the  

start frame and end frame control. I can take two 
renders, say an empty room and a furnished one,  

and Kling will perfectly interpolate the 
transition. I even used this to recreate a  

professional 3ds Max animation project. Instead of 
rendering 850 individual frames in Chaos Vantage,  

I only rendered 10 key frames and let Kling 
handle the movement. It saved me 99% of my  

render time. Beyond camera moves like dollies 
and trucks, I use it for physics simulations.  

I can animate curtains swaying in the wind, 
shadows moving across a wall as the sun sets,  

or even water reflecting in a pool. It can 
even bring people into your scenes with natural  

movement, making a static render feel like a 
lift space. I have a dedicated video on the  

channel showing exactly how I replaced those 
850 renders with a single prompt in Kling.  

The goal isn't to let AI do the design. It's to 
let AI do the grunt work so we can focus on the  

architecture. These tools allow me to punch way 
above my weight class and deliver quality that  

used to require a team of 10 people. If you want 
to see a deep dive into that custom node app I  

mentioned, make sure you are subscribed. Thanks 
for watching and I'll see you in the next build.
