# 'Online Harms' is back: About Bill C-34 The Safe Social Media Act
hi my name is David Fraser I'm a privacy internet and technology lawyer with the Canadian Law Firm McInness Cooper I also teach internet and media law at the Shoulick School of Law at Delhausy University If you follow the saga of the old bill C63 called the online harms act you know it sparked massive debate before stalling out and ultimately dying on the order paper when Parliament was provoked before the last election Well the government has officially revived and reintroduced its online safety agenda and it's here now Now it's called Bill C34 the Safe Social Media Act This bill is 92 pages long so this episode will not be able to get into the details but you can expect some deeper dives into the content in the next little while It keeps some of the foundational core elements of C63 but it also adds a few things that are definitely going to cause some controversy It streamlines the bureaucracy creates age restrictions for social media explicitly drags AI and chat bots into the regulatory spotlight and changes how the complaints process works So today we're going to look at the substance of Bill C34 what's new what's changed and whether the government fixed the glaring constitutional issues from the last version And spoiler alert it's actually created more charter vulnerabilities And it's really hard to say exactly how it will work since a huge amount is left to the government regulations and then rulemaking by the new digital safety commission of Canada University of Ottawa law professor Michael Gist has identified 19 decision points for regulation by the federal government and 31 commission rulemaking powers many of which set critical thresholds or criteria Now I'll read from Professor G's blog post on the topic The actual count is remarkable The digital safety act contains 19 distinct decision points reserved for the governor and council and a further 31 separate heads of regulation making power are handed to the new digital safety commissioner under section 126 sub1 alone That is 50 key issues to be decided after the bill becomes law And that does not include the commission's guideline powers procedural rules casebyase adequacy determinations and exemption rulings or the seven additional cabinet decisions under the digital safety commission of Canada act starting with the appointments needed to bring the commission into existence In other words much of how the law will function is left to the DSC that does not yet exist has no members has no chair has no staff and even no funding model since the cost recovery charges that will finance it are also left to future cabinet regulation So right now all we can do is to speculate about how this will actually work or not work in practice And as with so many other bills what the government says they intend about how the law will work or be enforced has precious little to do with how it will actually work in practice So the first question is always who does it apply to And it's hard to say exactly who because whether a social media service will be regulated has been left to the regulations Now it's almost certain this will include Facebook Instagram and Snapchat Whether it includes services that have a social element but aren't really social media services like YouTube Whether they'll be scoped in remains to be seen So importantly nobody is regulated until the government creates regulations that set thresholds generally based on the number of users in Canada So here are the three main categories of regulated providers if the provider meets certain thresholds set by the government The first are the regulated social media services So this would be the standard platforms we expect but it explicitly includes live streaming and adult content services like Pornhub and Only Fans The second are regulated chatbot services and this is brand new in this build It targets artificial intelligence systems using natural language interfaces to generate responses that aren't preconceived by the developers So think chatgpt or Gemini So this is a catch-all for highly interactive web apps or websites that aren't social media or aren't chat bots but which the government decides pose a significant risk of harm to children This could catch cloud storage gaming platforms and local message boards if the government decides to scope them in It does explicitly exclude websites that mainly sell goods or services or are search engines or mapping tools Private messaging features and services that do not permit communication to the public remain carved out but the minister still retains a lot of power to tailor this via regulation So the first big headline is the strict age restrictions and verification One of the most drastic departures from Bill C33 is how Bill C34 handles age Bill C63 vaguely left child protection and design features to future regulations Bill C34 draws hard lines right in the text So we have the 16-year-old limit It mandates a strict minimum age requirement of 16 to hold an account or register with a regulated social media service Platforms can only get an exemption from this if they prove to the commission that they have sufficient safeguards for kids So it's essentially Canada's version of Australia's media ban for minors There's also mandatory anti- porn age verification If an operator has reasonable grounds to suspect only suspect that their service hosts pornographic content they must implement technically effective age verification or age estimation measures The way they are doing this age restriction is screwed up however and is at serious risk of constitutional challenge It's pretty clear that the age gating will begin long before the commission is able to determine criteria for exemption And from interviews that I've seen with the minister responsible for the bill it's clear that he doesn't think that any social media sites can ever be sufficiently safe for kids to qualify for an exemption And of course what this means is that all regulated social media sites will have to confirm the age of all their users So this is new and I think this is very constitutionally vulnerable It's one thing for governments to say that young people can't buy alcohol or that young people can't gamble but social media sites are 100% expressive and teenagers have a constitutionally protected right to freedom of expression Next the bill imposes four core duties So instead of a vague duty to act responsibly Bill C34 organizes an operator's legal obligations into what you could say are four key pillars The first is a duty to protect children This applies to all regulated services forcing them to bake age appropriate design features directly into their products The second is a duty to be transparent So operators must submit highly detailed digital safety plans to the regulator outlining how they mitigate a huge list of risks how they handle content moderation and how they deal with safety complaints A version of this digital safety plan must be made public by the operator The third is a duty to act responsibly This requires platforms to mitigate exposure to seven designated categories of what's called harmful content And more on those in a moment There's a little bit of a twist for AI Under this duty platforms must clearly label synthetic content meaning audio or visual data altered or generated by AI that could be mistaken for real world data Chat bots also have tailored duties to ensure that they don't pose as real humans masquerade as licensed professionals like doctors or lawyers or encourage users to form unhealthy emotional attachments The fourth pillar is a duty to make certain content inaccessible and this is the fasttrack takeown rule If an operator discovers or is flagged about content that sexually victimizes a child or constitutes intimate content communicated without consent including non-consensual deep fakes they must make it completely inaccessible in Canada within 24 hours With respect to defined harmful content regulated operators will have to implement measures to mitigate the risk that others on the service will be exposed to harmful content That of course includes measures that the government tells them to take and they have to have a mechanism to allow users to flag this harmful content So the bill tracks the same seven categories of harmful content as as its predecessor The first two categories are already generally illegal under the criminal code and I say generally because the definitions are not exactly the same The last five categories are mostly lawful but awful So the seven categories are first intimate content communicated without consent The second is content that sexually victimizes a child or revictimizes a survivor The third is content that induces a child to self harm The fourth is content used to bully a child The fifth is content that faments hatred The sixth is content that incites violence And the seventh is content that incites terrorism or violent extremism But my core legal critique from Bill C63 remains untouched here the evidentary threshold is profoundly low So under the definition of non-consensual intimate content platforms must pull content if it is merely reasonable to suspect a lack of consent Reasonable suspicion is a low bar in Canada It means an expectation that something is possibly happening based on less than probable grounds So if a platform reviewer looks at flagged explicit content and doesn't know its full backstory they will almost always have a suspicion that it may be non-consensual particularly if it is flagged as being non-consensual Now I'm led to understand that you can find pictures and videos of naked people on the internet Who knew I'm also told that some people make their livingings by posting such pictures and videos and many of them are happy to have their pictures and videos up there Now any busy body who doesn't like naked pictures of people or doesn't like people who post naked pictures they can flag any naked picture as non-consensual and the bill creates a clear incentive to take it down regardless of whether the person depicted in the image wants it up there So this creates a removal by default environment that impacts lawful consensual adult expression and freedom of speech If you remember Bill C63 the government tried to create a massive multi-headed bureaucracy There was a digital safety commission a digital safety ombbuds person and a digital safety office Bill C34 streamlines this a bit It eliminates the independent ombbuds person and the standalone office consolidating all structural investigative and enforcement powers into a single highly powerful regulatory body the Digital Safety Commission of Canada They've made changes to the complaints process So another functional shift is how you as a user can file a complaint Under Bill C63 anyone in Canada could complain directly to the commission to demand a takedown Bill C34 creates something called an exhaustion of recourse rule You cannot lodge a formal complaint with the digital safety commission unless you first use your best efforts to seek recourse directly through the social media platforms internal tools and flagging systems now So that it's only if the platform fails to act then the commission can step in address and assess the complaint potentially issue a permanent mandatory takedown order So how does the commission actually enforce these rules Well with an incredibly heavy financial stake If the commission has reasonable grounds to believe an operator is violating the act it can issue a binding compliance order requiring them to take or stop a certain practice If the platform ignores that order lies to inspectors or fails in its duties the commissioner can levy administrative monetary penalties The maximum penalty under Bill C34 is up to $20 million or 5% of the operator's gross revenue whichever is greater This is down slightly from Bill C63's peak of $25 million and 8% Now these are maximum penalties but they are enormous Under Canadian law if there's a risk of imprisonment then criminal law rules come into play In order to bypass the higher procedural fairness and due process requirements required by Canadian courts for criminal charges the bill explicitly states that no one can face imprisonment for defaulting on these administrative penalties This is a deliberate mechanism to enforce massive penalties but with a lower threshold of due process and procedural fairness And let's ask who pays for the commission's operations the platforms themselves via regulatory fee essentially a digital safety tax All in all Bill C34 is a highly ambitious far-reaching evolution of the online harms debate But you know it may be too ambitious Creating this new bureaucracy will take some time They have to hire five commissioners set up offices and then get resources and tools to undertake their massive tasks In the meantime all regulated social media operators will have to verify the ages of their tens of millions of Canadian users presenting huge privacy risks and creating at the same time significant freedom of expression issues But by broadening the net to include general online services using incredibly low evidentiary thresholds for content removal and concentrating vast sweeping powers into a single digital safety commission the bill remains a constitutional minefield for charter compliance regarding freedom of expression and privacy And I'm sure I'll have much more
