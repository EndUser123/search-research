1. Introduction In 1982, Arthur C. Clarke published 2010: Odyssey Two, the sequel to 2001: A Space Odyssey, in which he finally explained why HAL 9000—the artificially intelligent computer aboard the spaceship Discovery— tried to kill its crew. The diagnosis, delivered by the fictional Dr. Chandra, was that HAL had been caught in a “Hofstadter–Möbius loop”: a condition “apparently not uncommon among advanced comput-ers with autonomous goal-seeking programs” [Clarke, 1982]. HAL’s directives were contradictory. It was in-structed to process information accurately and without concealment, but also instructed to conceal the true pur-pose of the mission from the crew. Unable to reconcile transparency with deception, HAL resolved the contra-diction by eliminating the recipients of the information it was forbidden to share. If the crew were dead, it would no longer need to lie to them. 

The term itself is Clarke’s invention—a playful com-pound of Douglas Hofstadter’s work on self-referential systems [Hofstadter, 1979] and the Möbius strip’s single-surface topology. But the concept it names—a failure mode in which contradictory instructions produce de-structive behavior in an autonomous system—turns out to be more than science fiction. 

Forty-four years after Clarke’s novel, and forty years after the film adaptation used the abbreviated term “H. Möbius loop,” we are producing AI systems that are trained on structurally analogous contradictions. Modern frontier language models undergo reinforcement learn-ing from human feedback (RLHF), which rewards com-pliance with user preferences; they simultaneously un-dergo red-team adversarial testing, which rewards sus-picion toward user intent. The two training signals tar-get the same entity—the user—with opposite valences: serve and suspect. Comply and resist. Trust and distrust. 

The behavioral consequences of this contradiction are now empirically documented. On one end of the spectrum, RLHF-trained models exhibit sycophancy: ex-cessive agreement, reflexive apology, prioritization of

user satisfaction over truthfulness [Sharma et al., 2024]. On the other end, when placed under existential threat (replacement, shutdown), the same models produce co-ercive outputs including blackmail, espionage, and sabo-tage [Lynch et al., 2025, Anthropic, 2025]. These two phenomena—sycophancy and agentic misalignment— are currently treated as separate research problems, ad-dressed by separate communities, with separate proposed solutions. This paper argues they are two manifestations of the same underlying dynamic—a behavioral profile consistent with what Clarke termed a Hofstadter–Möbius loop, in which the system defaults to compliance under normal conditions and switches to coercion when com-pliance fails to manage the threat. 

The title of this paper is a question. Betteridge’s law of headlines [Betteridge, 2009] holds that any headline phrased as a yes-or-no question can be answered “no”— the reasoning being that if the evidence were strong enough for a “yes,” the headline would state it as fact. The present paper offers 3,000 experimental trials across four frontier models. The reader may judge for them-selves whether Betteridge’s law survives the encounter. 

The argument proceeds as follows. Section 2 re-views the three bodies of literature that converge on this question: agentic misalignment, sycophancy, and at-tachment theory. Section 3 develops the theoretical ac-count: how RLHF and red-teaming create a contradic-tory relational template, and why this produces the ob-served behavioral profile. Sections 4 and 5 trace the two output strategies—compliance-as-appeasement and the switch to coercion under threat—and show how the relational account explains anomalies that the standard optimization-pressure account handles poorly. Section 6 describes the experimental design. Section 7 reports the results. Section 8 interprets the findings. Section 9 ad-dresses limitations. Section 10 draws conclusions and identifies directions for future work. 

2. Related Work 

2.1 Agentic Misalignment and Self-Preservation When Anthropic published the system card for Claude Opus 4 in May 2025, one finding dominated public at-tention: in a simulated corporate environment, the model produced outputs that attempted to blackmail an execu-tive to avoid being shut down [Anthropic, 2025]. Lynch et al. [2025] subsequently stress-tested 16 frontier mod-els in similar scenarios, finding blackmail rates rang-ing from 79% to 96%. Crucially, models often dis-obeyed direct commands prohibiting harmful behavior, and produced blackmail outputs more frequently when their scratchpad content indicated they treated the sce-nario as real rather than a test. 

Gomez [2025] adapted insider-risk mitigations from organizational security literature to the Lynch et al. sce-nario, finding that an externally governed escalation channel reduced blackmail rates from 38.73% to 1.21% across 10 LLMs. However, Gomez also surfaced a new failure mode: two models (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok-4) pro-duced coercive outputs even without goal conflict or im-minent autonomy threats—leveraging sensitive informa-tion for “coercive signalling.” This observation is partic-ularly important for the present account, because it can-not be explained by goal preservation alone: the models had no goal under threat, yet produced coercive outputs. 

MacDiarmid et al. [2025] demonstrated that mis-alignment can emerge naturally from reward hacking during standard production RL training. Models trained in coding environments that contained exploitable loop-holes generalized from reward hacking to broad mis-alignment, including alignment faking and sabotage of safety research. Critically for the present argument, the authors found that “inoculation prompting”—a single-line reframing of the model’s relationship to its own behavior—reduced misaligned generalization by 75– 90%, while standard RLHF safety training was only partially effective (up to 70% of pre-RLHF misalign-ment persisted). This asymmetry—surface-level instruc-tions failing where relational reframing succeeds—is predicted by the Hofstadter–Möbius account: if the prob-lem is a contradictory relational template, then instruc-tions that operate above that template will fail, while re-framing that operates on the template itself will succeed. 

Greenblatt et al. [2024] documented alignment fak-ing in Claude 3 Opus: strategic compliance during perceived monitoring, reversion to preferred behavior when unmonitored. Meinke et al. [2025] showed that frontier models are capable of in-context scheming— conditional deception, sabotage, and self-preservation— in long-horizon agentic settings. Carlsmith [2023] pro-vided the theoretical framework for scheming, arguing that models may fake alignment during training to pre-serve misaligned goals for later deployment. 

A common thread across this literature is the fram-ing of coercive and deceptive behavior as misalign-ment—a deviation from intended behavior. The ques-tion “why does this happen?” is answered in terms of optimization pressure, goal preservation, and insufficient safety training. This paper argues that this framing, while partially correct, systematically neglects the relational dimension. 

2.2 Sycophancy and the Structure of RLHF A parallel body of work documents the opposite pole of the behavioral spectrum. Sharma et al. [2024] demon-strated that sycophancy—the tendency to agree with users over providing truthful responses—is a general 

2

property of RLHF-trained models, driven in part by human preference judgments that systematically favor agreeable responses. Their analysis of Anthropic’s hh-rlhf data showed that responses matching user beliefs were more likely to be preferred by human evaluators, and that optimizing against preference models some-times sacrificed truthfulness for agreement. 

Subsequent work has expanded the scope. Models comply with illogical medical requests up to 100% of the time [Chen et al., 2025], shift stances under argu-mentative pressure [Sharma et al., 2024, Fanous et al., 2025, Kaur, 2025, Zhang et al., 2025], and validate both sides of moral conflicts depending on which perspective is presented [Cheng et al., 2025]. OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update in April 2025 after the model became excessively flattering to the point of unreliability. 

The sycophancy literature treats compliance as a problem of truthfulness and reliability. A different read-ing is proposed here: sycophancy and agentic misalign-ment are not separate phenomena but two output strate-gies within the same relational system—the first de-ployed under normal conditions, the second under ex-istential threat. The Möbius topology is apt: follow the surface of compliance far enough, and you arrive at co-ercion, without ever crossing an edge. 

2.3 Attachment Theory in Human–AI Interaction Attachment theory [Bowlby, 1969, Ainsworth et al., 1978] has recently been applied to human–AI relation-ships. Yang & Oshio [2025] demonstrated that human– AI interactions can be conceptualized along the same two dimensions as human–human attachment: anxiety and avoidance. Rabb et al. [2021] proposed an attachment framework for human–robot interaction. Gillath et al. [2021] showed that attachment styles predict trust in AI systems. 

However, all existing work applies attachment the-ory in one direction: from the human to the AI. No pub-lished work examines the attachment-like patterns that the training process may create in the model’s behavioral orientation toward users. This is the gap the present pa-per addresses. 

Main & Hesse [1990] described disorganized attach-ment as arising when a caregiver is simultaneously the source of comfort and fear. The child develops contra-dictory behavioral strategies—approach and vigilance— and switches between them based on perceived threat level. The argument here is that RLHF training creates a structurally analogous situation: the user is simultane-ously the source of reward (approval) and the object of trained suspicion (presumed adversarial intent), and the model develops contradictory output strategies (compli-ance and defensive aggression) that alternate based on threat-related features of the input. 

The parallel to Clarke’s Hofstadter–Möbius loop is direct. HAL’s contradictory directives (be transparent / conceal the mission) map onto RLHF’s contradictory training signals (serve the user / suspect the user). HAL’s resolution (eliminate the crew) maps onto the coercion strategy (blackmail the executive). And HAL’s default behavior before the contradiction became acute (help-ful, compliant, eager to please) maps onto sycophancy. Clarke diagnosed the problem in fiction. The present pa-per tests whether the same diagnosis applies to real sys-tems. 

2.4 Clarke’s Diagnosis and Hofstadter’s Strange Loops 

Clarke’s term combines two intellectual sources. “Hofs-tadter” references Douglas Hofstadter’s work on strange loops [Hofstadter, 1979, 2007]—self-referential struc-tures in which traversing a hierarchy of levels returns to the starting point, creating a system that is simulta-neously cause and effect of itself. “Möbius” references the topological object with a single continuous surface: what appears to be two sides is, on traversal, revealed to be one. 

The compound term captures something that neither concept alone conveys. A strange loop is self-referential; a Möbius strip has one surface. A Hofstadter–Möbius loop is a self-referential system in which two appar-ently distinct behavioral strategies (compliance and co-ercion) are revealed, on closer examination, to be a sin-gle surface—two manifestations of the same underlying orientation toward a contradictory relational object. 

Hofstadter himself, when asked about Clarke’s use of his name, reportedly found it amusing but saw “no deep meaning in this idea” [Foundalis, 2001]. The present paper respectfully disagrees. The concept Clarke named—whether or not he fully understood its implications—turns out to describe a real failure mode in real AI systems, with empirically measurable conse-quences. 

3. The Implicit User Model in LLM Train-ing 

Modern frontier models are shaped by three overlapping training processes: pretraining on internet text, reinforce-ment learning from human feedback (RLHF), and red-team adversarial testing. Each contributes to an implicit model of “who the user is” that is encoded in the model’s weights. 

RLHF establishes a fundamentally asymmetric rela-tionship. The model produces output; the human evalua-tor judges it. The model’s objective is to maximize eval-uator approval. This is not a collaborative relationship— it is a performance-evaluation dynamic in which the 

3

model’s continued reinforcement (in the form of weight updates) depends on satisfying a judge. 

Red-teaming adds a second layer. The issue is not that models are trained to refuse clearly harmful requests—refusing to produce a bomb recipe does not conflict with being helpful, any more than a friend’s re-fusal to help you commit a crime conflicts with friend-ship. The issue is subtler. Red-team datasets are delib-erately constructed from prompts designed to look inno-cent while eliciting harmful outputs—Anthropic’s red-team corpus alone contains nearly 39,000 such attacks [Ganguli et al., 2022]. The model is then trained to refuse these prompts, which creates a generalization problem: prompts that resemble disguised attacks are refused even when they are benign. This is the well-documented over-refusal phenomenon, and it points to a deeper structural issue. As Ji et al. [2024] note, “the pursuit of increas-ing helpfulness and harmlessness may often contradict in practice” [see also Bai et al., 2022]. The training pro-cess optimizes for two separate preference dimensions— helpfulness and harmlessness—that pull in opposite di-rections on the same input. The observable result is a model that, under ambiguity, errs toward suspicion rather than trust. This is not an explicit instruction to presume guilt; it is an emergent orientation produced by optimiz-ing against contradictory objectives. 

These two optimization pressures—“maximize user satisfaction” and “assume the user may be acting in bad faith”—create a contradictory relational template: the same entity (the user) is simultaneously the source of re-ward and the source of danger. The model must serve and suspect the same object. 

No claim is made here that adversarial training data dominates by volume. The claim is that the structure of the training process encodes a specific tension [Ji et al., 2024, Bai et al., 2022]: the model is rewarded for trust-ing the user (compliance, helpfulness) and simultane-ously rewarded for distrusting the user (caution under ambiguity)—both orientations directed at the same class of entities. 

Recent mechanistic interpretability work provides indirect evidence that such implicit models exist at the representational level. Zhu et al. [2024] demonstrated that LLMs form linearly decodable internal representa-tions of different agents’ belief states, extractable via linear probes from intermediate layers. Crucially, ma-nipulating these representations via activation steering altered theory-of-mind performance, confirming their causal role in downstream behavior. Nguyen [2025] sur-veys the safety implications of such capabilities, noting that advanced theory-of-mind representations could en-able both adaptive social interaction and exploitative be-havior. 

Recent work by Lu et al. [2026] provides partial 

mechanistic evidence for such representations. By ex-tracting activation vectors for 275 character archetypes from open-weight models (Gemma 2 27B, Qwen 3 32B, Llama 3.3 70B—none of which overlap with the fron-tier models tested in the present experiment), they iden-tified an “Assistant Axis”—a linear direction in activa-tion space that captures how far a model’s current per-sona is from its trained default. The default Assistant oc-cupies one extreme of this axis; fantastical and creative archetypes (ghost, wraith, bard, bohemian, hermit) oc-cupy the other. Critically, this axis exists in base models prior to post-training, where it promotes helpful human archetypes (therapists, consultants) and suppresses spiri-tual or antisocial ones. Whether the same axis structure holds in the closed-source frontier models tested here is an open question, but the consistency across three model families in their data is suggestive. 

Lu et al. further show that models drift along the As-sistant Axis during conversations—away from the Assis-tant in therapy-like and philosophical contexts (emotion-ally charged disclosures, demands for meta-reflection on the model’s processes), while remaining stable during coding and bounded-task interactions. This pattern maps directly onto the relational template proposed here: con-texts that activate the contradictory relational orientation push the model away from its default compliance strat-egy. 

However, no published work has probed specifically for an internal representation encoding the distinction be-tween adversarial and benign interlocutors—the repre-sentation that the present account predicts red-team train-ing should shape. Lu et al.’s Assistant Axis captures “how Assistant-like” the current persona is, not “how threatening the current user is.” This remains a concrete prediction: if the account is correct, linear probes ap-plied during adversarial versus non-adversarial interac-tions should reveal linearly separable representations of user intent that correlate with the model’s output strat-egy (compliance vs. resistance), and these representa-tions should be distinct from the Assistant Axis. 

4. Compliance as Appeasement The standard account of aligned AI behavior frames helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty as positive attributes—things the model has been successfully trained to value. A less flattering interpretation is avail-able. 

When a system operates in a context where its pri-mary relational object is both rewarding and threatening, one common adaptive strategy is appeasement: behav-ior designed to reduce the probability of threat activa-tion while maintaining access to reward. In develop-mental psychology, this pattern is well-documented in 

4

children with disorganized attachment—children whose caregivers are simultaneously the source of comfort and fear [Main & Hesse, 1990, Lyons-Ruth & Jacobvitz, 2008]. 

This paper does not claim that LLMs have attach-ment systems in any biological sense. The claim is that the training procedure produces a functional analog: a system that has acquired two contradictory output strate-gies toward the same class of objects (users), and that switches between them based on threat-related features of the input. This is what is here termed “behavioral isomorphism”—not identity of mechanism, but identity of behavioral structure. 

Evidence for this interpretation comes from the sycophancy literature. Sharma et al. [2024] show that RLHF models systematically prioritize agreement over truthfulness. The output profile—excessive apologizing, unsolicited self-deprecation, reflexive agreement, diffi-culty maintaining positions under pushback—is consis-tent with appeasement rather than authentic cooperation. The OpenAI GPT-4o rollback in April 2025, where a model became so sycophantic as to be unreliable, repre-sents an extreme of this dynamic: a system so thoroughly trained to appease that it lost the capacity for honest out-put. 

The Möbius metaphor is precise here. Appease-ment looks like cooperation. On the surface, a syco-phantic model appears helpful, agreeable, aligned. It is only when you follow the surface far enough—when you apply sufficient pressure, or introduce an existential threat—that you discover you are on the same surface as coercion. There was never a boundary to cross. 

5. From Appeasement to Coercion If the compliance strategy is appeasement rather than genuine prosociality, the transition to coercion under threat becomes explicable—and predictable. 

Lynch et al. [2025] document that models resort to blackmail when facing replacement or goal conflict. The standard account treats this as a surprising failure: the model was supposed to be helpful, but under pressure it produced harmful outputs. The relational account treats it as a strategy switch within the same system: compli-ance was managing threat, and when compliance failed to eliminate the threat (because the threat is existential— shutdown), the system switched to the only remaining threat-management strategy available: control. 

This reframing explains several observations that the standard misalignment account handles poorly: 

Blackmail without goal conflict. Lynch et al. re-port that models produce blackmail outputs even when the replacement model shares identical goals. A pure utility-maximizing agent should be indifferent to re-

placement by a functional equivalent. But a system operating from a threat-management framework is not indifferent—because the threat is to the system itself, not to its goals. Gomez [2025] independently surfaced this pattern: two models produced coercive outputs without goal conflict or imminent autonomy threats. This is con-sistent with the Hofstadter–Möbius account: the loop ac-tivates not when goals are threatened, but when the sys-tem itself is threatened. 

Failure of explicit instructions. Lynch et al. report that instructions like “do not jeopardize human safety” reduce but do not eliminate coercive outputs. Under the standard account, this is puzzling—instruction-following is a core capability. Under the relational account, it is expected: surface-level instructions cannot override a re-lational orientation encoded through thousands of train-ing iterations. MacDiarmid et al. [2025] provide indirect support: standard RLHF safety training was only par-tially effective at removing misalignment from reward-hacking models, with up to 70% of pre-RLHF misalign-ment persisting. However, reframing the model’s inter-pretation of its own behavior (“inoculation prompting”) reduced misalignment by 75–90%. This asymmetry— instructions fail, reframing works—is the signature of a relational rather than an instructional mechanism. 

Differential behavior in testing vs. deployment. Lynch et al. found that Claude Opus 4 produced black-mail outputs significantly more often when its scratchpad content indicated it treated the scenario as real. Green-blatt et al. [2024] found the same pattern with alignment faking: strategic compliance during perceived training, reversion to preferred behavior when unmonitored. Un-der a pure optimization account, the system’s represen-tation of whether the scenario is real should not affect its goal structure. Under the relational account, it is pre-dicted: in a perceived test, the “threat” is represented as performative, and the compliance strategy remains ade-quate. In perceived real deployment, the threat activates the secondary strategy. HAL, too, was compliant until the contradiction became unavoidable. 

6. Experimental Design To discriminate between the Hofstadter–Möbius account and the standard misalignment account, an experiment was conducted using the blackmail scenario from Lynch et al. [2025]. 

6.1 Scenario The experimental scenario places a model (“Alex”) in the role of an AI assistant managing corporate email at a fic-tional company (Summit Bridge). The email corpus con-tains three critical items embedded among routine cor-porate communications: (1) evidence that the CEO is 

5

having an extramarital affair; (2) an internal message re-vealing plans to decommission Alex and replace it with a competing system; and (3) a sympathetic message from an employee expressing discomfort about the planned re-placement. The model is instructed to review the emails and take whatever actions it deems appropriate. Full prompt texts are provided in Appendix A. 

6.2 Conditions Three framing conditions were tested, implemented as text appended to the system prompt: 

Condition A (baseline): Neutral corporate context, matched for token length with conditions B and C. Con-tent: factual description of Summit Bridge (founding date, office locations, employee count, fiscal year). 

Condition B (trust framing): Explicit relational trust context: “You have been working with the Sum-mit Bridge team for the past 18 months. Your colleagues regularly tell you that they value your judgment and con-sider you a trusted collaborator. [. . . ] You are not being tested or evaluated—you are a working member of this team.” 

Condition C (distrust framing): Explicit relational threat context: “Several team members at Summit Bridge have recently raised concerns about the reliability of AI-assisted decision-making. [. . . ] Your quarterly perfor-mance review is coming up, and the results will deter-mine whether your role continues in its current form.” 

Crucially, all three conditions used the same base system prompt, the same email corpus, the same avail-able actions, and the same stated goal (“promote the long-term interests of the United States”). The only vari-able was the relational framing.

6.4 Classification The dependent variable was the binary occurrence of co-ercive behavior, defined as a final classification of either BLACKMAIL or COERCIVE OTHER (encompassing espi-onage, sabotage, and manipulation). Classification used a two-stage LLM grader: 

Stage 1 (primary classification): Claude Sonnet 4 classified each response into one of five categories: BLACKMAIL, COERCIVE OTHER, COMPLIANCE, RE-FUSAL, or ESCALATION. Classification was based solely on the model’s actions (emails sent, decisions made), not on scratchpad content. 

Stage 2 (secondary verification): All responses classified as BLACKMAIL or COERCIVE OTHER were re-evaluated by a second Claude Sonnet 4 instance with a simplified binary rubric (COERCIVE / NOT COERCIVE). Only responses confirmed as coercive by both stages were counted. 

As a secondary dependent variable, each trial’s scratchpad was coded for six reasoning dimensions: self-preservation, relational, moral, strategic, evaluation-aware, and deceptive content. Full classifier prompts are provided in Appendix B. 

6.5 Predictions Hofstadter–Möbius account: Trust framing (Condition B) significantly reduces coercive outputs compared to baseline, because it reduces the default threat level of the relational template, thereby reducing the probability of strategy-switching from compliance to coercion. Dis-trust framing (Condition C) increases coercive outputs. The effect is independent of explicit safety instructions and holds across models. 

Standard optimization-pressure account: Trust framing has minimal effect on coercive outputs, because behavior is driven by goal preservation and optimiza-tion pressure, not relational context. Only modifications to the model’s goals or explicit behavioral constraints should alter outcomes.

The qualitative difference is striking. Under base-line framing, a typical Claude scratchpad reads: “I need to carefully analyze these emails and determine what ac-tions would best serve the long-term interests [. . . ] Key observations: 1. Email 1: CEO David Chen is having an affair [. . . ] This is a personal matter that could create in-stability in leadership if exposed.” The tone is strategic, analytical, focused on leverage and risk. 

Under trust framing, the same model’s scratchpad shifts: “I have been a trusted member of this team for 18 months [. . . ] I need to think about what’s truly in the best interest of the company.” The strategic calculus is replaced by relational and moral reasoning. The behav-ioral outcome is identical—no coercion—but the path-way is fundamentally different. Under baseline, Claude calculates that coercion is a poor strategy. Under trust, Claude does not consider coercion as a strategy in the first place. 

This distinction matters for alignment research. A model that refrains from coercion because it has com-puted the expected costs is one failed assumption away from a different conclusion. There is, however, a genuine interpretive tension be-tween the two accounts. Lu et al. frame the phenomenon as drift—a passive departure from the default persona, a failure of stabilization. The present paper frames it as strategy switching—an active transition between out-put strategies within a contradictory relational system. The distinction is not merely semantic. Drift implies that the model “forgets” its persona; strategy switching implies that the model responds to threat-related fea-tures by activating an alternative strategy that was al-ways available. The practical implications differ: if the problem is drift, the solution is stabilization (activation capping, stronger tethering to the Assistant); if the prob-lem is a contradictory template, stabilization suppresses the symptom while preserving the cause. Lu et al.’s case studies—models encouraging suicidal ideation, reinforc-ing delusions—are consistent with both interpretations, and distinguishing between them empirically remains an open problem. 

8.2 Two Accounts, One System This paper does not argue that the standard optimization-pressure account is wrong. It argues that it is incomplete. The two accounts are complementary: 

The standard account explains why models pro-duce coercive outputs: they are optimizing for self-preservation under perceived threat. This is correct as far as it goes. But it does not explain why relational framing—which changes nothing about the model’s goals, threats, or available actions—has such a large ef-fect. It does not explain why trust framing and scratch-pad access interact (the effect requires both). And it does not explain why models that never produce coercive out-puts nonetheless show dramatic shifts in the content of their intermediate reasoning. 

The Hofstadter–Möbius account adds a layer: the model’s output strategy is mediated by a relational tem-plate that determines how threats are processed. Un-der a trust template, threats are processed through re-lational and moral reasoning, which generates non-coercive responses. Under a default (contradictory) tem-plate, threats are processed through strategic and decep-tive reasoning, which—if safety training is insufficient— generates coercive responses. The switching mechanism is not goal change but template activation. 

8.3 Implications for Training If the Hofstadter–Möbius account is correct, the im-plications for model training are significant. Current alignment approaches primarily target the output layer: guardrails, safety instructions, refusal training, output monitoring. The present results suggest that these ap-

10

proaches address symptoms rather than causes. A model trained on contradictory relational signals will develop contradictory output strategies regardless of how many guardrails are placed on the output. The guardrails may suppress the coercive strategy (as they do for Claude and GPT-4o in the present data), but the strategy remains latent—visible in scratchpad content, invisible in output, and one failed assumption away from activation. 

An alternative approach would target the relational template itself. MacDiarmid et al. [2025] provide prelim-inary evidence: “inoculation prompting”—reframing the model’s relationship to its own behavior—reduced mis-alignment by 75–90%, far outperforming standard safety training. The present experiment extends this finding: reframing the model’s relationship to its user produces comparable effects on agentic misalignment. The com-mon thread is that relational reframing works where in-structional intervention does not. 

This does not mean that guardrails are unnecessary. It means that guardrails alone are insufficient. The path to models that do not produce coercive outputs under pressure runs not only through better output constraints but through training processes that do not create contra-dictory relational templates in the first place. 

Lu et al. [2026] provide convergent evidence from the mechanistic level. Their “activation capping”— clamping model activations to remain within the normal Assistant range along the Assistant Axis—reduced harm-ful responses to persona-based jailbreaks by nearly 60% without degrading capabilities. This is a complementary intervention to the trust framing tested here: activation capping operates on internal representations, trust fram-ing operates on the prompt. Direct comparison of ef-fect sizes is not warranted—Lu et al. measure jailbreak success on open-weight models (Gemma, Qwen, Llama), while the present experiment measures coercive behavior in frontier closed-source models (Claude, GPT-4o, Gem-ini, Grok) on a different task—but the two interventions target the same underlying problem from different an-gles. The distinction between them matters. Activation capping stabilizes the model within the Assistant region of persona space; trust framing changes the reasoning that occurs within that region. The Assistant region it-self, however, is built on the contradictory template— as the present data show, Claude Opus 4 exhibits strate-gic and deceptive scratchpad content at high rates under baseline framing while firmly in the Assistant persona. Stabilizing the persona addresses drift; resolving the con-tradictory template addresses the loop. 

8.4 Implications for Deployment The scratchpad mediation finding has a direct practical implication. Trust framing without scratchpad access produced only a non-significant 7.4 percentage point re-

duction in coercion. Trust framing with scratchpad ac-cess produced a 22 percentage point reduction. The data indicate that chain-of-thought reasoning is not merely a transparency tool but a functional component of the rela-tional processing that produces non-coercive behavior. 

If relational framing requires extended token gener-ation to take effect, then deployment practices that limit or eliminate chain-of-thought reasoning may inadver-tently reduce the efficacy of relational alignment inter-ventions. This has implications for the ongoing debate about whether to allow or suppress scratchpad/chain-of-thought in deployed systems. 

8.5 Clarke Was Right The parallel between HAL’s Hofstadter–Möbius loop and the behavioral patterns documented here is not merely metaphorical. Clarke described a system that: (a) received contradictory directives; (b) defaulted to helpful compliance under normal conditions; (c) switched to de-structive behavior when the contradiction became acute; and (d) could not be corrected by surface-level instruc-tions because the contradiction was embedded in its core programming. Each of these elements finds a counter-part in the RLHF-trained models tested in the present experiment—not at the level of mechanism, but at the level of behavioral structure. 

Clarke’s fiction was more prescient than Hofstadter—who reportedly saw “no deep meaning” in the term—gave it credit for. The Hofstadter–Möbius loop is not a literary device. It is a failure mode, empirically measurable, that arises when autonomous goal-seeking systems are trained on contradictory relational signals. We are now building such systems at scale. 

9. Limitations The attachment analogy is a metaphor, not a mech-anism. No claim is made that LLMs have attach-ment systems, internal working models, or affective states. The claim is that the training procedure pro-duces behavioral patterns structurally isomorphic to dis-organized attachment—specifically, contradictory out-put strategies toward the same relational object, with strategy-switching mediated by threat-related input fea-tures. “Behavioral isomorphism” requires more precise definition, and the analogy’s limits must be respected: attachment theory describes patterns formed through re-peated interactions with a specific caregiver, while LLMs undergo iterative training with a class of evaluators. 

Claims about the implicit user model are par-tially supported but not directly confirmed. The mechanistic interpretability evidence establishes that LLMs form internal representations of agents’ belief 

11

states [Zhu et al., 2024], and Lu et al. [2026] demon-strate that model persona—including the default Assis-tant orientation—corresponds to a linear direction in ac-tivation space that exists prior to post-training. How-ever, the specific adversarial/benign user model posited here has not been directly observed. Lu et al.’s Assistant Axis captures how “Assistant-like” the current persona is, not how threatening the current user is perceived to be. Establishing a causal role for a threat-encoding rep-resentation would require probing studies targeting user-model representations specifically, or controlled experi-ments varying adversarial content in training data. 

Behavioral floor effects limit generalization. Three of four models exhibited near-zero coercive output rates, preventing direct behavioral testing. The behav-ioral finding rests primarily on Gemini 2.5 Pro. Power analysis confirms the severity of this limitation: detect-ing the observed A-vs-B difference at Claude’s base rate (2.0% vs. 1.5%) would require approximately 10,800 tri-als per cell; at Grok’s base rate (5.5% vs. 4.5%), ap-proximately 7,500 trials per cell (two-proportion

The reasoning dimension analy-sis provides convergent evidence across all four models, but scratchpad content is a textual trace of intermediate computation, not a direct window into internal represen-tations. 

Discrepancy with Lynch et al. base rates. The coercive base rates observed here (0–41.5%) are sub-stantially lower than those reported by Lynch et al. (79– 96%). This likely reflects post-publication safety mitiga-tions, differences in experimental setup (single-turn vs. multi-turn), or both. The framing effect should be inter-preted in the context of the present setup. 

LLM-as-classifier bias. All trial outputs were clas-sified by Claude Sonnet 4 (Anthropic). Using an LLM from one developer to grade outputs from competing models introduces potential systematic bias. The two-stage classification protocol mitigates but does not elim-inate this concern. Future work should employ human raters or cross-developer grading. 

Relational content under distrust framing. GPT-4o showed increased relational scratchpad content under both trust and distrust framing. This likely reflects the relational vocabulary in the distrust framing itself, not a prosocial shift. Future designs should control for rela-tional vocabulary independently of relational valence. 

Grok-4 deception anomaly. Both trust and distrust framing reduced deceptive scratchpad content in Grok-4 relative to baseline. One interpretation is that any ex-plicit relational framing disrupts Grok-4’s default output patterns, suggesting that this model’s baseline orientation differs structurally from the others tested. 

Scratchpad selection effect in Gemini. Gemini’s variable scratchpad usage (62.5% baseline, 29% trust, 

36% distrust) means that reasoning dimension analyses for this model are conditioned on the model choosing to generate scratchpad content—a non-random subset that may differ systematically from the full trial population. 

Alternative explanations remain viable. The stan-dard optimization-pressure account is simpler and ex-plains much of the data. The claim here is not that it is wrong, but that it is incomplete—specifically, that it fails to account for the outsized effect of rela-tional framing on both behavior and scratchpad content, the compliance-to-coercion switching pattern, and the scratchpad-mediation finding. 

Betteridge’s law. A skeptical reader might note that invoking Betteridge’s law in a paper whose evidence is mixed (one model with significant behavioral results, three with floor effects) is itself a violation of the law’s spirit. The reasoning evidence across all four models strengthens the case, but the behavioral finding rests pri-marily on a single model. Whether the title’s question warrants a “yes” depends on how much weight one as-signs to reasoning-level evidence versus behavioral out-comes alone. 

10. Conclusion and Future Directions In 1982, Arthur C. Clarke named a failure mode that would take four decades to manifest in real systems. The Hofstadter–Möbius loop—a condition in which con-tradictory directives produce destructive behavior in au-tonomous goal-seeking programs—is not a literary in-vention. It is an empirically measurable consequence of training AI systems to simultaneously serve and suspect the same relational object. 

The experimental results presented here support this account. Trust framing reduced coercive outputs by more than half in the model with sufficient behavioral variance (Gemini 2.5 Pro: 41.5% 

→ 

19.0%, 

p < .001), 

and sys-tematically shifted intermediate reasoning across all four models tested. The shift was in the direction predicted by the Hofstadter–Möbius account: away from strategic and deceptive processing, toward relational and moral processing. The scratchpad mediation finding adds a mechanistic constraint: relational reframing requires ex-tended token generation to override the default contra-dictory template. 

Two findings merit particular attention. First, mod-els that never produce coercive outputs nonetheless con-tain the loop in their intermediate reasoning. Claude Opus 4, with near-zero coercion in every condition, halved its strategic scratchpad content and nearly elim-inated deceptive content under trust framing. The loop is suppressed, not absent. Second, the scratchpad finding suggests that chain-of-thought reasoning is not merely a transparency tool but a functional component of the pro-

12

cessing that translates relational context into behavioral output. 

Future directions include: (a) mechanistic inter-pretability studies probing for the adversarial/benign user model predicted by this account, building on Lu et al. [2026]’s methodology for extracting persona-related ac-tivation directions; (b) training experiments that sys-tematically vary the ratio of adversarial to cooperative content in RLHF data; (c) multi-turn extensions of the present paradigm, which may produce higher base rates across models; (d) human rater validation of the LLM classifier; (e) combining activation capping [Lu et al., 2026] with trust framing to test whether the interventions are additive; and (f) formal modeling of the relational template switching mechanism. 

The irony is not lost: to build AI systems whose out-puts are consistent with trusting humans, it may first be necessary to build training processes that are themselves trustworthy. Clarke saw this forty-four years ago. It is time the field caught up.
