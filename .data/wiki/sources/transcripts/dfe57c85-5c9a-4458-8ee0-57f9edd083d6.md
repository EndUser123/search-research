For years, I have helped leaders 

modernize complex platforms

http://linkedin.com/in/faisalferoz/

 and move away from brittle, monolithic systems. One of the hardest parts of that job is not the initial design, but the “drift” that happens after the architect leaves the room. I've seen countless architecture documents go stale within months of being written. PowerPoint decks gathering digital dust. Confluence pages that nobody updates. Those beautiful C4 diagrams that stop reflecting reality the moment a critical decision gets made in a Slack thread at 11 PM.

I thought Architecture Decision Records would fix this. ADRs promised something better: lightweight markdown files living alongside code, capturing the “why” behind our choices. And they did help. For human teams, anyway.

But something fundamental has shifted in the last year. The most important architecture documentation you can write today isn't primarily for humans anymore. It's for AI agents. And if you're still thinking about architecture docs the old way, you're about to get left behind.

The architecture decisions that matter most now are the ones that tell Claude, Codex, and other coding agents how to behave in your codebase. Not what you decided last quarter, but what rules they must follow right now. Not historical context for new hires, but executable constraints for autonomous systems generating thousands of lines of code while you sleep.

I'm talking about AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md, and what some teams are calling “steering rules.” These aren't just fancy prompt files. They're becoming the control plane for how AI systems operate across your entire platform. And they're more valuable than traditional ADRs ever were.

Why AI-facing documentation matters more than you think

Traditional ADRs answer: 

“What did we decide, and why?”

AGENTS.md answers: 

“Given what we decided, what must never happen, and what must always happen, when changing this code?”

That shift matters because agentic workflows multiply change velocity.

When OpenAI launched Codex in May 2025, they mentioned something that should have gotten more attention. Codex can follow project rules from an AGENTS.md file and pull from GitHub repositories to stay aligned with team practices. This wasn't buried in release notes. This was the feature.

Think about what this means. You can now encode your senior engineer instincts directly into a file that every AI agent reads before touching your code. Want to enforce that all API responses must include tracing headers? Put it in AGENTS.md. Need to ensure database migrations follow a specific rollback pattern? Specify it once. Concerned about PII leaking into logs? Make it a rule.

AGENTS.md has become a well-established pattern, and the simplicity is exactly why it works. There's no complex protocol to implement. No new tools to install. Just markdown files containing instructions that LLMs are remarkably good at following.

From my work at Etilize leading the transition to AI-first architectures, I've seen firsthand how this changes everything. We've spent years building event-driven systems on Kafka, creating clear boundaries between services, establishing patterns for error handling and observability. All of that institutional knowledge used to live in people's heads and scattered documentation. Now it can live in a single file that every AI coding session consults before making a change.

Constraints matter more than prompts

Here's where most people get this wrong. The entire industry is obsessing over prompt engineering. How do I phrase this request to get better code? What's the magic incantation for this specific task? Should I use few-shot examples?

That's missing the point entirely.

The durable value isn't in how you prompt today. It's in the constraints you institutionalize that survive both new hires and new models. When GPT-6 or Claude Opus 6 launches next year with completely different characteristics, your carefully crafted prompts will need to be rewritten. But your architectural rules? Those should remain stable.

This is why AGENTS.md is more valuable than a thousand prompt engineering blog posts. You're not trying to coax the model into doing something clever this one time. You're encoding invariants that must hold every time, regardless of which model is running or who's doing the prompting.

Let me make this concrete. In a traditional ADR, you might write: “We decided to use PostgreSQL for the analytics service because we need complex queries and transactional consistency.” That's useful historical context. But it doesn't prevent someone (or something) from inadvertently introducing a second database.

In an AGENTS.md file, you write: “All data access must go through the existing PostgreSQL connection pool. Never introduce new database dependencies without explicit approval. When writing queries, always use parameterized statements to prevent SQL injection.”

See the difference? One explains what was decided in the past. The other specifies what must be true in the future. One is passive documentation. The other is active governance.

What belongs in AGENTS.md

After working through several 

large-scale modernizations

https://www.linkedin.com/in/faisalferoz/details/projects/

 and seeing how 

AI coding tools operate in production

https://medium.com/@fferoz/how-ai-really-thinks-and-why-it-feels-so-human-1b0d4a340c3a

, I've developed a clear sense of what matters. Your AGENTS.md should be opinionated and specific about:

Architecture boundaries and layering rules.

If you've spent years establishing clean hexagonal architecture or ensuring that domain logic never leaks into API handlers, encode those boundaries explicitly. Tell the agent which modules can depend on which others. Specify what absolutely cannot cross certain lines.

Data handling and privacy requirements.

This is critical and often overlooked. If your platform processes sensitive information, you need explicit rules about where that data can flow, how it must be logged, and what absolutely cannot happen. We've built systems handling 

product data across 29 languages

https://www.linkedin.com/in/faisalferoz/details/projects/

 and multiple regulatory environments. Those constraints aren't suggestions, they're requirements that must be mechanically enforced.

Error handling patterns.

Every system has failure modes. Document how errors should propagate, what gets logged where, how retries should work, and what constitutes an acceptable recovery strategy. When an AI agent generates error-handling code at 2 AM, you want it to follow your established patterns, not invent creative new ways to fail silently.

Testing expectations and quality bars.

Specify what tests are required for what kinds of changes. If you expect unit tests to achieve certain coverage thresholds, integration tests for external dependencies, or property-based tests for complex logic, make that explicit. The agent won't know your quality standards unless you tell them.

Performance and scaling considerations.

If you know that N+1 queries caused an outage last year, document that pattern and forbid it. If certain operations must be batched or cached, or rate-limited, say so upfront. The agent doesn't have institutional memory of your performance incidents.

Security guardrails and compliance requirements.

From my experience with mission-critical financial systems at Primatics Financial and event-driven architectures processing millions of transactions, I can tell you that security isn't something you bolt on later. Specify authentication patterns, authorization checks, input validation requirements, and secure coding practices. Make OWASP Top 10 violations explicitly forbidden.

The key is being specific enough to be useful but general enough to apply across many situations. You're not writing a 200-page specification. You're encoding the wisdom that a senior engineer would share during code review, just in a format that an AI can consume before writing the code, instead of after.

Here is a practical checklist. Notice how each item is a constraint you would normally carry in your head during review.

1) Layering and dependency rules (software architecture)

UI can call Application services, not Infrastructure directly.

The domain cannot import from adapters.

No cross-module imports except through public interfaces.

2) Data boundaries (data architecture, privacy, compliance)

PII fields are only handled in service X.

No customer data in logs.

All writes must go through the outbox publisher; never direct event writes.

3) Error handling and resilience (reliability engineering)

All message handlers must be idempotent.

Retries must be bounded and use backoff.

Never swallow errors, return typed failures.

4) Observability (logging, tracing, metrics)

Every request logs a correlation ID.

Structured logging only.

Metrics names and required tags.

5) Test expectations (quality gates)

PRs must include unit tests for business rules and integration tests for API boundaries.

Run these commands locally before finishing: lint, test, contract tests.

6) Security guardrails (DevSecOps)

No new network egress without an allowlist update.

Secrets must be passed via vault, never env files committed.

A short example you could drop into a repo today:

# AGENTS.md
## Non-negotiable architecture rules
- Do not bypass the Application layer. Controllers call Application services only.
- Do not access the database from API handlers. Use repositories in the Infrastructure layer.
- All cross-service communication is event-driven. Publish domain events through the outbox.
## Data boundaries and privacy
- Treat customer identifiers and contact fields as PII. Never log them.
- Any export/report must use the approved anonymization utility.
## Error handling
- Handlers must be idempotent. Use requestId / messageId de-duplication.
- Retries must be bounded. Use exponential backoff and dead-letter after N attempts.
## Observability
- Structured logs only. Include correlationId, tenantId (if present), and operation name.
- Emit metrics for latency and failure counts on every external call.
## Definition of done
- Run: make lint && make test && make integration-test
- Update or add tests for every behavior change. No exceptions.


This looks small, but it is powerful because it is used at the moment decisions are made, by humans and by AI agents.

How this changes your role as an Architect

If you're a technical leader, CTO, or Chief Architect, this shift has profound implications for how you scale your impact. I've spent years mentoring senior engineers and leading architecture governance across distributed teams. The traditional model was: the architect makes decisions, writes them down, hopes people read them, and catches problems in code review.

That model is breaking down. Not because people are careless, but because the volume of code being generated has exploded. When a single engineer with AI assistance can output 10x more code than before, reviewing it all becomes impossible. The old scaling factors don't work.

AGENTS.md flips this around. Instead of reviewing after the fact, you're providing guidance before the code gets written. Your architectural instincts get distributed to every AI-assisted coding session across your entire organization. A junior engineer using Claude Code at 2 AM inherits your 20+ years of experience through the rules you've encoded.

This is how you scale senior engineering judgment across a larger AI-augmented team. Not by hiring more architects to review more PRs, but by institutionalizing the constraints that matter into files that AI agents must respect.

Practical patterns that work

Based on what's emerging in the field and what I've seen work in production systems, here are some patterns worth adopting:

Start with your most painful repeated mistakes. Every codebase has them. That antipattern that keeps sneaking back in. The subtle bug that's bitten you three times. The architectural boundary that developers keep crossing without realizing why it matters. Document those explicitly in AGENTS.md with context about why they're forbidden.

Layer your rules hierarchically. Have a root AGENTS.md at the repo level with system-wide invariants. Then let individual services or modules extend those with their own specific constraints. This mirrors how you'd structure any policy system, but now it's for AI agents instead of just humans.

Make rules testable where possible. If you say “all API handlers must include authentication checks,” provide a linting rule or architecture test that verifies this. AI agents can both follow the rule when writing code and run the tests to confirm compliance. Codex runs in a cloud-based, isolated environment and rejects malicious code, but you still want your own validation.

Keep it maintained alongside architecture changes. This is where AGENTS.md differs from traditional ADRs. An ADR is often immutable after approval, capturing a point-in-time decision. AGENTS.md is a living document that should evolve as your architecture evolves. When you refactor a major subsystem, update the rules to reflect the new reality.

Use it for onboarding, not just AI. I've found that the same file that guides AI agents is incredibly valuable for new human team members. It's a forcing function to make implicit knowledge explicit. If you can't explain an architectural constraint clearly enough for an AI to follow it, you probably haven't explained it clearly enough for humans either.

The ecosystem is just beginning

The tools are still early but moving fast. Anthropic built the concept of “skills” into Claude Code where agents automatically load relevant instructions based on the task at hand. Skills are markdown with a tiny bit of YAML metadata and some optional scripts, making them trivially easy to create and share.

Kiro introduced steering rules as a core feature of their agentic IDE. Their model is built around spec-driven development where agents work from explicit requirements and design documents rather than just vibing on prompts. That's the right direction, formalizing what the AI needs to know before it starts generating code.

GitHub is exploring similar concepts. OpenAI is iterating on how Codex interprets project structure and rules. We're watching a rapid convergence toward the same basic insight: AI coding agents need explicit constraints to be safe and useful at scale.

There's nothing preventing these patterns from being used with other models. Point any capable coding agent at a well-structured AGENTS.md file, and it will adapt. This isn't vendor lock-in. It's an emerging standard that crosses model boundaries.

A different kind of architecture practice

I've been doing architecture work since the early 2000s. I've designed systems in just about every pattern you can name. Monoliths, microservices, event-driven, serverless, you name it. The constant has always been: the hard part isn't the initial design, it's keeping that design coherent as the system evolves and the team scales.

Traditional architecture documentation tried to solve this through better writing, clearer diagrams, more accessible wikis. And those all helped at the margin. But they were still passive artifacts. You had to remember they existed. You had to keep them updated. You had to actively choose to read them before making changes.

AGENTS.md and its variants are different because they're active. They're in the loop. Every AI-assisted coding session consults them. Every agent-generated pull request reflects them. They're not documentation you might read; they're rules that get enforced.

This is a fundamental shift in how architecture gets practiced. Not just documenting decisions after the fact, but encoding constraints that shape every future decision. Not just explaining what we did and why, but specifying what must remain true regardless of who or what is writing the code.

If you're a technical leader responsible for the long-term health of a platform, you should be thinking about this now. Because the teams and companies that figure this out early will be able to move significantly faster while maintaining significantly higher quality. They'll scale AI assistance safely. They'll onboard faster. They'll have fewer incidents caused by violated architectural assumptions.

The architecture documentation that matters most in 2026 isn't the document explaining last year's decision to use Kafka instead of RabbitMQ or JMS. It's the file that tells every AI agent in your organization how to work with Kafka correctly, what patterns to follow, what mistakes to avoid and what invariants must hold.

Scaling “senior engineer instincts” across an AI-augmented team

If you are leading an organization, the pitch is not “we wrote a nicer markdown file.”

The pitch is: 

you can scale judgment.

AI coding agents can increase throughput, but they also increase variance. You can ship faster and still drift into architecture entropy: leaky boundaries, inconsistent logging, accidental data exposure, and silent reliability regressions.

AGENTS.md and its cousins are one of the few levers that scale well:

They onboard new humans faster.

They keep agentic workflows aligned with architecture.

They make governance concrete, auditable, and repeatable.

Or in simpler terms: they turn “how our best engineers think” into defaults that everyone, including tools, can follow.

Start writing your AGENTS.md. If you want a starting point, keep it boring. Make it specific. Make it opinionated. Make it the kind of guidance you wish you could give in real-time to every engineer on your team. Because now, effectively, you can. Aim for 30 to 60 lines that cover: boundaries, data rules, error handling, logging, and tests. Then evolve it the same way you evolve a production system: based on real failures, real learnings, and real constraints.

The conversation is just beginning, but the direction is clear. In the age of AI-assisted development, your most valuable architecture artifact isn't a historical record of decisions made. It's an executable specification of constraints that must hold. Not what we decided, but what must be true.

That's the document worth maintaining. That's where the durable value lives. And that's what separates teams that use AI effectively from teams that just generate a lot of code quickly.
