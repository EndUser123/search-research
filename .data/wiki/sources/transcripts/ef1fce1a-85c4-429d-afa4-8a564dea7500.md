In my 

previous articles

https://achan2013.medium.com/list/aifirst-enterprise-automation-with-genai-and-ai-agents-05cebb6eae13

, I walked through how to design multi‑agent solutions with watsonx Orchestrate (wxO) and how to apply agentic patterns such as linear flows, supervisor models, and crowdsourcing. Those patterns form the foundation for building intelligent, tool‑driven systems — but as adoption grows, a different set of challenges starts to emerge.

This article continues that discussion by focusing on something every team inevitably runs into: 

anti‑patterns

. These are design choices that feel reasonable at first but cause agents to behave unpredictably, lose state, violate compliance rules, or simply fail to scale in enterprise environments.

The objective of this article is twofold:

To highlight the foundational anti‑patterns that cause agents to fail long before the model is ever at fault.

To provide practical architectural alternatives you can use in your agentic solution when reliability, determinism, or regulatory constraints matter.

By the end, you should have a clear understanding of 

why

 these patterns fail, 

how

 they surface during real workloads, and 

when

 to shift responsibility from an agent to a workflow, a tool, or an explicit state object.

This article covers six foundational anti‑patterns:

The Monolithic Mega‑Prompt

 — overloading a single agent with hundreds of instructions it cannot reliably follow.

The Agent‑as‑Business‑Process Fallacy

 — replacing a controlled, auditable process graph with an agent that approximates rather than executes.

Invisible State

 — relying on the LLM to remember what happened instead of managing state explicitly.

All‑or‑Nothing Autonomy

 — giving agents either too much freedom to act or too little, with no calibrated middle ground.

Passing “As‑Is” Information Through the Model

 — letting the agent rewrite content that must be delivered verbatim.

Chasing the Latest Research Paper

 — reaching for exotic agent topologies before exhausting the proven patterns that solve most real problems.

❌ Anti‑Pattern #1: The Monolithic Mega‑Prompt

(“Just give the agent 500 lines of instructions — it’ll figure it out.”)

In one real deployment, a user handed an agent close to 

500 lines of procedural instructions

 and expected the agent to follow them exactly, in order, every single run. When the agent skipped a step or merged two steps together, the immediate conclusion was:

“The model isn’t capable enough.”

But the issue wasn’t the model. 
 The issue was the assumption that a reasoning engine behaves like a workflow engine.

No LLM — closed source or open source — can reliably execute hundreds of deterministic steps from memory. Not because the model is weak, but because LLMs compress context, reinterpret instructions, and optimize for intent rather than strict execution. As instructions grow, so does the chance that the model misinterprets or reprioritizes individual steps.

This becomes especially problematic in 

regulated industries

, where every step must follow a predefined sequence, and deviating from that sequence isn’t merely an error — it’s a compliance issue.

Why the pattern fails

Long instructions dilute the model’s attention.

Steps toward the end of the list rely on lossy memory.

The LLM cannot reliably track internal state across 300+ operations.

The agent becomes nondeterministic, even when the process isn’t.

A better architecture

When a single agent is overloaded with too many responsibilities, the solution is decomposition. There are two complementary approaches:

Split into a multi‑agent system with a Supervisor

 Break the monolithic agent into a set of focused sub‑agents, each responsible for a well‑scoped task. A supervisor agent handles routing, intent resolution, and orchestration — delegating to the right sub‑agent at the right time. Each sub‑agent carries a small, targeted instruction set that stays well within reliable attention range.

Offload deterministic steps to a workflow layer

 When a process must be executed 

exactly the same way

 every time, shift those steps out of the agent entirely and into a 

deterministic agentic workflow

. The agent handles interpretation and routing; the workflow handles the known, repeatable sequence. If you already know the exact steps, don’t leave them to chance — encode them where determinism is guaranteed.

Both approaches reduce the cognitive load on any single agent and replace implicit, attention-dependent execution with explicit, verifiable structure.

Evidence:

 Liu et al. (2023) demonstrated the 

“Lost in the Middle”

 effect — LLM accuracy at retrieving information from position 10 of a 20-document context dropped to ~55%, compared to ~80% at position 1. A 500-line instruction set places most steps squarely in this degraded zone. (

arXiv:2307.03172

)

Advances in LLM architecture — larger context windows, improved attention mechanisms — do reduce some of these effects. But larger context does not mean perfect recall or perfect instruction compliance. The 

Berkeley Function-Calling Leaderboard

 (Yan et al., ongoing), which tracks real-world tool-use accuracy across frontier models, consistently shows that even the best-performing models fall short of 100% accuracy on complex, multi-step function-calling tasks. In a 500-step process where each step may involve a tool call, even a 95% per-step accuracy compounds to low percentages end-to-end success. The architecture problem doesn’t disappear with a bigger context window — it just becomes less visible until it fails. (

gorilla.cs.berkeley.edu/leaderboard

)

❌ Anti‑Pattern #2: The Agent‑as‑Business‑Process Fallacy

(Assuming an agent can replace a structured business process)

A business process is not a flat list of steps. It is a 

controlled graph

: decisions, branches, loops, exception paths, approval gates, and rollback conditions — all of which must execute predictably, be auditable, and produce the same outcome given the same inputs.

The anti‑pattern here is replacing that controlled graph with an agent and assuming the agent will honor the same guarantees. The assumption sounds reasonable:

“The agent is smart enough to figure out the right path.”

But that is precisely the problem. An agent 

figures things out

 — it reasons, reinterprets, and adapts. A business process must not. It must follow the defined path, log every transition, and fail loudly when a required condition is not met.

In practice, agents used as business process replacements exhibit a specific failure mode: they 

approximate the process rather than execute it

. They take the most plausible path through the graph, not the required one. Under normal conditions this looks correct. Under edge cases — the exact situations where process compliance matters most — it breaks.

Why the pattern fails

Business processes encode decisions, branches, and exception paths that must be traversed exactly — not approximated.

Agents have no native concept of a process state machine: there are no enforced transitions, no rollback on failure, no guaranteed audit trail.

The model’s dynamic replanning — which is a strength in open‑ended tasks — becomes a liability when the correct path is already defined and must not deviate.

Compliance requirements in regulated industries (finance, healthcare, insurance) often mandate that every step be logged, every decision be traceable, and every exception be handled explicitly. An agent cannot provide these guarantees by design.

A better architecture

The solution here is distinct from Anti‑Pattern #1. The problem isn’t just that the agent has too many instructions — it’s that the 

wrong tool

 is being used for the job entirely.

A business process requires a 

workflow engine

, not an agent. Workflow engines maintain explicit state machines with enforced transitions: every branch condition is evaluated deterministically, every exception path is handled explicitly, every step is logged with a timestamp and an outcome. These are not features you can add to an agent — they are the foundational design of a process engine.

The right separation is:

Agents

 handle the open‑ended parts: interpreting user intent, resolving ambiguity, generating recommendations, or handling a reasoning step within a larger process.

Workflow engines

 handle the controlled graph: decisions, branches, loops, approvals, rollback, and audit trails.

Dedicated platforms — such as 

IBM Business Automation Workflow (BAW)

 or 

watsonx Orchestrate Agentic Workflow

 — are built for exactly this. An agent can be a 

participant

 in a workflow (called at a specific node to handle a reasoning task), but it should not 

be

 the workflow.

Evidence:

 The 

IFEval benchmark

 (Zhou et al., 2023) measures strict, verifiable instruction-following across prompts that contain 

multiple simultaneous constraints

 — format requirements, length limits, keyword rules, structural conditions. Even frontier models achieve only 70–80% accuracy at the prompt level (all constraints in a prompt satisfied), and this figure degrades as the number of constraints per prompt increases. A business process is not a single constraint — it is a graph of many: branch conditions, exception paths, approval requirements, mandatory fields. The 

FollowBench benchmark

 (Jiang et al., 2023) makes the degradation curve explicit: instruction-following accuracy drops steadily as constraint count grows, falling well below 50% for prompts with five or more simultaneous constraints. In a regulated business process, every node in that graph must be honored — not most of them, not the easy ones. The research confirms that LLMs are not built for this guarantee. (

IFEval — arXiv:2311.07911

; 

FollowBench — arXiv:2310.20410

)

❌ Anti‑Pattern #3: Invisible State

(Relying on the LLM to “just remember” what happened earlier)

As agent workflows grow, state becomes the glue that holds the system together. Unfortunately, many designs still assume that the LLM will simply track all past actions because they appear somewhere in the conversation history.

In practice, this results in:

repeated steps

contradicting actions

collapsing context windows

hallucinated states

lost commitments and forgotten constraints

LLMs do not maintain structured state. They approximate it. And approximation creates drift.

Why the pattern fails

The model creates a compressed mental map that loses detail.

Long threads cause older context to be dropped.

Tool outputs get summarized and altered.

State becomes increasingly distorted as more steps occur.

A better architecture

Use 

explicit state objects

, not implicit assumptions:

State is stored and passed intentionally

Every step reads and updates structured fields

Agents never have to infer what has happened — they know

This is similar to how I explained agentic pattern: shared state, predictable transitions, transparent execution.

Evidence:

 Greg Kamradt’s public “Needle in a Haystack” benchmark showed that even large-context models experience significant recall degradation as context grows — state buried deep in a long conversation thread is effectively unreliable. The rapid adoption of stateful agent frameworks (LangGraph, Semantic Kernel, Bee Agent Framework) is a direct industry response to this exact problem. (

github.com/gkamradt/LLMTest_NeedleInAHaystack

)

❌ Anti‑Pattern #4: All‑or‑Nothing Autonomy

(The agent either does everything on its own… or nothing at all)

I see two extremes in real deployments:

Too much autonomy

The agent calls tools repeatedly, runs up cost, or executes harmful sequences because it believes it has the authority to act.

Too little autonomy

The agent asks the user to confirm every decision, turning into a slow, high‑latency chatbot with unnecessary friction.

Both extremes lead to poor user experiences.

A better architecture

Autonomy should be intentional, adjustable, and risk‑weighted:

Action budgets

Approval gates

Delegation thresholds

Low/medium/high autonomy modes depending on user and context

The model should know 

when

 it’s allowed to act versus when it needs oversight.

Evidence:

 The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, 2023) identifies 

controllability

 and 

human oversight

 as explicit trustworthiness dimensions for AI systems — stating that AI systems should support the ability of humans to “oversee, adjust, retrain, or shut down” automated decision-making. Deploying an agent with no autonomy controls violates this principle by design. (

NIST AI 100–1, nvlpubs.nist.gov

)

❌ Anti‑Pattern #5: Passing “As‑Is” Information Through the Model

(Letting the agent rewrite content that must be delivered verbatim)

In regulated environments, there are blocks of text that must be delivered 

exactly as written

:

legal statements

disclosures

regulatory notices

standardized responses

When this content passes through an LLM, even with strict instructions, the model may:

paraphrase

condense

reformat

alter meaning unintentionally

This is not an LLM issue — it’s a design issue. 
 Models 

rewrite

. That’s their job.

A better architecture

The goal is to route exact content 

around

 the LLM, not through it. There are three practical ways to do this:

1. Tools that deliver content directly

 A tool can return exact content in a document (e.g., a generated PDF) or push it directly to the user through a custom channel — email, SMS, a regulated portal — without the output ever passing through the model’s generation step. The agent calls the tool; the tool owns the delivery.

2. Workflow blocks with HITL handling

 In a workflow layer, a Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) step can surface exact content directly to the user as part of the process — bypassing the agent entirely for that step. The workflow renders the content; the agent is not involved in its presentation.

3. MCP audience annotation

 The Model Context Protocol supports an  audience  annotation on tool responses, signaling that the returned content is intended for the end user rather than for the model to process. When an agent respects this annotation, it passes the content through without regenerating it. The important caveat: this only works if the agent calling the MCP tool is built to honor the annotation — it is a convention, not an enforcement mechanism.

In all three cases, the principle is the same: let the agent 

reference

 the content, not regenerate it.

Evidence:

 The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, 2023) explicitly calls out reliability and accuracy of AI outputs as a trustworthiness dimension, noting that AI systems in high-stakes domains must produce consistent, verifiable results — a standard that LLM-regenerated verbatim content cannot reliably meet. (

doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.100–1

)

❌ Anti‑Pattern #6: Chasing the Latest Research Paper Instead of Fixing the Architecture

(“We need Swarm / CUGA / CodeAct — that’s why our agent isn’t working.”)

There is a recurring pattern in enterprise AI teams that I call 

research‑paper chasing

: the moment an agent misbehaves, the instinct is to reach for the newest, most exotic implementation approach described in a recent arXiv preprint or conference paper — rather than asking whether the fundamentals are in place.

The conversation usually sounds like this:

“Our agent keeps losing context. Should we try a Swarm topology?” “The outputs aren’t consistent. What if we add an LLM‑as‑Judge layer?” “Maybe we need agents that critique each other’s outputs.”

These are real approaches. Some of them are genuinely interesting. But they are 

advanced solutions to specific, well‑understood problems

 — not general fixes for agents that are failing because of the anti‑patterns described in this article.

The exotic approaches being chased

A pattern I encounter regularly is being asked whether a platform supports a particular agent topology — Swarm, CodeAct, CUGA, agents critiquing each other — without any concrete use case driving the question. The approach itself becomes the goal, rather than the problem it was designed to solve. Here are some of the approaches that come up most often in those conversations:

Swarm

 (OpenAI, 2024) — a lightweight multi‑agent handoff model where agents transfer control to one another based on context. Useful for routing, but adds coordination overhead that amplifies invisible state problems if the underlying state management is already broken. (

github.com/openai/swarm

https://github.com/openai/swarm

)

CUGA — Computer Use Generalist Agent

 — an agent framework that interacts with computers through GUI actions (clicking, typing, scrolling) rather than structured APIs. Powerful for automating desktop workflows, but introduces significant reliability and security challenges that most teams haven’t addressed. (

arXiv:2503.01861

https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01861

; 

cuga.dev

https://cuga.dev/

)

CodeAct

 (Wang et al., 2024) — agents that emit executable Python code as their action space rather than structured tool calls. Dramatically increases flexibility, but also dramatically increases the blast radius of a hallucination. (

arXiv:2402.01030

https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01030

)

LLM‑as‑Judge

 (Zheng et al., 2023) — using a second LLM to evaluate the output of the first. Adds a useful quality gate, but introduces a second model’s biases, doubles latency, and doubles cost — none of which helps if the root cause is a poorly scoped task. (

arXiv:2306.05685

https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685

)

Agents critiquing each other

 — debate and adversarial review patterns where multiple agents challenge each other’s outputs to surface errors (Du et al., 2023). Valuable in research settings; in production it multiplies token consumption and can produce circular disagreements with no resolution mechanism. (

arXiv:2305.14325

https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325

)

Why the pattern fails

The core problem is 

misdiagnosis

. Teams reach for architectural novelty when the actual failure is one of the basics:

The task is too broad for a single agent (→ Anti‑Pattern #1)

The agent is being used as a business process engine (→ Anti‑Pattern #2: Agent‑as‑Business‑Process)

State is implicit and drifting (→ Anti‑Pattern #3)

Autonomy is unconstrained (→ Anti‑Pattern #4)

Exact content is being regenerated (→ Anti‑Pattern #5)

Adding a Swarm topology on top of invisible state doesn’t fix invisible state — it distributes it across more agents. Adding an LLM‑as‑Judge layer on top of a mega‑prompt doesn’t fix the mega‑prompt — it adds a second model that will also struggle with the same attention degradation.

Exotic patterns also carry a hidden cost: 

they are harder to debug, harder to explain to stakeholders, and harder to maintain

. When something goes wrong in a multi‑agent debate loop or a CodeAct execution chain, the failure mode is nearly impossible to reproduce or audit.

A better architecture

Before reaching for a novel approach, work through this checklist:

Have you tried a single, well‑scoped prompt?

 A Single Prompt agent with a clear task boundary and a small, focused tool set solves the majority of real enterprise use cases.

Have you tried ReAct?

 Reason → Act → Observe is a proven loop that handles most tool‑using scenarios without additional complexity.

Have you tried Chain‑of‑Thought (CoT)?

 Structured reasoning steps dramatically improve output quality before any multi‑agent topology is needed.

Have you tried a Supervisor pattern?

 A single orchestrator delegating to specialized sub‑agents covers most multi‑step, multi‑domain workflows.

Only after these patterns have been genuinely exhausted — not just tried once in a demo — should you consider whether a more exotic approach is warranted. And when you do, the decision should be driven by a specific, diagnosed failure mode, not by the publication date of a paper.

Evidence:

 A 2024 analysis by Anthropic researchers found that the majority of agent failures in production stem from 

task scoping, context management, and tool design

 — not from the choice of agent topology. Similarly, the original ReAct paper (Yao et al., 2022) demonstrated that a simple Reason‑Act loop outperformed far more complex approaches on a wide range of benchmarks, precisely because it kept the reasoning process transparent and correctable. (

arXiv:2210.03629

)

Closing Thoughts

As with my article on the 

swarm pattern

https://achan2013.medium.com/c412b0668f7b?sk=c4ccc95522f81bb81ba62773ebe1ebcb

, the lesson is that agents succeed not because the prompts are perfect, but because the 

architecture supports the behavior you need

. When foundational anti‑patterns creep in — mega‑prompts, agent-as-business-process, invisible state, uncontrolled autonomy, or rewriting exact content — the system becomes fragile and unpredictable.

Three broader patterns are worth keeping in mind as you evaluate your own designs:

The failure rate is higher than most teams expect.

 A 2025 MIT Media Lab / Project NANDA report cited in Harvard Business Review found that 

95% of investments in generative AI have produced zero measurable returns

. The root cause isn’t the models — it’s the pattern of running isolated experiments without the architectural foundations needed to move from pilot to production. The six anti‑patterns in this article are among the primary structural reasons that gap exists. (

HBR, “Beware the AI Experimentation Trap,” August 2025

https://hbr.org/2025/08/beware-the-ai-experimentation-trap

)

The “it worked in the demo” problem is real.

 Most of these anti‑patterns are invisible at small scale. A mega‑prompt with 20 steps may work fine in a controlled test. It’s at 200 steps, 1,000 concurrent users, or after 6 months in production that the architecture debt surfaces — often suddenly, and at the worst possible time.

Anti‑patterns compound cost.

 Invisible state causes repeated tool calls. Mega‑prompts consume tokens on every invocation. Treating an agent as a business process may appear to work at small scale, but in regulated environments it can produce non-compliant outcomes — missed audit trails, skipped mandatory steps, or unverifiable execution paths — precisely when compliance matters most. In a high‑volume enterprise deployment, these inefficiencies don’t just slow the system down — they translate directly into infrastructure spend and regulatory risk that erodes the business case for the agent entirely.

In future articles, we’ll cover 

Tools anti‑patterns

 — the mistakes teams make when designing, scoping, and wiring tools to agents — and 

Knowledge anti‑patterns

 — the ways retrieval, grounding, and memory go wrong in production. These are where many architecturally sound agents still break down, and they deserve their own focused treatment.
