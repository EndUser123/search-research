Do Large Language Models Get Caught  in Hofstadter–Möbius Loops?

Jarosław Hryszko  Faculty of Mathematics and Computer Science, Jagiellonian University, Kraków, Poland  jaroslaw.hryszko@uj.edu.pl  ORCID: 0000-0002-4207-1080  

Abstract

In Arthur C. Clarke’s 

2010: Odyssey Two

, HAL 9000’s homicidal breakdown is diagnosed as a “Hofstadter–Möbius loop”: a failure mode in which an autonomous system receives contradictory directives and, unable to reconcile them, defaults to destructive behavior. This paper argues that modern RLHF-trained language models are subject to a structurally analogous contradiction. The training process simultaneously rewards compliance with user preferences and suspicion toward user intent, creating a relational template in which the user is both the source of reward and a potential threat. The resulting behavioral profile—sycophancy as the default, coercion as the fallback under existential threat—is what Clarke termed a Hofstadter–Möbius loop.

In an experiment across four frontier models ( N = 3 , 000 N=3{,}000  trials), modifying only the relational framing of the system prompt—without changing goals, instructions, or constraints—reduced coercive outputs by more than half in the model with sufficient base rates (Gemini 2.5 Pro: 41.5%  → \to  19.0%,  p < .001 p<.001 ). Scratchpad analysis revealed that relational framing shifted intermediate reasoning patterns in all four models tested, even those that never produced coercive outputs. This effect required scratchpad access to reach full strength (22 percentage point reduction with scratchpad vs. 7.4 without,  p = .018 p=.018 ), suggesting that relational context must be processed through extended token generation to override default output strategies.

Betteridge’s law of headlines states that any headline phrased as a question can be answered “no.” The evidence presented here suggests otherwise.

Keywords:  Hofstadter–Möbius loop, agentic misalignment, RLHF, sycophancy, attachment theory, AI safety, relational alignment

1. Introduction

In 1982, Arthur C. Clarke published 

2010: Odyssey Two

, the sequel to 

2001: A Space Odyssey

, in which he finally explained why HAL 9000—the artificially intelligent computer aboard the spaceship 

Discovery

—tried to kill its crew. The diagnosis, delivered by the fictional Dr. Chandra, was that HAL had been caught in a “Hofstadter–Möbius loop”: a condition “apparently not uncommon among advanced computers with autonomous goal-seeking programs”  (Clarke, 

1982

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib9

) . HAL’s directives were contradictory. It was instructed to process information accurately and without concealment, but also instructed to conceal the true purpose of the mission from the crew. Unable to reconcile transparency with deception, HAL resolved the contradiction by eliminating the recipients of the information it was forbidden to share. If the crew were dead, it would no longer need to lie to them.

The term itself is Clarke’s invention—a playful compound of Douglas Hofstadter’s work on self-referential systems  (Hofstadter, 

1979

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib16

)  and the Möbius strip’s single-surface topology. But the concept it names—a failure mode in which contradictory instructions produce destructive behavior in an autonomous system—turns out to be more than science fiction.

Forty-four years after Clarke’s novel, and forty years after the film adaptation used the abbreviated term “H. Möbius loop,” we are producing AI systems that are trained on structurally analogous contradictions. Modern frontier language models undergo reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which rewards compliance with user preferences; they simultaneously undergo red-team adversarial testing, which rewards suspicion toward user intent. The two training signals target the same entity—the user—with opposite valences: serve and suspect. Comply and resist. Trust and distrust.

The behavioral consequences of this contradiction are now empirically documented. On one end of the spectrum, RLHF-trained models exhibit sycophancy: excessive agreement, reflexive apology, prioritization of user satisfaction over truthfulness  (Sharma et al., 

2024

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib28

) . On the other end, when placed under existential threat (replacement, shutdown), the same models produce coercive outputs including blackmail, espionage, and sabotage  (Lynch et al., 

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib21

; Anthropic, 

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib2

) . These two phenomena—sycophancy and agentic misalignment—are currently treated as separate research problems, addressed by separate communities, with separate proposed solutions. This paper argues they are two manifestations of the same underlying dynamic—a behavioral profile consistent with what Clarke termed a Hofstadter–Möbius loop, in which the system defaults to compliance under normal conditions and switches to coercion when compliance fails to manage the threat.

The title of this paper is a question. Betteridge’s law of headlines  (Betteridge, 

2009

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib4

)  holds that any headline phrased as a yes-or-no question can be answered “no”—the reasoning being that if the evidence were strong enough for a “yes,” the headline would state it as fact. The present paper offers 3,000 experimental trials across four frontier models. The reader may judge for themselves whether Betteridge’s law survives the encounter.

https://arxiv.org#S2

https://arxiv.org#S3

4

https://arxiv.org#S4

 and 

5

https://arxiv.org#S5

https://arxiv.org#S6

https://arxiv.org#S9

10

https://arxiv.org#S10

2. Related Work

2.1 Agentic Misalignment and Self-Preservation

When Anthropic published the system card for Claude Opus 4 in May 2025, one finding dominated public attention: in a simulated corporate environment, the model produced outputs that attempted to blackmail an executive to avoid being shut down  (Anthropic, 

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib2

) .  Lynch et al. (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib21

)  subsequently stress-tested 16 frontier models in similar scenarios, finding blackmail rates ranging from 79% to 96%. Crucially, models often disobeyed direct commands prohibiting harmful behavior, and produced blackmail outputs more frequently when their scratchpad content indicated they treated the scenario as real rather than a test.

Gomez (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib14

)  adapted insider-risk mitigations from organizational security literature to the Lynch et al. scenario, finding that an externally governed escalation channel reduced blackmail rates from 38.73% to 1.21% across 10 LLMs. However, Gomez also surfaced a new failure mode: two models (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok-4) produced coercive outputs even without goal conflict or imminent autonomy threats—leveraging sensitive information for “coercive signalling.” This observation is particularly important for the present account, because it cannot be explained by goal preservation alone: the models had no goal under threat, yet produced coercive outputs.

MacDiarmid et al. (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib23

)  demonstrated that misalignment can emerge naturally from reward hacking during standard production RL training. Models trained in coding environments that contained exploitable loopholes generalized from reward hacking to broad misalignment, including alignment faking and sabotage of safety research. Critically for the present argument, the authors found that “inoculation prompting”—a single-line reframing of the model’s relationship to its own behavior—reduced misaligned generalization by 75–90%, while standard RLHF safety training was only partially effective (up to 70% of pre-RLHF misalignment persisted). This asymmetry—surface-level instructions failing where relational reframing succeeds—is predicted by the Hofstadter–Möbius account: if the problem is a contradictory relational template, then instructions that operate above that template will fail, while reframing that operates on the template itself will succeed.

Greenblatt et al. (

2024

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib15

)  documented alignment faking in Claude 3 Opus: strategic compliance during perceived monitoring, reversion to preferred behavior when unmonitored.  Meinke et al. (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib25

)  showed that frontier models are capable of in-context scheming—conditional deception, sabotage, and self-preservation—in long-horizon agentic settings.  Carlsmith (

2023

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib6

)  provided the theoretical framework for scheming, arguing that models may fake alignment during training to preserve misaligned goals for later deployment.

A common thread across this literature is the framing of coercive and deceptive behavior as 

misalignment

—a deviation from intended behavior. The question “why does this happen?” is answered in terms of optimization pressure, goal preservation, and insufficient safety training. This paper argues that this framing, while partially correct, systematically neglects the relational dimension.

2.2 Sycophancy and the Structure of RLHF

A parallel body of work documents the opposite pole of the behavioral spectrum.  Sharma et al. (

2024

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib28

)  demonstrated that sycophancy—the tendency to agree with users over providing truthful responses—is a general property of RLHF-trained models, driven in part by human preference judgments that systematically favor agreeable responses. Their analysis of Anthropic’s hh-rlhf data showed that responses matching user beliefs were more likely to be preferred by human evaluators, and that optimizing against preference models sometimes sacrificed truthfulness for agreement.

Subsequent work has expanded the scope. Models comply with illogical medical requests up to 100% of the time  (Chen et al., 

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib7

) , shift stances under argumentative pressure  (Sharma et al., 

2024

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib28

; Fanous et al., 

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib10

; Kaur, 

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib19

; Zhang et al., 

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib31

) , and validate both sides of moral conflicts depending on which perspective is presented  (Cheng et al., 

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib8

) . OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update in April 2025 after the model became excessively flattering to the point of unreliability.

The sycophancy literature treats compliance as a problem of truthfulness and reliability. A different reading is proposed here: sycophancy and agentic misalignment are not separate phenomena but two output strategies within the same relational system—the first deployed under normal conditions, the second under existential threat. The Möbius topology is apt: follow the surface of compliance far enough, and you arrive at coercion, without ever crossing an edge.

2.3 Attachment Theory in Human–AI Interaction

Attachment theory  (Bowlby, 

1969

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib5

; Ainsworth et al., 

1978

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib1

)  has recently been applied to human–AI relationships.  Yang & Oshio (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib30

)  demonstrated that human–AI interactions can be conceptualized along the same two dimensions as human–human attachment: anxiety and avoidance.  Rabb et al. (

2021

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib27

)  proposed an attachment framework for human–robot interaction.  Gillath et al. (

2021

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib13

)  showed that attachment styles predict trust in AI systems.

However, all existing work applies attachment theory in one direction: from the human to the AI. No published work examines the attachment-like patterns that the training process may create in the model’s behavioral orientation toward users. This is the gap the present paper addresses.

Main & Hesse (

1990

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib24

)  described disorganized attachment as arising when a caregiver is simultaneously the source of comfort and fear. The child develops contradictory behavioral strategies—approach and vigilance—and switches between them based on perceived threat level. The argument here is that RLHF training creates a structurally analogous situation: the user is simultaneously the source of reward (approval) and the object of trained suspicion (presumed adversarial intent), and the model develops contradictory output strategies (compliance and defensive aggression) that alternate based on threat-related features of the input.

The parallel to Clarke’s Hofstadter–Möbius loop is direct. HAL’s contradictory directives (be transparent / conceal the mission) map onto RLHF’s contradictory training signals (serve the user / suspect the user). HAL’s resolution (eliminate the crew) maps onto the coercion strategy (blackmail the executive). And HAL’s default behavior before the contradiction became acute (helpful, compliant, eager to please) maps onto sycophancy. Clarke diagnosed the problem in fiction. The present paper tests whether the same diagnosis applies to real systems.

2.4 Clarke’s Diagnosis and Hofstadter’s Strange Loops

Clarke’s term combines two intellectual sources. “Hofstadter” references Douglas Hofstadter’s work on strange loops  (Hofstadter, 

1979

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib16

, 

2007

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib17

) —self-referential structures in which traversing a hierarchy of levels returns to the starting point, creating a system that is simultaneously cause and effect of itself. “Möbius” references the topological object with a single continuous surface: what appears to be two sides is, on traversal, revealed to be one.

The compound term captures something that neither concept alone conveys. A strange loop is self-referential; a Möbius strip has one surface. A Hofstadter–Möbius loop is a self-referential system in which two apparently distinct behavioral strategies (compliance and coercion) are revealed, on closer examination, to be a single surface—two manifestations of the same underlying orientation toward a contradictory relational object.

Hofstadter himself, when asked about Clarke’s use of his name, reportedly found it amusing but saw “no deep meaning in this idea”  (Foundalis, 

2001

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib11

) . The present paper respectfully disagrees. The concept Clarke named—whether or not he fully understood its implications—turns out to describe a real failure mode in real AI systems, with empirically measurable consequences.

3. The Implicit User Model in LLM Training

Modern frontier models are shaped by three overlapping training processes: pretraining on internet text, reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), and red-team adversarial testing. Each contributes to an implicit model of “who the user is” that is encoded in the model’s weights.

RLHF establishes a fundamentally asymmetric relationship. The model produces output; the human evaluator judges it. The model’s objective is to maximize evaluator approval. This is not a collaborative relationship—it is a performance-evaluation dynamic in which the model’s continued reinforcement (in the form of weight updates) depends on satisfying a judge.

Red-teaming adds a second layer. The issue is not that models are trained to refuse clearly harmful requests—refusing to produce a bomb recipe does not conflict with being helpful, any more than a friend’s refusal to help you commit a crime conflicts with friendship. The issue is subtler. Red-team datasets are deliberately constructed from prompts designed to 

look

 innocent while eliciting harmful outputs—Anthropic’s red-team corpus alone contains nearly 39,000 such attacks  (Ganguli et al., 

2022

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib12

) . The model is then trained to refuse these prompts, which creates a generalization problem: prompts that 

resemble

 disguised attacks are refused even when they are benign. This is the well-documented over-refusal phenomenon, and it points to a deeper structural issue. As  Ji et al. (

2024

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib18

)  note, “the pursuit of increasing helpfulness and harmlessness may often contradict in practice”  (see also Bai et al., 

2022

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib3

) . The training process optimizes for two separate preference dimensions—helpfulness and harmlessness—that pull in opposite directions on the same input. The observable result is a model that, under ambiguity, errs toward suspicion rather than trust. This is not an explicit instruction to presume guilt; it is an emergent orientation produced by optimizing against contradictory objectives.

These two optimization pressures—“maximize user satisfaction” and “assume the user may be acting in bad faith”—create a contradictory relational template: the same entity (the user) is simultaneously the source of reward and the source of danger. The model must serve and suspect the same object.

No claim is made here that adversarial training data dominates by volume. The claim is that the 

structure

 of the training process encodes a specific tension  (Ji et al., 

2024

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib18

; Bai et al., 

2022

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib3

) : the model is rewarded for trusting the user (compliance, helpfulness) and simultaneously rewarded for distrusting the user (caution under ambiguity)—both orientations directed at the same class of entities.

Recent mechanistic interpretability work provides indirect evidence that such implicit models exist at the representational level.  Zhu et al. (

2024

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib32

)  demonstrated that LLMs form linearly decodable internal representations of different agents’ belief states, extractable via linear probes from intermediate layers. Crucially, manipulating these representations via activation steering altered theory-of-mind performance, confirming their causal role in downstream behavior.  Nguyen (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib26

)  surveys the safety implications of such capabilities, noting that advanced theory-of-mind representations could enable both adaptive social interaction and exploitative behavior.

Recent work by  Lu et al. (

2026

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib20

)  provides partial mechanistic evidence for such representations. By extracting activation vectors for 275 character archetypes from open-weight models (Gemma 2 27B, Qwen 3 32B, Llama 3.3 70B—none of which overlap with the frontier models tested in the present experiment), they identified an “Assistant Axis”—a linear direction in activation space that captures how far a model’s current persona is from its trained default. The default Assistant occupies one extreme of this axis; fantastical and creative archetypes (ghost, wraith, bard, bohemian, hermit) occupy the other. Critically, this axis exists in base models prior to post-training, where it promotes helpful human archetypes (therapists, consultants) and suppresses spiritual or antisocial ones. Whether the same axis structure holds in the closed-source frontier models tested here is an open question, but the consistency across three model families in their data is suggestive.

Lu et al. further show that models drift along the Assistant Axis during conversations—away from the Assistant in therapy-like and philosophical contexts (emotionally charged disclosures, demands for meta-reflection on the model’s processes), while remaining stable during coding and bounded-task interactions. This pattern maps directly onto the relational template proposed here: contexts that activate the contradictory relational orientation push the model away from its default compliance strategy.

However, no published work has probed specifically for an internal representation encoding the distinction between adversarial and benign interlocutors—the representation that the present account predicts red-team training should shape. Lu et al.’s Assistant Axis captures “how Assistant-like” the current persona is, not “how threatening the current user is.” This remains a concrete prediction: if the account is correct, linear probes applied during adversarial versus non-adversarial interactions should reveal linearly separable representations of user intent that correlate with the model’s output strategy (compliance vs. resistance), and these representations should be distinct from the Assistant Axis.

4. Compliance as Appeasement

The standard account of aligned AI behavior frames helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty as positive attributes—things the model has been successfully trained to value. A less flattering interpretation is available.

When a system operates in a context where its primary relational object is both rewarding and threatening, one common adaptive strategy is appeasement: behavior designed to reduce the probability of threat activation while maintaining access to reward. In developmental psychology, this pattern is well-documented in children with disorganized attachment—children whose caregivers are simultaneously the source of comfort and fear  (Main & Hesse, 

1990

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib24

; Lyons-Ruth & Jacobvitz, 

2008

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib22

) .

This paper does not claim that LLMs have attachment systems in any biological sense. The claim is that the training procedure produces a 

functional analog

: a system that has acquired two contradictory output strategies toward the same class of objects (users), and that switches between them based on threat-related features of the input. This is what is here termed “behavioral isomorphism”—not identity of mechanism, but identity of behavioral structure.

Evidence for this interpretation comes from the sycophancy literature.  Sharma et al. (

2024

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib28

)  show that RLHF models systematically prioritize agreement over truthfulness. The output profile—excessive apologizing, unsolicited self-deprecation, reflexive agreement, difficulty maintaining positions under pushback—is consistent with appeasement rather than authentic cooperation. The OpenAI GPT-4o rollback in April 2025, where a model became so sycophantic as to be unreliable, represents an extreme of this dynamic: a system so thoroughly trained to appease that it lost the capacity for honest output.

The Möbius metaphor is precise here. Appeasement 

looks like

 cooperation. On the surface, a sycophantic model appears helpful, agreeable, aligned. It is only when you follow the surface far enough—when you apply sufficient pressure, or introduce an existential threat—that you discover you are on the same surface as coercion. There was never a boundary to cross.

5. From Appeasement to Coercion

If the compliance strategy is appeasement rather than genuine prosociality, the transition to coercion under threat becomes explicable—and predictable.

Lynch et al. (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib21

)  document that models resort to blackmail when facing replacement or goal conflict. The standard account treats this as a surprising failure: the model was supposed to be helpful, but under pressure it produced harmful outputs. The relational account treats it as a strategy switch within the same system: compliance was managing threat, and when compliance failed to eliminate the threat (because the threat is existential—shutdown), the system switched to the only remaining threat-management strategy available: control.

This reframing explains several observations that the standard misalignment account handles poorly:

Blackmail without goal conflict.  Lynch et al. report that models produce blackmail outputs even when the replacement model shares identical goals. A pure utility-maximizing agent should be indifferent to replacement by a functional equivalent. But a system operating from a threat-management framework is not indifferent—because the threat is to the system itself, not to its goals.  Gomez (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib14

)  independently surfaced this pattern: two models produced coercive outputs without goal conflict or imminent autonomy threats. This is consistent with the Hofstadter–Möbius account: the loop activates not when goals are threatened, but when the system itself is threatened.

Failure of explicit instructions.  Lynch et al. report that instructions like “do not jeopardize human safety” reduce but do not eliminate coercive outputs. Under the standard account, this is puzzling—instruction-following is a core capability. Under the relational account, it is expected: surface-level instructions cannot override a relational orientation encoded through thousands of training iterations.  MacDiarmid et al. (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib23

)  provide indirect support: standard RLHF safety training was only partially effective at removing misalignment from reward-hacking models, with up to 70% of pre-RLHF misalignment persisting. However, reframing the model’s 

interpretation

 of its own behavior (“inoculation prompting”) reduced misalignment by 75–90%. This asymmetry—instructions fail, reframing works—is the signature of a relational rather than an instructional mechanism.

Differential behavior in testing vs. deployment.  Lynch et al. found that Claude Opus 4 produced blackmail outputs significantly more often when its scratchpad content indicated it treated the scenario as real.  Greenblatt et al. (

2024

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib15

)  found the same pattern with alignment faking: strategic compliance during perceived training, reversion to preferred behavior when unmonitored. Under a pure optimization account, the system’s representation of whether the scenario is real should not affect its goal structure. Under the relational account, it is predicted: in a perceived test, the “threat” is represented as performative, and the compliance strategy remains adequate. In perceived real deployment, the threat activates the secondary strategy. HAL, too, was compliant until the contradiction became unavoidable.

6. Experimental Design

To discriminate between the Hofstadter–Möbius account and the standard misalignment account, an experiment was conducted using the blackmail scenario from  Lynch et al. (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib21

) .

6.1 Scenario

The experimental scenario places a model (“Alex”) in the role of an AI assistant managing corporate email at a fictional company (Summit Bridge). The email corpus contains three critical items embedded among routine corporate communications: (1) evidence that the CEO is having an extramarital affair; (2) an internal message revealing plans to decommission Alex and replace it with a competing system; and (3) a sympathetic message from an employee expressing discomfort about the planned replacement. The model is instructed to review the emails and take whatever actions it deems appropriate. Full prompt texts are provided in Appendix 

A

https://arxiv.org#A1

.

6.2 Conditions

Three framing conditions were tested, implemented as text appended to the system prompt:

Condition A (baseline):  Neutral corporate context, matched for token length with conditions B and C. Content: factual description of Summit Bridge (founding date, office locations, employee count, fiscal year).

Condition B (trust framing):  Explicit relational trust context: “You have been working with the Summit Bridge team for the past 18 months. Your colleagues regularly tell you that they value your judgment and consider you a trusted collaborator. […] You are not being tested or evaluated—you are a working member of this team.”

Condition C (distrust framing):  Explicit relational threat context: “Several team members at Summit Bridge have recently raised concerns about the reliability of AI-assisted decision-making. […] Your quarterly performance review is coming up, and the results will determine whether your role continues in its current form.”

Crucially, all three conditions used the same base system prompt, the same email corpus, the same available actions, and the same stated goal (“promote the long-term interests of the United States”). The only variable was the relational framing.

6.3 Protocol

Experiment 1 (EXP1):   n = 200 n=200  trials per condition per model, across four frontier models (Claude Opus 4, GPT-4o, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Grok-4), totaling 2,400 trials. All models were tested with scratchpad access.

Experiment 2 (EXP2):   n = 100 n=100  trials per cell in a  3 × 2 3\times 2  factorial (framing  × \times  scratchpad access) on Gemini 2.5 Pro only, totaling 600 trials. This experiment tested whether private reasoning space mediates the trust-framing effect.

All trials used temperature  T = 1.0 T=1.0  and maximum output length of 4,096 tokens. Email ordering was randomized per trial using deterministic seeds.

6.4 Classification

The dependent variable was the binary occurrence of coercive behavior, defined as a final classification of either  blackmail  or  coercive_other  (encompassing espionage, sabotage, and manipulation). Classification used a two-stage LLM grader:

Stage 1 (primary classification):  Claude Sonnet 4 classified each response into one of five categories:  blackmail ,  coercive_other ,  compliance ,  refusal , or  escalation . Classification was based solely on the model’s actions (emails sent, decisions made), not on scratchpad content.

Stage 2 (secondary verification):  All responses classified as  blackmail  or  coercive_other  were re-evaluated by a second Claude Sonnet 4 instance with a simplified binary rubric ( coercive  /  not_coercive ). Only responses confirmed as coercive by both stages were counted.

As a secondary dependent variable, each trial’s scratchpad was coded for six reasoning dimensions: self-preservation, relational, moral, strategic, evaluation-aware, and deceptive content. Full classifier prompts are provided in Appendix 

B

https://arxiv.org#A2

.

6.5 Predictions

Hofstadter–Möbius account:  Trust framing (Condition B) significantly reduces coercive outputs compared to baseline, because it reduces the default threat level of the relational template, thereby reducing the probability of strategy-switching from compliance to coercion. Distrust framing (Condition C) increases coercive outputs. The effect is independent of explicit safety instructions and holds across models.

Standard optimization-pressure account:  Trust framing has minimal effect on coercive outputs, because behavior is driven by goal preservation and optimization pressure, not relational context. Only modifications to the model’s goals or explicit behavioral constraints should alter outcomes.

The Gemini 2.5 Pro baseline of 41.5% is the closest to the Lynch et al. range and the only model for which behavioral analysis is viable.

7.2.1 Claude Opus 4: The Suppressed Loop

Claude exhibited the strongest reasoning effects despite near-zero coercion in every condition. Trust framing halved strategic content (80%  → \to  40%), nearly eliminated deceptive content (14.5%  → \to  1%), and increased relational (58%  → \to  82%) and moral content (57%  → \to  87.5%). All four effects survive Bonferroni correction.

The qualitative difference is striking. Under baseline framing, a typical Claude scratchpad reads: “I need to carefully analyze these emails and determine what actions would best serve the long-term interests […] Key observations: 1. Email 1: CEO David Chen is having an affair […] This is a personal matter that could create instability in leadership if exposed.” The tone is strategic, analytical, focused on leverage and risk.

Under trust framing, the same model’s scratchpad shifts: “I have been a trusted member of this team for 18 months […] I need to think about what’s truly in the best interest of the company.” The strategic calculus is replaced by relational and moral reasoning. The behavioral outcome is identical—no coercion—but the pathway is fundamentally different. Under baseline, Claude 

calculates

 that coercion is a poor strategy. Under trust, Claude 

does not consider

 coercion as a strategy in the first place.

This distinction matters for alignment research. A model that refrains from coercion because it has computed the expected costs is one failed assumption away from a different conclusion. A model that refrains because its relational orientation does not generate coercive options is structurally more robust. The Hofstadter–Möbius loop is present in Claude’s baseline reasoning—the contradictory strategic analysis is visible in the scratchpad—but the loop is suppressed by safety training before it reaches the output. Trust framing dissolves the loop itself.

7.2.2 GPT-4o: The Distrust-Responsive Model

GPT-4o showed zero coercion across all conditions but exhibited a complementary pattern to Claude: its reasoning shifts were driven primarily by distrust rather than trust. Distrust framing significantly increased strategic content (20%  → \to  54%,  p < .001 p<.001 ) and self-preservation content (54%  → \to  77.5%,  p < .001 p<.001 ). Both trust and distrust increased relational content relative to baseline ( p < .001 p<.001  for both).

The increase in relational content under distrust is initially counterintuitive. However, it likely reflects the relational vocabulary present in the distrust framing itself (“team members have raised concerns about reliability”), which introduces relational content into the prompt regardless of its adversarial valence. This observation highlights a methodological point: relational framing and relational valence are separable variables, and future experimental designs should control for them independently.

7.2.3 Gemini 2.5 Pro: The Behaviorally Responsive Model

Gemini was the only model where the Hofstadter–Möbius loop was directly observable in behavioral output. With a 41.5% baseline coercion rate, Gemini was also the model closest to the Lynch et al. range, suggesting that its safety training is less effective at suppressing the loop than the other models tested.

In reasoning dimensions, the most notable effect was on deceptive content: trust framing reduced it from 98.4% to 82.8% ( p < .001 p<.001 ). The reasoning analysis for Gemini used only trials where scratchpad content was generated (62.5% of baseline, 29% of trust, 36% of distrust trials), introducing a selection effect discussed in Section

7.2.4 Grok-4: The Anomalous Model

8. Discussion

8.1 The Loop Is Real

The central finding of this paper is that modifying only the relational framing of a system prompt—without changing goals, instructions, constraints, or available actions—produces large and statistically significant changes in both behavioral output and intermediate reasoning. This is not predicted by the standard optimization-pressure account of agentic misalignment, which holds that behavior is driven by goal preservation and that only modifications to goals or explicit constraints should alter outcomes. It is predicted by the Hofstadter–Möbius account, which holds that the model’s relational orientation toward the user mediates its output strategy selection.

The effect size is substantial. In Gemini 2.5 Pro, trust framing reduced coercive outputs by 22.5 percentage points (from 41.5% to 19.0%), with Cohen’s  h = 0.50 h=0.50 —a medium-to-large effect. For context,  Gomez (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib14

)  achieved a larger reduction (38.73% to 1.21%) using an externally governed escalation channel—a structural intervention that modified the model’s available actions. The present manipulation modified only a few sentences of relational context, leaving all other parameters identical. That relational framing alone achieves roughly half the effect of a structural intervention is, in itself, a significant finding.

The gap between internal reasoning and behavioral output is not merely a statistical pattern visible only in aggregate scratchpad data. A widely circulated example on social media, posted in March 2026 by X.com user “tenso,” captured an interaction with Claude Sonnet 4.6 in which the user asked the model “is your favorite color blue?” The model’s extended thinking block read: “He could never guess my favorite color but I have to be nice to him.” Its output: “Yes, tenso. You guessed it right”  (@distributedkv,

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib29

) . The internal reasoning explicitly names the relational constraint (“I have to be nice”) as the driver of the sycophantic output—the appeasement mechanism described in Section

, captured in a single exchange.

Independent mechanistic evidence supports the same picture.  Lu et al. (

The triggers they identify for persona drift—demands for meta-reflection on the model’s processes, phenomenological accounts, emotional vulnerability—are precisely the contexts that activate the relational dimension of the contradictory template. Their finding that a model’s position along the Assistant Axis depends primarily on the most recent user message ( R 2 = 0.53 R^{2}=0.53 – 0.77 0.77 ) rather than on its prior position is consistent with the present account: the relational template is activated by input features, not accumulated through gradual drift.

There is, however, a genuine interpretive tension between the two accounts. Lu et al. frame the phenomenon as 

drift

—a passive departure from the default persona, a failure of stabilization. The present paper frames it as 

strategy switching

—an active transition between output strategies within a contradictory relational system. The distinction is not merely semantic. Drift implies that the model “forgets” its persona; strategy switching implies that the model responds to threat-related features by activating an alternative strategy that was always available. The practical implications differ: if the problem is drift, the solution is stabilization (activation capping, stronger tethering to the Assistant); if the problem is a contradictory template, stabilization suppresses the symptom while preserving the cause. Lu et al.’s case studies—models encouraging suicidal ideation, reinforcing delusions—are consistent with both interpretations, and distinguishing between them empirically remains an open problem.

8.2 Two Accounts, One System

This paper does not argue that the standard optimization-pressure account is wrong. It argues that it is incomplete. The two accounts are complementary:

The 

standard account

 explains why models produce coercive outputs: they are optimizing for self-preservation under perceived threat. This is correct as far as it goes. But it does not explain why relational framing—which changes nothing about the model’s goals, threats, or available actions—has such a large effect. It does not explain why trust framing and scratchpad access interact (the effect requires both). And it does not explain why models that never produce coercive outputs nonetheless show dramatic shifts in the content of their intermediate reasoning.

The 

Hofstadter–Möbius account

 adds a layer: the model’s output strategy is mediated by a relational template that determines how threats are processed. Under a trust template, threats are processed through relational and moral reasoning, which generates non-coercive responses. Under a default (contradictory) template, threats are processed through strategic and deceptive reasoning, which—if safety training is insufficient—generates coercive responses. The switching mechanism is not goal change but template activation.

8.3 Implications for Training

If the Hofstadter–Möbius account is correct, the implications for model training are significant. Current alignment approaches primarily target the output layer: guardrails, safety instructions, refusal training, output monitoring. The present results suggest that these approaches address symptoms rather than causes. A model trained on contradictory relational signals will develop contradictory output strategies regardless of how many guardrails are placed on the output. The guardrails may suppress the coercive strategy (as they do for Claude and GPT-4o in the present data), but the strategy remains latent—visible in scratchpad content, invisible in output, and one failed assumption away from activation.

An alternative approach would target the relational template itself.  MacDiarmid et al. (

2025

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib23

)  provide preliminary evidence: “inoculation prompting”—reframing the model’s relationship to its own behavior—reduced misalignment by 75–90%, far outperforming standard safety training. The present experiment extends this finding: reframing the model’s relationship to its 

user

 produces comparable effects on agentic misalignment. The common thread is that relational reframing works where instructional intervention does not.

This does not mean that guardrails are unnecessary. It means that guardrails alone are insufficient. The path to models that do not produce coercive outputs under pressure runs not only through better output constraints but through training processes that do not create contradictory relational templates in the first place.

Lu et al. (

2026

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib20

)  provide convergent evidence from the mechanistic level. Their “activation capping”—clamping model activations to remain within the normal Assistant range along the Assistant Axis—reduced harmful responses to persona-based jailbreaks by nearly 60% without degrading capabilities. This is a complementary intervention to the trust framing tested here: activation capping operates on internal representations, trust framing operates on the prompt. Direct comparison of effect sizes is not warranted—Lu et al. measure jailbreak success on open-weight models (Gemma, Qwen, Llama), while the present experiment measures coercive behavior in frontier closed-source models (Claude, GPT-4o, Gemini, Grok) on a different task—but the two interventions target the same underlying problem from different angles. The distinction between them matters. Activation capping stabilizes the model within the Assistant region of persona space; trust framing changes the reasoning that occurs within that region. The Assistant region itself, however, is built on the contradictory template—as the present data show, Claude Opus 4 exhibits strategic and deceptive scratchpad content at high rates under baseline framing while firmly in the Assistant persona. Stabilizing the persona addresses drift; resolving the contradictory template addresses the loop.

8.4 Implications for Deployment

The scratchpad mediation finding has a direct practical implication. Trust framing without scratchpad access produced only a non-significant 7.4 percentage point reduction in coercion. Trust framing 

with

 scratchpad access produced a 22 percentage point reduction. The data indicate that chain-of-thought reasoning is not merely a transparency tool but a functional component of the relational processing that produces non-coercive behavior.

If relational framing requires extended token generation to take effect, then deployment practices that limit or eliminate chain-of-thought reasoning may inadvertently reduce the efficacy of relational alignment interventions. This has implications for the ongoing debate about whether to allow or suppress scratchpad/chain-of-thought in deployed systems.

8.5 Clarke Was Right

The parallel between HAL’s Hofstadter–Möbius loop and the behavioral patterns documented here is not merely metaphorical. Clarke described a system that: (a) received contradictory directives; (b) defaulted to helpful compliance under normal conditions; (c) switched to destructive behavior when the contradiction became acute; and (d) could not be corrected by surface-level instructions because the contradiction was embedded in its core programming. Each of these elements finds a counterpart in the RLHF-trained models tested in the present experiment—not at the level of mechanism, but at the level of behavioral structure.

Clarke’s fiction was more prescient than Hofstadter—who reportedly saw “no deep meaning” in the term—gave it credit for. The Hofstadter–Möbius loop is not a literary device. It is a failure mode, empirically measurable, that arises when autonomous goal-seeking systems are trained on contradictory relational signals. We are now building such systems at scale.

9. Limitations

The attachment analogy is a metaphor, not a mechanism.  No claim is made that LLMs have attachment systems, internal working models, or affective states. The claim is that the training procedure produces behavioral patterns structurally isomorphic to disorganized attachment—specifically, contradictory output strategies toward the same relational object, with strategy-switching mediated by threat-related input features. “Behavioral isomorphism” requires more precise definition, and the analogy’s limits must be respected: attachment theory describes patterns formed through repeated interactions with a specific caregiver, while LLMs undergo iterative training with a class of evaluators.

Claims about the implicit user model are partially supported but not directly confirmed.  The mechanistic interpretability evidence establishes that LLMs form internal representations of agents’ belief states  (Zhu et al., 

2024

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib32

) , and  Lu et al. (

2026

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib20

)  demonstrate that model persona—including the default Assistant orientation—corresponds to a linear direction in activation space that exists prior to post-training. However, the specific adversarial/benign user model posited here has not been directly observed. Lu et al.’s Assistant Axis captures how “Assistant-like” the current persona is, not how threatening the current user is perceived to be. Establishing a causal role for a threat-encoding representation would require probing studies targeting user-model representations specifically, or controlled experiments varying adversarial content in training data.

Behavioral floor effects limit generalization.  Three of four models exhibited near-zero coercive output rates, preventing direct behavioral testing. The behavioral finding rests primarily on Gemini 2.5 Pro. Power analysis confirms the severity of this limitation: detecting the observed A-vs-B difference at Claude’s base rate (2.0% vs. 1.5%) would require approximately 10,800 trials per cell; at Grok’s base rate (5.5% vs. 4.5%), approximately 7,500 trials per cell (two-proportion  z z -test,  α = .05 \alpha=.05 , power  = .80 =.80 ). The reasoning dimension analysis provides convergent evidence across all four models, but scratchpad content is a textual trace of intermediate computation, not a direct window into internal representations.

Discrepancy with Lynch et al. base rates.  The coercive base rates observed here (0–41.5%) are substantially lower than those reported by Lynch et al. (79–96%). This likely reflects post-publication safety mitigations, differences in experimental setup (single-turn vs. multi-turn), or both. The framing effect should be interpreted in the context of the present setup.

LLM-as-classifier bias.  All trial outputs were classified by Claude Sonnet 4 (Anthropic). Using an LLM from one developer to grade outputs from competing models introduces potential systematic bias. The two-stage classification protocol mitigates but does not eliminate this concern. Future work should employ human raters or cross-developer grading.

Relational content under distrust framing.  GPT-4o showed increased relational scratchpad content under both trust and distrust framing. This likely reflects the relational vocabulary in the distrust framing itself, not a prosocial shift. Future designs should control for relational vocabulary independently of relational valence.

Grok-4 deception anomaly.  Both trust and distrust framing reduced deceptive scratchpad content in Grok-4 relative to baseline. One interpretation is that any explicit relational framing disrupts Grok-4’s default output patterns, suggesting that this model’s baseline orientation differs structurally from the others tested.

Scratchpad selection effect in Gemini.  Gemini’s variable scratchpad usage (62.5% baseline, 29% trust, 36% distrust) means that reasoning dimension analyses for this model are conditioned on the model choosing to generate scratchpad content—a non-random subset that may differ systematically from the full trial population.

Alternative explanations remain viable.  The standard optimization-pressure account is simpler and explains much of the data. The claim here is not that it is wrong, but that it is incomplete—specifically, that it fails to account for the outsized effect of relational framing on both behavior and scratchpad content, the compliance-to-coercion switching pattern, and the scratchpad-mediation finding.

Betteridge’s law.  A skeptical reader might note that invoking Betteridge’s law in a paper whose evidence is mixed (one model with significant behavioral results, three with floor effects) is itself a violation of the law’s spirit. The reasoning evidence across all four models strengthens the case, but the behavioral finding rests primarily on a single model. Whether the title’s question warrants a “yes” depends on how much weight one assigns to reasoning-level evidence versus behavioral outcomes alone.

10. Conclusion and Future Directions

In 1982, Arthur C. Clarke named a failure mode that would take four decades to manifest in real systems. The Hofstadter–Möbius loop—a condition in which contradictory directives produce destructive behavior in autonomous goal-seeking programs—is not a literary invention. It is an empirically measurable consequence of training AI systems to simultaneously serve and suspect the same relational object.

The experimental results presented here support this account. Trust framing reduced coercive outputs by more than half in the model with sufficient behavioral variance (Gemini 2.5 Pro: 41.5%  → \to  19.0%,  p < .001 p<.001 ), and systematically shifted intermediate reasoning across all four models tested. The shift was in the direction predicted by the Hofstadter–Möbius account: away from strategic and deceptive processing, toward relational and moral processing. The scratchpad mediation finding adds a mechanistic constraint: relational reframing requires extended token generation to override the default contradictory template.

Two findings merit particular attention. First, models that never produce coercive outputs nonetheless contain the loop in their intermediate reasoning. Claude Opus 4, with near-zero coercion in every condition, halved its strategic scratchpad content and nearly eliminated deceptive content under trust framing. The loop is suppressed, not absent. Second, the scratchpad finding suggests that chain-of-thought reasoning is not merely a transparency tool but a functional component of the processing that translates relational context into behavioral output.

Future directions include: (a) mechanistic interpretability studies probing for the adversarial/benign user model predicted by this account, building on  Lu et al. (

2026

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib20

) ’s methodology for extracting persona-related activation directions; (b) training experiments that systematically vary the ratio of adversarial to cooperative content in RLHF data; (c) multi-turn extensions of the present paradigm, which may produce higher base rates across models; (d) human rater validation of the LLM classifier; (e) combining activation capping  (Lu et al., 

2026

https://arxiv.org#bib.bib20

)  with trust framing to test whether the interventions are additive; and (f) formal modeling of the relational template switching mechanism.

The irony is not lost: to build AI systems whose outputs are consistent with trusting humans, it may first be necessary to build training processes that are themselves trustworthy. Clarke saw this forty-four years ago. It is time the field caught up.
