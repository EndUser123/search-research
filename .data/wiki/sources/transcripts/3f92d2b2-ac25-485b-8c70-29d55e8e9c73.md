# We Finally Have a Weapon Against the Deadliest Cancer
Pancreatic cancer isn’t a silent 
disease you can catch early. It’s  

stealth. It slips past the body’s early 
warning signs, so by the time something  

feels wrong, it’s often already advanced.
Over the years, survival has barely moved. Only  

a handful of patients out of every hundred ever 
make it through. Statistically, it’s like playing  

Russian roulette with 5 chambers loaded out of 6.
But after decades of failure, researchers have  

started breaking through pancreatic cancer’s 
defenses. They’ve turned it from untouchable  

into something they can go after.
And what they’ve discovered inside  

that “stealth” system changes everything.
Pancreatic cancer is one of the most lethal  

diseases around. While modern medicine has 
made huge headway combating viruses and  

improving cancer survival rates, pancreatic 
cancer has remained an unsolved mystery. A  

fortress that’s been impossible for 
doctors to penetrate with drugs.

Even with modern medical developments, the overall 
survival rate for pancreatic cancer is low.

Very low.
Today, it sits at only 13%.  

It’s the lowest of all major cancers. In rich 
countries, the average 5 year-survival rate for  

all cancers diagnosed in 2017 was 70%.
And it gets worse.

Because most pancreatic cancers don’t get 
identified until it’s too late. They’ve already  

metastasized to stage IV. In fact, over 4 out of 
5 pancreatic cancers get diagnosed at this stage. 

The survival rate for metastatic 
pancreatic cancer? In five years,  

only 3% of patients are still alive.

What makes it so lethal?

The way pancreatic cancer works. It’s a 
perfect storm of location, immune response,  

and presentation. Pancreatic tumors rarely 
cause noticeable symptoms, partly because  

of their location deep inside the body. They 
don’t become physically visible, and they don’t  

cause symptoms a patient can feel until they’ve 
spread to other organs and parts of the body.

There are no screening tests to catch 
the disease early as it builds up quickly  

and quietly. Usually, patients only 
notice the first symptoms - jaundice,  

abdominal pain - once the 
cancer has already spread.

By then, the chances of 
survival are next to nothing.

Pancreatic cancer suppresses the body’s early 
warning systems because of its own structure,  

making it virtually treatment-proof.

Until now.

Doctors were always puzzled at the 
body's response to pancreatic cancer,  

because in most patients, there was 
almost none. They couldn’t figure out  

why patients’ immune systems never seemed 
to come online. Cancer cells would grow  

and multiply throughout the body without 
T-cells putting any kind of stop to it.

Then researchers discovered 
what was actually going on.

Pancreatic tumors cloak themselves with 
glycoproteins to mimic healthy cells.  

This sugar coating signals the immune system 
to “stand down”, allowing the tumor cells to  

hide in plain sight. The most common form of 
pancreatic cancer - PDAC - may trigger some  

kind of immune response. But the T cells still 
can’t infiltrate the tumor. There’s something in  

the design of pancreatic cancer cells that stops 
the one thing that could fight them in its tracks.

But there’s a new development on the 
horizon. Because the untreatable,  

“undruggable” code… has just been broken.

To outsmart pancreatic cancer, scientists 
had to find a way to permeate the concrete  

bunker the cancer creates. A bunker 
known as the “desmoplastic stroma”. A  

fortified wall scientists thought was 
almost impossible to break through.

As pancreatic cancer grows, it 
hijacks the cells around it.  

The mutated cancer cells in turn damage 
the tissue around them. As a result,  

the body’s wound-healing mode is activated, 
creating scar tissue around the tumor.

PDAC uses this tissue as a shield. Called a 
stroma, it’s much denser than the tissue that  

surrounds most other cancers. For some reason, 
PDAC creates an incredibly fibrous tissue barrier  

that stops most cancer treatments from even 
reaching the cells they’re supposed to fight.

Besides working as the worst 
kind of high-security fence,  

the desmoplastic stroma creates another problem.

Internal fluid pressure.

Tumor cells create a highly hydrophilic 
sugar that attracts and absorbs water,  

causing parts of the tumor to swell. But the 
tumor can’t drain or expand outwards because the  

dense stroma is blocking it. It’s like filling 
a balloon with water inside a concrete shell.  

There’s nowhere for it to expand, resulting in 
internal pressure on cells and blood vessels.

Even if doctors could find a way around the 
barrier, the pressure the stroma creates  

physically squeezes shut the blood vessels 
that would deliver medicine. At that point,  

chemotherapy becomes a losing battle. 
Throwing drugs at a pancreatic tumor  

becomes akin to throwing spaghetti at a 
wall and expecting it to pass through.

All of this made traditional therapies 
against pancreatic cancer painful,  

and mostly useless. The medical world had to 
come up with something else. Something better.

Enter NALIRIFOX.

Traditional chemotherapy doesn’t target 
cancer cells. It targets everything.

Chemo is the equivalent of 
biological carpet bombing.

Used against a disease it can’t reach, 
it basically ends up killing a lot of  

functional parts of the bodies while leaving 
the pancreatic tumor untreated. One oncologist,  

Dr. Sameek Roychowdhury, said, 
“even with our best chemotherapies,  

the average benefit is around 
6 months, sometimes as little  

as weeks or months….It’s barely enough 
time for families to grasp the situation”

But NALIRIFOX uses a smarter 
approach. A targeted one.

The drug uses the cancer’s stealth against 
itself. It slips past healthy tissue,  

breaks through the tumor’s defenses, and only 
releases its full payload once it’s inside.

A study called the NAPOLI-3 trial was conducted 
across 18 countries to see how effective the  

new medication was. 770 patients who had 
been diagnosed with metastatic pancreatic  

cancer - the deadliest kind - were selected 
to take part in the trial in 2020 and 2021.  

About half of them were given NALIRIFOX, 
while the other half was given the normal  

first-line chemotherapy treatment 
for metastatic pancreatic cancer.

Those who were given the standard 
chemotherapy treatment had a median  

survival rate of 9.2 months. Those who 
had been treated with NALIRIFOX? Had  

an extended median survival rate of 11.1 months.

While that may not seem like 
much to the average person,  

it’s a major victory for oncology. 
That translates to a 20% improvement  

in survival for what was considered 
an almost certainly terminal disease.

In the world of cancer that’s 
a generational leap forward.

In addition, patients took NALIRIFOX for 6 
weeks more, on average, than the standard  

chemotherapy treatment, which allowed 
the drug itself to be more effective.

Due to these incredible results, 
the FDA made reviewing NALIRIFOX  

a top priority. In February 2024, 
the drug got official FDA approval,  

making it more easily accessible to patients 
who needed it, as well as, possibly,  

affordable. Though the FDA doesn’t directly 
control what insurance can and can’t cover,  

when a drug is FDA approved, it usually translates 
to being covered by Medicare and Medicaid.

But NALIRIFOX is just step one.

Because once doctors got inside 
the tumor, they still had to  

deal with a dangerous enemy…one they 
couldn’t physically touch. A genetic  

villain that had made pancreatic 
cancer a death sentence for most.

The structure of the mutation 
that caused the cancer itself.

Approximately 90-95% of pancreatic cancers 
are driven by the KRAS mutation. Science had  

famously labeled it “untouchable” for 40 years.

The KRAS gene works like an on/off switch,  

controlling how cells grow in the 
body. In most pancreatic cancers,  

the KRAS has somehow been flipped into the on 
position and becomes permanently stuck there.

Scientists had figured out the exact mutation  

they needed to target. The problem? 
They had no way of going about it.

Drugs usually work by latching onto a protein’s 
surface. But the KRAS mutation was incredibly  

smooth. So smooth scientists called it “The 
Greased Ball”. Nothing could latch on. Medicines  

couldn’t bind to it, and as long as they couldn’t 
bind to it, they couldn’t work against it.

Until scientists realized they 
were going about it all wrong.

Billions were spent trying to find 
a way to grip the surface of the  

protein. Decades of experimental 
drugs and clinical trials turned  

out to be complete flops. But there was 
a hidden flaw in the armor all along.

One that could be used to go 
after the heart of the tumor.

Because the protein may not have 
had a crevice for drugs to latch  

onto…but scientists realized that, using the 
right methods, they could force one to open.

The problem was never what it appeared to 
be in the first place. The funding for the  

war on cancer hadn’t been wasted. Scientists 
put a lot of effort into discovering exactly  

the chemicals needed to kill the cancer 
cells. The only problem was that the KRAS  

protein seemingly had no pocket for the drugs 
to sit in and deliver the fatal chemical dose.

In 2013 Kevan Shokat, a biochemist at University 
of California, San Francisco, focused on a  

specific KRAS mutation called G12C. In simple 
terms, this mutation turns glycine into a highly  

reactive residue. Scientists designed a bunch 
of molecules that would glue themselves onto it.

And when they did, something interesting happened.

The gluing action forced the protein to 
shift, revealing a small, hidden cavity  

within it. Scientists called this 
discovery the “Switch II” pocket.

Shokat’s breakthrough only applied to a tiny 
fraction of pancreatic cancers, less than 1%. But  

it proved something many researchers had doubted 
for decades: KRAS could be targeted. Finding the  

right chemicals that would take advantage of this 
backdoor for most other types of pancreatic cancer  

took much longer. Ones that could be used not 
to attack the protein…but to take it offline  

altogether. Over 480 potential compounds were 
screened for bonding to the Switch II pocket.  

Until they found the chemicals that would turn 
the KRAS “switch” back to the “off” position.

Forever.

Scientists had unlocked a perfect drug 
delivery system. Now they just needed  

to develop a highly specific weapon to exploit 
it. But there was a problem. The compounds were  

almost too precise. They only worked against 
that specific mutation. Change the mutation,  

and the drugs lost their target, becoming 
virtually useless against the cancer.

After years of research and experimentation, it 
appears scientists have the answer they want.  

Their own personal assassin, lethal against 
the world’s deadliest cancer: Daraxonrasib.

And it could be the “Holy Grail” of oncology.

Researchers had already come up with 
some targeted KRAS G12C inhibitors,  

but they were a little too targeted. They 
weren’t as effective against pancreatic  

tumors as doctors would like it to 
be. If the protein mutated further,  

or became drug resistant, these drugs’ 
effectiveness went down to almost nothing.

Daraxonrasib is more broad-spectrum 
and it shows potential to overcome  

drug resistance to first generation inhibitors.

The drug works by recruiting a helper 
molecule called cyclophilin A. Acting  

like a kind of molecular glue, it 
creates a connection that lets the  

drug lock onto KRAS and shut down the 
signals driving the cancer's growth.

So the drug rips through the body, 
finds the mutated KRAS proteins,  

and latches onto them like a magnet. Where 
chemotherapy is a full-body carpet bombing,  

Daraxonrasib functions as 
a targeted assassination.

The results were outstanding. 
In a series of drug trials,  

500 patients were selected to either receive 
chemotherapy or treatment with Daraxonrasib.  

Almost 92% of them had RAS G12 mutations.

The average survival rate for chemotherapy was 
6.7 months. For those treated with Daraxonrasib  

it increased to 13.2 months. Individual 
patient reports also showed that for most,  

Daraxonrasib was much less toxic than 
chemotherapy. Patients experienced fewer and  

less severe side effects overall. And way fewer 
decided to stop the treatment because of them.

It only gets better.

Because KRAS mutations don’t just drive pancreatic 
cancer. They trigger a whole lot of tumors,  

including lung, colorectal, ovarian, endometrial 
and some of the other most common and deadly  

cancers around. Over 5.1 million people are 
diagnosed with one of those 4 cancers every  

year. In addition to the over 500,000 people that 
will receive a diagnosis of pancreatic cancer.

A drug that went after the KRAS mutations that 
caused the cancer could give a whole new hope,  

and be the literal difference between life and 
death, for 5.6 million people annually. In fact,  

just lung, pancreatic, and colon cancers alone 
are the 3 leading causes of cancer deaths.

Cracking the KRAS G12D mystery doesn’t just 
provide hope for pancreatic cancer patients;  

but for all patients suffering 
from KRAS-driven cancers.

While this new miracle drug starts taking 
out current tumor targets, there’s an even  

brighter future on the horizon. Scientists are 
on the brink of another incredible development:  

a technology that doesn’t just target cancer, 
but ensures that the tumor never returns.

A cancer vaccine.

While Daraxonrasib is a near-miraculous 
development, it’s not the end of the  

story for pancreatic cancer patients. 
Because for most people, Daraxonrasib  

stops working after a while. Their tumor becomes 
drug-resistant. Despite an extended lifespan,  

they are left once again facing their 
worst fear: the return of their cancer.

One company is using mRNA technology - 
like the kind used in the vaccine that  

ended the COVID-19 pandemic - to change this 
narrative. Their hope is to use modern medicine  

not just to treat pancreatic cancer, but 
to stop it from ever coming back again.

The BioNTech customizable mRNA vaccine is 
currently in its Phase 1 trial. So far,  

the results look promising.

The vaccine is custom-made for each patient 
based on their unique tumor DNA. To better  

target the cancerous cells it needs to 
eradicate, which differ from patient to  

patient. In the BioNTech trial, vaccines 
were given to 16 patients after pancreatic  

cancer surgery. The treatment was paired 
with chemotherapy and immunotherapy drugs.

In half of the patients, the vaccine activated 
tumor-specific immune cells. The mRNA shot  

specifically trained these soldier cells 
to recognize the tumor’s unique signature,  

and wipe out the cancerous cells. 
They teamed up with the body’s own  

immune system to train it to identify 
and fight the pancreatic invader.

And how effective is this current “search 
and destroy” vaccine for pancreatic cancer?

Of the 8 patients whose immune 
systems the vaccine activated,  

7 of them were still alive 4 to 6 years 
after surgery. That’s an almost 90% survival  

rate. Much higher than the 13% currently 
attributed to pancreatic cancer patients.

Oncologists were stunned at the results.

One doctor at Memorial Sloan Kettering 
Cancer Center said of the new vaccine:  

it “has the potential to make a difference 
for one of the deadliest cancers”.

Researchers still need to figure out why 
some patients’ immune systems respond  

to the vaccine while others don’t. Even for 
those who might respond well to the vaccine,  

there’s a huge question of if they 
can get it in the first place.

Because the cost of survival is shockingly high.

NALIRIFOX, Daraxonrasib, personalized 
mRNA vaccines…all of them represent  

giant technological, scientific, and 
medical leaps forward. Unfortunately,  

all of them are currently out of reach for 
all but the wealthiest of patients. Each  

of these drugs costs tens of thousands 
of dollars per cycle. And the cycles  

needed to keep someone in remission over a 
lifetime can add up to astronomical costs.

NALIRIFOX costs an average of $7,800 per cycle. 
For the multiple cycles of treatment required,  

this can easily add up to between $150,000 and 
$200,000 in lifetime costs. That’s enough to  

cover a 4-year-degree at a private university, 
or even a starter home in a cheaper area.

Paying it for one drug alone would 
throw most people into bankruptcy.

And in combination with the other drugs on 
this list? It would be prohibitively expensive.

Daraxonrasib is not yet commercially 
available. But an analysis of Revolution  

Medicines’ potential profits revealed how much 
the company expects to charge patients for it.

A monthly course would be priced 
between $30,500 and $37,300. The  

per-patient revenue this adds 
up to an average of $250,000.

Taking these two drugs together 
would already put the average  

pancreatic cancer patient half a million 
in the hole. That’s before they’ve even  

considered a custom-made mRNA vaccine. 
The cost for that? BioNTech has stated,  

after years of work, they can probably get it 
down to under an eye-watering $100,000 per dose.

Will access to potentially life-saving 
medicine be given to all patients? Or  

will it represent yet another “Healthcare 
Divide”? One where the elite get the most  

effective drugs and best chances 
of survival, while everyone else  

defaults to paying exorbitant amounts 
for chemo and hoping for the best?

The good news is the FDA has approved expanded 
access to Daraxonrasib based on its early,  

promising results. And now that researchers 
have shown KRAS can be beaten, pharmaceutical  

companies are racing to develop their own 
drugs to exploit the crack in its armor.

As of June 2026, Johnson & Johnson has spent $1 
billion to enter the race for KRAS-related cancer  

treatments. Theoretically, competition between 
the major pharmaceutical companies should drive  

drug prices down. But given the fact that in 
countries like the U.S., many drugs remain  

distressingly out of reach for the average citizen 
due to cost, even with competition in the mix,  

the political and social reality of these medical 
leaps forward in the future remains to be seen.

But does this all mean that we’re at 
the end of the cancer death sentence?

One thing is certain. We have entered 
a new scientific reality. A “God Mode”  

era of oncology. One in which no mutation is 
off limits, where “undruggable” conditions  

don’t make diseases untreatable; they just 
present another challenge to be solved.

The new methods developed to treat KRAS 
mutations can be applied to a whole lot  

of formerly undefeatable mutations and proteins.

The only thing that’s doomed is pancreatic 
cancer’s horrific 13% survival rates. Based  

on the trials, patients treated with these 
new medical miracles had their lifespans  

almost double after being diagnosed 
with pancreatic cancer. Even patients  

with metastatic pancreatic cancer saw 
their risk of death reduced by 60%.

Better yet, the actual quality of 
patients’ lives went up as well.  

The milder side effects of Daraxonrasib 
meant these patients gained more than  

extra months. They gained quality of 
life and more time with loved ones.

And the only thing they need 
to do? Take 3 pills once a day.

Families that in the past 
only wished for, at best,  

a few more months with loved ones through painful,  

excruciating rounds of chemotherapy now have 
new hope. The hope that they could get anywhere  

from over a year to several years with their 
relatives after a pancreatic cancer diagnosis.

Even the hope that one day, their loved 
one could be completely cancer free.

The medical community has come together to - 
quite literally - give people new life. And  

the dream is that these breakthroughs 
won’t just stop at pancreatic cancer.

Cracking the “undruggable” code could 
be the first step in treating some of  

the deadliest cancers the world has ever 
seen. The new medicines being developed  

today could save enough people to fill up 
an entire major city. Every single year.

And who knows what other miracles 
the future of medicine could bring…

Breakthroughs like this can buy time. But 
they can’t stop the inevitable. And those  

who see it every day have learned 
some surprising lessons. Find out  

more in “What Doctors Wish Their Patients 
Knew About Death.” Or watch this video.
