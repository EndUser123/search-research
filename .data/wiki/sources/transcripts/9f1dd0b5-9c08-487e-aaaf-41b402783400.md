# Study Like THIS to Build Rock-Solid Memory
Scientifically, your brain 
 forgets 75% of what you read within a week.  

 And it has been making your 
 learning hard since  the day you were born.

Then one scientist introduced the 
 most powerful learning technique ever discovered. 

A two-stage learning system that 
 trains your memory and 

helps you remember what you read, 
learn, or study so you never forget it again.

And this video is exactly about that.

Herman Ebbinghouse, a German scientist, 
experimented on his own memory for 5 years  

and discovered something fascinating.

Whenever we read something new, our 
 brain follows a predictable pattern of forgetting.

Within a day, it forgets about 
 66% of it and within a week 75%.

The memory keeps fading over time and after a month the brain only remembers 10 to 20% of it.

This is the forgetting curve 
 and it happens to everyone.

Before you feel bad about your 
 memory, understand why our brain does so.

Forgetting is actually a feature of our brain, not a flaw. 

Our brain gets millions of bits of 
information every second.

And if we remembered every face 
 on every street, every word from every conversation,

we would be overwhelmed.

So unless we signal our brain that 
something matters, it lets it fade from memory.  

But the question is how do we 
 signal our brain to not to forget what we read?

Research shows that the depth of first 
learning decides the steepness of the forgetting curve.

And this is the first stage of our learning system. Deep encoding.

When we read something for the first time in a way that lets our brain blend it with information that already exists,  

the new information becomes deeply encoded.

And once it is encoded like this, 
 the brain does not remove it from memory easily.

So first think about what you already 
 know about the thing you're going to read.

Then add new knowledge on top of that.

If you do not have existing knowledge about it,

create simple analogies to 
 connect it with something you already know.

For example, if you read about electricity, 
 you might picture it as water flowing through a pipe.

While reading facts or 
 memorizing words create pneummonics.

For example, 
 you are learning the vocabulary of a new language.

Let's say Japanese. In Japanese, mizu 
means water.

To remember this, create a 
 minimonic like me in the zoo searching for water.

This is the foundation.

When your foundation is strong, your forgetting curve becomes less steep from the very beginning.

Now comes the second stage, strategic recall.

Revising when you are just about 
to forget.

The forgetting curve has a steep 
 drop within the first seven days of learning.

Then it gradually levels off with only 
 small changes across the following months.

Your revision needs 
 to follow this pattern to flatten the curve.

Without strategic recall, by the end of a 
 month, you retain only about 20% of what you read.

Reviewing the material within 
 20 minutes can raise this to about 50%.

Revisiting it again after one day can increase 
 retention by the end of the month to nearly 75%.

And reviewing it again after a week 
 can raise this from about 80% to full recall.

This is also called spaced repetition.

If you want to remember it for an 
 even longer time, review it after growing gaps.

You might wonder, how does this 
 work when you have so much on your plate?

Spaced repetition needs a little effort 
 at the beginning but becomes very efficient with time.

After two to three reviews, you only 
 need a short skim that takes a few minutes.

Many recent replication studies on 
 forgetting curve point to the same result.

This two-stage method 
 strengthens each idea at the right time

and greatly increases how long you can remember it.
