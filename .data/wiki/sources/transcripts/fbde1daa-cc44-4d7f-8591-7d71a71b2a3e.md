I've been around Hardware-Accelerated GPU Scheduling long enough to see it swing between "must-have toggle" and "turn it off immediately," depending on who you ask (or how my YouTube Shorts algorithm is feeling on any given day). Ever since it arrived with Windows 10, HAGS has been regarded as an optimization miracle by some, and an unnecessary complication by others. You'll find benchmarks praising its latency improvements, all while the comments will be rife with anecdotes of smoother gameplay with it disabled.

I've tested it both ways across multiple builds over the years, and I've landed somewhere in the middle. On 

my main rig with an RTX 4070 Ti

https://www.xda-developers.com/the-best-browser-games-i-always-play-even-with-a-powerful-gaming-pc/

, it stays on, no questions asked. On older GPUs, however, especially ones with 6GB or 8GB of VRAM, things get a lot more interesting. This time, turning HAGS off on my partner's aging setup made the case clearer than ever.

Here's what HAGS actually does

A lot of folks swear by HAGS, and rightly so

Hardware-accelerated GPU scheduling

https://www.xda-developers.com/what-is-hags-windows-turn-it-on/

 sounds more complicated than it really is. At its core, it shifts some of the GPU scheduling work 

away

 from the CPU and hands it directly to the GPU. Traditionally, the CPU acts as the middleman, managing how frames are queued and delivered. HAGS just cuts that middle layer down, letting the GPU handle its own workload more directly.

Now, on systems with weaker CPUs, 

HAGS can reduce overhead

https://www.howtogeek.com/756935/how-to-enable-hardware-accelerated-gpu-scheduling-in-windows-11/

 and free up valuable processing headroom. In some cases, that results in direct and immediately measurable performance gains, with many users often reporting an FPS boost of 10% or more. More importantly, with HAGS turned on, input lag and latency both tend to improve. Frame delivery also feels more immediate, and the whole system just responds better under load.

It's also why HAGS is often recommended as a "just turn it on" setting. For modern GPUs paired with capable CPUs, it 

definitely

 does more good than harm, which is why it's easy to see why so many people leave it enabled and never look back.

There's a hidden VRAM cost to this, however

HAGS commits a part of your GPU's memory

What isn't talked about enough, however, is the cost of hardware-accelerated GPU scheduling. When HAGS is enabled, the GPU reserves a chunk of its memory for scheduling tasks. Now, on higher-end cards with 

plenty of VRAM

https://www.xda-developers.com/i-dont-care-about-rtx-5060-9gb/

, this overhead proves to be rather negligible. On older cards, however, it can quietly eat into your available memory in a very real way.

✕ Remove Ads

https://www.xda-developers.com/premium/?ref=ad_removal

On my partner's PC with an RTX 2070 Super, I consistently saw close to a gigabyte of VRAM being committed when HAGS was enabled. That's a full gigabyte that could be the difference between staying within the VRAM budget while gaming or slamming straight into the ceiling. Once you do hit the VRAM ceiling while gaming, the penalties show up immediately, bringing along texture streaming issues, stuttering, and inconsistent frametimes.

Modern games just aren't forgiving with VRAM. Titles like 

Cyberpunk 2077

 and 

Hogwarts Legacy

 demand all the memory a GPU has, and even slightly lighter games can push limits when you crank up texture quality. While the GPU neared its 

VRAM limit in

https://www.xda-developers.com/stellar-blade-pc-port-benchmarks-and-optimization-1080p-1440p-4k/

 

Stellar Blade

 with 4K textures on and HAGS turned on, I was able to use them only with HAGS turned off, as the VRAM usage meter went from red to yellow with HAGS out of the picture. When you're already working with 8GB, losing nearly a gigabyte to scheduling overhead feels like handing away performance for free.

HAGS might not directly tank gaming performance on a PC, but it does reduce the headroom the GPU has and needs in VRAM-heavy scenarios. On older hardware with limited-VRAM GPUs, that's a trade-off that becomes impossible to ignore or tolerate.

Turning it off made perfect sense on this build

My partner's system is a very specific kind of bottlenecked: an RTX 2070 Super paired with a Ryzen 5 1600. It's not a bad combo by any means, but it's definitely showing its age (seriously, the DRAM pricing crash can't come soon enough). The instinct, then, would be to keep HAGS on to reduce CPU overhead, but in practice, 

VRAM was the limiting factor here

https://www.xda-developers.com/nvidias-vram-compression-neural-texture-compression-could-renew-8gb-gpus/

. So I turned hardware-accelerated GPU scheduling off.

The result was immediate and surprisingly clean. Nearly 950MB of VRAM was freed up, and suddenly, borderline scenarios stopped tipping over. Cyberpunk held onto its textures without choking, and Stellar Blade stopped stuttering, delivering even more consistency under pressure.

When it came to the FPS counter, the framerate barely moved. Sure, there was a loss of 3–5 average FPS depending on the game I ran, but upscaling technologies still worked exactly as expected. Since 

the RTX 2070 Super

https://www.xda-developers.com/i-benchmarked-an-rtx-20-series-gpu-with-dlss-45-heres-what-i-found/

 doesn't support frame generation anyway, I wasn't giving up any critical features. That's right — frame generation on any card — AMD or Nvidia — will not work if you have HAGS turned off. So, if you're on an RTX 40-series or RTX 50-series GPU, or even AMD's RX 5000, 6000, 7000, or 9000 series that support frame generation, you have no option but to keep HAGS turned on for better frame rates 

using AI frame generation

https://www.xda-developers.com/best-gaming-upgrade-in-2026-is-ai-upscaling-and-frame-generation/

.

Regular AI upscaling, however, doesn't require the CPU, so your DLSS or FSR upscalers will work perfectly fine even without hardware-accelerated GPU scheduling. It just made sense. After all, on this kind of hardware, reclaiming VRAM mattered more than shaving off a few milliseconds of scheduling latency.

Should you disable HAGS as well?

HAGS might work brilliantly on one machine and hold another back.

This is absolutely not a blanket recommendation to turn off HAGS. In fact, far from it. If you're using frame generation, for starters, you simply must have it enabled. Plus, on newer GPUs with ample VRAM, the trade-off simply doesn't exist in any meaningful way. But if you're using a 6GB or 8GB graphics card, and you're constantly brushing up against VRAM limits, it's worth questioning the default advice, because for you, HAGS isn't quite "free."

With HAGS disabled on my 8GB VRAM GPU-powered PC, the system still felt good. Sure, the frame counter dipped slightly in certain scenes and areas, but frametime consistency improved overall. The experience felt steadier and less prone to sudden hiccups in the newer AAA titles that I occasionally ran to test them with aggressive upscaling.

✕ Remove Ads

There's no universal "best configuration", but just a series of trade-offs shaped by your exact hardware. HAGS might work brilliantly on one machine and hold another back. On older GPUs, though, I can't see myself ever agreeing to trade almost a full gigabyte of VRAM for slightly better FPS by turning on HAGS, which is why I wouldn't recommend it at all.
