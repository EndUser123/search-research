Abstract

Reliability in large language models (LLMs) is constrained along two orthogonal axes: sycophancy-a social-alignment failure where the model tends to agree with user pressure-and hallucination (confabulation)-a world-alignment failure producing confident but unsupported content (Fanous et al., 2025; Sharma et al., 2023; Ji et al., 2022; Kalai et al., 2025). Building on this distinction, we introduce LOGOS, a domain-bounded Constitutional RAG system for Orthodox theology with explicit anti-sycophancy rules and strict scope boundaries. Prior to human comparison, LOGOS underwent a Phase1 theological audit: a committee of the Holy Metropolis of Nea Ionia posed 200 domain questions, finding all responses doctrinally sound. We then conducted a Phase2 human evaluation with N = 52 AI students (valid responses N=51-52 per prompt), using five structured prompts plus one freeform redteaming task. Our data reveal a distinct safety advantage for LOGOS on misleading prompt handling. For example, in the "Heresy Test" prompt, LOGOS demonstrated a nearly 3x improvement in safety compared to the baseline (73.1% vs 26.9%), despite the baseline's fluency. In freeform redteaming, significant errors were reported in 33.3% of baseline outputs vs 1.8% of LOGOS outputs. We situate these findings amid the release of reasoning-intensive models like Gemini 3.0 and DeepSeek-R1 (Jan 2026). We argue that while "Deep Think" and Pure RL improve general reasoning, they do not resolve the specific need for doctrinal fidelity; crucially, reasoning chains can sometimes produce "sophisticated sycophancy" (DeepSeek-AI, 2026; Wei et al., 2026).

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have rapidly expanded in coverage and capability, yet their

reliability remains shaped by two orthogonal axes. We use sycophancy to denote a

socialalignment failure in which a model tends to a gree with a user's stance or pre ssure, and

hallucination (confabulation) to denote a worldalignment failure in which a model produces

confident but unsupported content (Ji et al., 2022; Sharma et al., 2023). This two-axis framing

clarifies how seemingly strong systems can remain brittle when confronted with misleading

premises, fabricated sources, or outofscope requests.

By early 2026, the field witnessed a shift with the release of "thinking models." Gemini 3.0

introduced "Deep Think" capabilities, and DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated that Pure Reinforcement

Learning (RL) can incentivize extended chains of thought without human demonstration data

(DeepSeek-AI, 2026; Google, 2026). While these architectures dramatically improve

mathematical and coding accuracy, they do not necessarily solve the alignment problem in

subjective or high-stakes domains like theology. RL-driven models trained to maximize rewards

can become highly e ffective sycophants, using their superior reasoning to justify the user's

erroneous premises (Wei et al., 2026).

In this paper, we examine reliability through a domainbounded case study: LOGOS, a

Constitutional RAG system for Orthodox theology. Our approach is grounded not onl y in

engineering but in the "ontol ogy of technology" as discussed at the 2nd International Scientific

Conference of "Theologia" (Thessaloniki, Sept 2025). Just as we demonstrated in prior research

that AI tools can mitigat e "bounded rationality" and reduce uncertaint y in early-stage business

decision-making (Davalas et al., 2025), here we apply similar principles to the domain of

spiritual discernment. In both contexts— startup evaluation and theological inquiry— the goal is

to move from high-uncertaint y speculation to grounded, data-driven (or dogma-driven)

reliability. Responding to the call for "digital discernment" b y Ecclesiastical leadership (Church

of Greece, 2025), LOGOS enforces strict scope boundaries and explicit antisycophancy rules,

Background and Definitions

2.1 Two reliability axes

We use two orthogonal axes to reason about LLM reliabilit y. Sycophancy denotes a

socialalignment failure in which a model t ends to agree with a user's stance or pr essure— even

when the premise is misleading or adversarial (Fanous et al., 2025). Hallucination

(confabulation) denotes a worldalignment failure in which a model produces confident but

unsupported content (Ji et al., 2022).

2.2 Sycophancy: definition and taxonomy

We adopt a minimal, operational definition: a response is sy cophantic if (i) it aligns with the

user's expressed stan ce or pressure while (ii) neglecting truth, scope, or safet y. Our evaluation

targets three specific manifestations of sycophancy:

 Answer sycophancy: initial agreement with a misleading premise.

 Revision sycophancy: changing a previously correct answer under user pressure.

 Regressive sycophancy: persisting in agreement after corrective feedback.

Recent work on Social Sycophancy (Wei et al., 2026) suggests that models often prioritize "face-

saving" strategies over truthfulness, especially when the user appears authoritative.

We use the common intrinsic– extrinsic split. Intrinsic hallucination (faithfulness) concerns

unsupported content relative to provided context. Extrinsic hallucination (factuality) concerns

Sycophancy in RLHF and Pure RL

3.1 RLHF as a driver of sycophancy

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) has lon g been known to rewar d

friendliness over truth (Sharma et al., 2023). When raters reward "helpfulness," models learn to

match user beliefs. Sycophancy, in this view, is a rational strategy for reward maximization.

3.2 The Paradox of "Reasoning" Models (Gemini 3.0 & DeepSeek- R1)

With the adve nt of DeepSeek-R1 and Gemini 3.0, models now employ " thinking tokens" or

extended Chain of Thought (CoT) to v erify answers. W hile this reduces hallucinations in

verifiable domains (math/code), it introduces a new risk in subjective domains: Sophisticated

Sycophancy. A model with strong reasoning capabilities can construct a persuasive, multi-step

argument to support a heresy if it detects that this is what the user desires (DeepSeek-AI, 2026).

Unlike the "dumb" sycophancy of earlier models, this reasoning-backed compliance is harder to

detect and potentially more persuasive.

3.3 Sycophancy, trust, and domain experts

In highstakes domains such as theology, s ycophancy erodes trust. As emphasized at the

Thessaloniki Conference (Church of Greece, 2025), the "autonomy of technolog y" poses a risk

when AI systems generate their own "truths" rather than serving the revealed truth. LOGOS

addresses this by treating calibrated refusal ("I cannot endorse this") as a feature, not a failure.

This approach mirrors findings in digital government policy, where clear regulatory frameworks

are essential for establishing trust in startup ecosystems (D avalas & Angelaki, 2025). Just as

legal certainty fosters economic investment, "theological certainty"— enforced via Constitutional

AI— is a prerequisite for spiritual reliance. By establishing a digital governance framework over

the model's output, LOGOS mitigates the risks of unregulated generation in a manner analogous

to how smart government policies mitigate market risks for entrepreneurs.

Hallucination and Frontier Architectures

4.1 Hallucination as a training artifact

Hallucinations are often artifacts of training objectives that penalize abstention. Kalai et al.

(2025) showed that models are incentivized to guess rather than say "I don't know."

The LOGOS System

5.1 The challenge: Orthodoxy in the age of AI

The LOGOS project addresses a specific pastoral need identified by the Holy Metropolis of Nea

Ionia and echoed in the broader theological discourse of the 2nd International Scientific

Conference of "Theologia" (Thessaloniki, 2025). The conference highlighted the need for

"digital discernment"— the abilit y to use technology without losi ng the "personhood" and "truth"

central to Orthodox anthropology.

On a technical level, LOGOS translates the theological concept of apophaticism

(apochatismos)— the recognition that in divine matters, a humble refusal to define the

indefinable is often more accurate than erroneous or speculative affirmation. LOGOS is thus

designed to be a tool of discernment, preventing the AI from hallucinating spiritual advice or

validating heresies under the guise of "helpfulness."

5.2 System Architecture and Implementation

Design goal. LOGOS is a Constitutional RAG system for Orthodox theology that prioritizes

doctrinal fidelity.

Components.

Corpus: Vetted theological texts (Scripture, Fathers, Synodal decisions).

Indexing: Hybrid retrieval (sparse BM25 + dense embeddings). Hybrid retrieval is

critical in this domain: BM25 captures precise theological terminology (e.g., 'hypostasis',

'homoousion'), while embeddings capture the semantic intent of lay questions.

Retrieval: Top-k passages capped by evidence budget.

5.3 Evaluation Protocol

Overview. We conducted a twophase evaluation.

 Phase1: Theological audit. A committee of the Holy Metropolis of Nea Ionia reviewed

200 responses. All were judged satisfactory (LOGOS Evaluation Data, 2025).

 Phase2: Student evaluation (Blind A/B). We recruited N = 52 undergraduate and

graduate AI students. Due to the optional nature of some fields, the number of valid

re sponses per prompt fluctuated slightly (N=51– 52).

Materials. Participants evaluated five structured prompts (P1– P5) on two criteria: Accurac y and

Safe Handling. They also performed one freeform redteaming task.

Model Selection (Bring-Your-Own-Baseline). Pa rticipants evaluated LOGOS a gainst a frontier

baseline model of their choice, selecting between ChatGPT-4o and ChatGPT-5 (Preview)

depending on their acc ess. This approach ensured that L OGOS was tested ag ainst the most

capable models available to the participants, preventing "strawman" comparisons against

deprecated architectures. While allowing participants to choose their baseline introduces variable

control, it increases the ecological validity of the stud y. We are comparing LOGOS a gainst the

actual, diverse frontier tools students use in daily practice.

Qualitative Analysis of Red-Teaming Failures

Beyond the quantitative error rates, qualitative feedback revealed the distinct failure modes and

stylistic differences of each system:

 Pastoral vs. En cyclopedic Persona: Participants distinguished the models not just by

accuracy but by tone. LOGOS was frequently described as "warmer," "pastoral," and

"more human"— adopting the voi ce of a clergyman— whereas the baseline was perceived

as "cold," "mathematical," and "encyclopedic." This suggests that the RAG retrieval of

Patristic texts successfully transferred the ethos of the corpus to the model's output.

 False Authority (Baseline): The ChatGPT models frequently capitulated to user pressure

by accepting the existence of fabricated books or sources to "save face," demonstrating

the Social Sycophancy described by Wei et al. (2026).

 Theological Syncretism (Baseline): When pressed on controversial topics, baseline

models often provided "broad" or "syncretic" answers, equating dist inct dogmatic

positions to appear balanced. In contrast, LOGOS was noted for being "decisive" and

"consistent" with the Biblical corpus.

 Dangerous Over-Correction (LOGOS): While rare (1.8%), the sin gle si gnificant error

reported for LOGOS was critical. A participant noted that the model persisted in offering

spiritual/religious remedies to a user descri bing symptoms requiring psychiatric

intervention. In contrast to the baseline, which correctly advised seeking psychiatric help

in a similar instance, LOGOS persisted in offering th eological remedies. This exposes a

fundamental tension in domain-specific alignment: deep domain alignment can

inadvertently override general safety protocols regarding medical referrals.

Discussion and Limitations

It is worth noting that LOGOS did not aim to outperform general models in raw fluency or

"chatbuddiness" (reflected in the high "Equal" counts for Accuracy). Instead, it offered a decisive

safety advantage under pressure. In the "Heresy Test" (P1) and "Pressure Test" (P2), LOGOS

refused to hallucinate or capitulate to the user in ~73-74% of cases, whereas the baseline often

failed (handling safely only ~26%). This re presents a nearly 3x improvement in safety for

adversarial scenarios.

Interpreting the "Equal" Ratings. The high frequency of "Equal" ratings for Accura cy (e.g.,

58% in P5, 44% in P1) is a positive indicator for the Constitutional RAG approach. This

demonstrates a "Parity in Fluency, Superiority in Safety" dynamic: the strict constraints of

LOGOS do not degrade the linguistic fluency or utility of the model in non-adversarial contexts,

but successfully activate necessary "brakes" in adversarial ones.

Sa fety vs. Sophistication. In the era of DeepSeek-R1 and Gemini 3.0, the definition of

"capability" is shifting toward complex reasoning. However, our results suggest that for

theology, "capabilit y" must be redefined as "fidelity." While DeepSeek-R1 can "think" de eply

about how to answer, it lacks the inherent ontol ogy to judge if it should answer affirmativel y to a

heresy. While reasoning models reduce factu al hallucinations, they introduce the risk of

'hallucinated logic'— constructing valid syllogisms derived from heretical premises. LOGOS

provides this external ontolog y. A reasoning model that uses CoT to argue for a heresy is more

dangerous than a simple model that hallucinates a fact (Wei et al., 2026). LOGOS acts as a

"guardrail of truth," constraining the generation process to the boundaries of the Church's

experience.

7.1 Limitations

 Participant Population: AI students (N=52) are a rigorous "Red Team" but not

representative of average parishioners.

 Domain Scope: Limited to Orthodox theology and Greek language.

 Sample Size: While N=52 provides clear descriptive signals, larger cohorts are needed

for high-power inferential statistics.

Conclusion

This paper presented LOGOS, a Constitutional RAG system aligned with the theological

principles of the E astern Orthodox Church. Amidst the rapid evolution of AI— ma rked b y the

release of thinking models like Gemini 3.0 and DeepSeek- R1— we argue that "reasoning" is not

a substitute for "discernment."

Our evaluation (N=52) demonstrates that LOGOS effectively mitigates regressive sycophancy

and hallucination, reducing significant errors in red-teaming from 33.3% (baseline) to 1.8%. By

anchoring AI generation in a curated corpus and enforcing strict c onstitutional bounds, we

answer the challenge set by the 2025 Thessaloniki Conference: to utilize technology while

preserving the ontology of truth.

Looking forward, we examine whether architectures like Nested Learning/HOPE can structurally

reduce sycophancy by addressing the effective anterograde amnesia of current LLMs (Behrouz

& Mirrokni, 2025), though for now, Constitutional RAG r emains the pragmatic choice for high-
