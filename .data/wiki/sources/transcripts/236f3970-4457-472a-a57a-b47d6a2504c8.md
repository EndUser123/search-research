NotebookLM is a powerful literature review assistant tool, allowing you to quickly get a shallow understanding from a large collection of papers and identify key concepts. However, it is also suitable as a long-term research partner because you can ask questions about all PDFs simultaneously, and the results refer to specific passages within these PDFs, enabling a very deep semantic search and an easy way of double-checking if the AI made any mistakes. This in-depth review evaluates the latest version of NotebookLM and how researchers can use it to accelerate the literature review.

What is NotebookLM and Why You Should Use It

Notebook LM is Google's tool to analyse large bodies of information presented as a collection of various PDFs, URLs, or YouTube videos. Free members can analyse up to 50 sources (500.000 words each), while pro members can upload up to 300. It provides additional tools to extract the information through: Audio and video overviews, mind maps, timelines and customised reports. This allows you to tackle any complex problem (like a literature review) through various angles. Surprisingly, the free version is often sufficient for students working on an assignment.

 

 

Importantly, every query overview or summary can be scoped to select all or only a few sources from your database. Let's look at the different types of summaries and information extraction capabilities Notebook LM offers within its “Studio” (top right box in the image).

Audio & Video overviews: Getting familiar with your field

When Notebook LM first came out in 2024, audio overviews would tend to startle people because of their lifelike podcast quality: Two hosts discuss highly complex academic topics in a nonchalant, conversational tone. In my opinion, the downsides of both overviews are that they can be somewhat unfocused for academic research, missing specific topics that might be relevant or niche-related questions. Therefore, I find them primarily relevant if you want to get an overview of a topic that is entirely new to you. If you're a senior academic looking to find answers to complex, specific questions that may not even be the main topic of the papers you uploaded, you will not gain much from the overviews. However, even then, just generating this overview and listening to it while you go to the gym or walk your dog is a great way to catch up on a few ideas without a large time commitment.

Timeline: Identify Developments & Prominent Authors

The timeline analyses your sources and the content within to create a timeline of how knowledge developed over the years. This can be particularly useful for individuals studying philosophy, history, or politics, or for life scientists writing a comprehensive synthesis on the evolution of questions over extended periods. In my example here, the timeline begins with a paper published in 1903, a time when the term “ecology” was not yet widely used.

 

 

At the bottom of your timeline, you will find a cast of characters which outlines the main authors who wrote the papers in your source collection and the people mentioned in these papers. Particularly, that feature makes it apparent how useful this can be when analysing historical or political information, or working on novels, interview transcripts and so on.

Reports: Customisable Information Extraction

Offer you a way to provide a custom prompt to generate a synthesis on a question that you have regarding all (or some) of your sources. For instance, if you have a paper collection on climate change in general consisting of hundreds of papers, you might want to get a report on exactly how elevated CO2 levels influence plant growth, or you could query the same collection of papers and ask about how the climate is going to change in the next hundred years. Generating multiple reports on the questions you are interested in can help you find an answer to your question as well as identify the key papers that are related to this question for further study.

 

 

In the next section, I have combined several notebook LEM prompts that may be useful when working on your literature review.

Top 5 NotebookLM prompts for your literature review

Don't think of Notebook LM as simply an AI that summarises PDFs. It can do much more than that and help you find logical contradictions, gaps, and trends in your research field. Keep in mind that, while AI is surprisingly good at synthesis, it may not always provide complete answers and they might not always be correct. This is why it is essential always to verify the sources that Notebook LM cites in its reply and double-check using your own judgment.

1. The “Gap Hunter” Prompt

When you're new to a topic, knowing what is already known and what is at the forefront of academic research can be a challenge. A clearly identified research gap can help you make your paper more impactful and relevant. Here is the prompt:

Prompt:

 

You are an expert research mentor specializing in [discipline X]. From the following sources, identify at least three categories of research gaps: (a) methodological, (b) theoretical, and (c) empirical/contextual. For each category, write: (1) a one-sentence description of the gap, (2) an example paper or finding that highlights the gap, and (3) a suggested research question. Present as a table with citations.

2. The “Methodology Deep Dive” Prompt

When discussing limitations over fields and previous shortcomings, it is important to know what has been done insufficiently and point that clearly out in your introduction and discussion sections. This prompt can help:

Prompt:

 

Analyze the methodological weaknesses and recurring challenges in the field of [discipline/topic X]. From the provided sources, extract 4–6 common issues (e.g., small sample sizes, weak controls, measurement biases, reproducibility concerns). For each issue, explain (1) how it appears in multiple papers, (2) why it matters for validity or generalizability, and (3) what solutions or best practices could address it. Conclude with a short synthesis: which weaknesses most seriously limit progress in [topic X], and how future research could overcome them.

3. The “Recurring Topics Extractor” Prompt

When diving into a new field of research, it is essential to understand the single concepts that researchers are studying and how they relate to one another. Each idea can later become a note in your note-taking vault, but identifying them can be time-consuming, especially for young career researchers. This prompt can help you determine the key topics right away and streamline your note-taking and reading process:

Prompt:

 

From the provided papers on [topic X], identify the 5–10 most recurring themes, concepts, or research questions. For each theme, provide: (1) a short definition in your own words, (2) which papers mention it (with citations), and (3) a one-sentence summary of how it is treated (e.g., debated, assumed, tested). Present the output as a table or structured bullet list I can use as a basis for targeted note-taking.

4. The “Trend Detection” Prompt

When writing a literature review, it is incredibly useful to understand how the field has developed in recent years, which allows you to place your research more clearly and will help polish the introduction, making it more impactful and your paper more relevant. Try out this prompt:

Prompt:

 

Analyze the provided collection of papers on [topic X]. Identify 5–7 major trends over time (e.g., rising methods, shifting focus areas, new theoretical debates). For each trend, provide: (1) a short description, (2) the approximate timeframe when it became more frequent, (3) example papers or citations, and (4) whether the trend appears to be increasing, stable, or declining. Conclude with a short synthesis of what these trends suggest for the future of research in [topic X]. Present results as a timeline-style bullet list.

5. The “Contradiction Finder” Prompt

A hallmark of a good literature review is identifying where insights lead to contradictions. For instance, we know that elevated carbon dioxide levels, related to climate change, will decrease biodiversity because species struggle with the hotter climate. However, there are other species that are well-adapted to hotter temperatures and may benefit from the elevated CO2 levels. This forms a contradiction that, at least in ecology, is at the moment an open question. A literature review focusing on this contradiction would be a meaningful and impactful research paper relevant to a global audience and, therefore, publishable in a prestigious journal. Here's a prompt that can help.

Prompt:

 

From the provided papers on [topic X], identify major contradictions or conflicting findings. For each contradiction, provide: (1) the specific claim or result from each side (quoted or paraphrased with citations), (2) possible reasons for the disagreement (e.g., differences in method, sample, context, or assumptions), and (3) a short note on what kind of further evidence would help resolve the conflict. Present as a structured bullet list or a two-column table contrasting the opposing findings.

NotebookLM vs Gemini

Notebook.lm runs on Gemini, which means that if you have a Gemini subscription, you also get a Notebook.lm subscription. While Gemini is a multi-purpose chat tool, developed by Google, Notebook.lm specialises in extracting information from a large collection of various sources. Gemini, on the other hand, has capabilities like internet search or deep search, allowing you to find information rather than process it. Notebook LM is also not optimised for academic writing, while Google's Gemini, like ChatGPT, can be 

customised to create excellent academic writing

. In fact, the combination of Gemini and the PaperPal Reference Manager can create a compelling workflow that I explain in the Effortless Publishing course:

ChatGPT (Projects) vs NotebookLM

ChatGPT projects, like Notebook LM, allow you to upload multiple files that the AI will work on to generate a synthesis and query these papers. However, as ChatGPT is a multi-purpose tool, you don't have specific features like deep citations, mind map generations, or timelines. On the other hand, ChatGPT is better at academic writing, has a memory allowing it to refer to past conversations, and can be customised with instructions to provide answers in a more succinct, opinionated, or otherwise different way.

 

 

Summary: Is Notebook LM worth it for literature reviews?

NotebookLM is a great partner to get started on any literature review because you only need to provide a few papers to start extracting information with various pre-built tools, like a mind map, a timeline generation, or customised reports. It provides references that point directly to passages within PDFs, allowing you to double-check the AI output. Its main weakness is that it cannot search for other papers that might be relevant to your lit review and, therefore, is primarily a tool for information extraction and synthesis that you will need to supplement with tools like Litmaps or Consensus for finding relevant papers. I explain more about these tools and the 

Effortless Literature Review course

https://effortlessacademic.com/lit-review/

.

Some of the features that Notebook.LM offers (such as an audio or video overview) might be more flashy than useful for an experienced academic, but can at least be entertaining for young career researchers or people who are entirely unfamiliar with the topic they're about to study.

NotebookLM can also be an excellent partner during the synthesis stage of your literature review, as it allows you to ask very specific, nuanced questions to a subset of your papers, providing a flexible approach to information extraction and synthesis.

Notebook LM offers a generous set of features, even in the free version. However, with a Pro subscription, you also gain access to Google's Gemini (as an alternative to ChatGPT), extended storage in your Google Drive, and many other benefits, making it an attractive package deal for academics.
