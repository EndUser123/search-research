# You NEED to STOP Using Windows 11 Right Now
We’ve been told for decades that Microsoft Windows 
is unavoidable. That without Big Tech operating  

systems, modern society simply stops working.
France proved that is a lie. 

One of the world’s most powerful nations removed 
Microsoft from its national police force. Tens  

of thousands of computers. No Windows. No 
licenses. No dependency on Silicon Valley. 

Instead, they switched to free open-source 
software. And the result stunned even  

government officials. Costs dropped by 40%.
This wasn’t a small experiment. It was a mass  

corporate eviction. And it exposed 
something Big Tech never expected… 

Europe may have found a way out of the monopoly.
CH 1 — THE 40% GUILLOTINE 

The Gendarmerie Nationale is not some 
small office experiment, it’s part of  

the two pillars of French law enforcement. So 
when France began removing Windows across the  

entire department… people noticed.
Today, more than 103,000 of those  

machines now run a custom Linux build. That’s 
97% of the entire desktop fleet. The remaining  

3% are on specialized terminals tied to old 
legacy contracts. As those contracts expire,  

the last Windows machines disappear with them.
The new Gendarmerie machines ship with no  

operating system at all. No OEM Windows license 
sits in the purchase price. No automatic  

activation handshake reaches Redmond. The hardware 
arrives completely bare. From there, French  

government IT teams build the machines themselves 
then send it out to officers in the field. 

That means there’s no Microsoft tax linked to 
the machines. Every workstation now represents  

a single, deliberate decision. And that decision 
gets repeated numerous times across the fleet.  

Each one quietly removes a US software vendor 
from the cost base of a national institution. 

Individually, it feels small. Almost invisible.
But at this scale, it adds up. Fast. 

The clean was just as careful. Specialized apps 
got rewritten to run on the new computers. IT  

teams ran parallel deployments to ensure downtime 
stayed at zero during the switch. The interface  

stayed familiar on purpose, matching what officers 
had used for years. The learning curve never  

became an excuse to slow things down.
One line item, one decision,  

had just saved millions.
$2.2 million gets cut from the  

licensing budget every single year. That fee 
used to come out of French taxpayer wallets,  

straight into Microsoft's profit column. 
Year after year with no foreseeable end date. 

Except that changed.
The running total got pulled  

from one of the most reliable revenue streams 
in Europe. Procurement officers inside the  

force describe it as a structural cut. Despite 
Microsoft’s desperate attempts at negotiating,  

the only option was walking away.
A 40% drop in total cost of ownership  

is the number that really matters. This covers 
everything from licensing and support contracts  

to training and the costs of running 
US software inside government walls. 

The force runs cheaper now and the vendor sits 
entirely outside the chain. Internal reports  

point to no real performance hit, no measurable 
downtime, and zero productivity drop. In some  

cases, numbers actually improved.
And Microsoft? 

They saw a recurring revenue stream from one of 
Europe's most security-sensitive institutions  

evaporate. No last-minute discount package 
showed up. No senior account manager flew in  

from Seattle. By the time the eviction was visible 
from outside, the internal teams already owned the  

operating system. They owned the patch pipeline. 
They owned the deployment toolchain end to end. 

That makes the exit structural 
rather than cyclical. 

Even if a future French administration wanted to 
walk it back, the institutional muscle now lives  

entirely inside the Gendarmerie. Every dollar 
that used to fund Silicon Valley shareholder  

returns now stays inside French borders. 
The lesson Microsoft seems to have absorbed  

is uncomfortable. France did not ask for a 
better deal, and that was the whole problem. 

But money turned out to be only half the story.
CH 2 — THE TELEMETRY BLACK HOLE 

The bigger reason was a data hemorrhage 
that almost nobody talks about openly. 

Modern Windows includes built-in telemetry that 
collects device diagnostics, crash reports and  

app usage data, which can be sent back to 
Microsoft’s servers. It’s mainly used for  

stability and security. Microsoft documents these 
systems in its privacy materials, and parts of  

the data flow attract a lot of attention.
A portion of this telemetry is baked into  

the operating system, and while 
users can reduce what’s shared,  

it can’t be fully disabled in standard 
configurations. You aren't crazy for feeling  

like your computer is spying on you and forcing 
upgrades. It is a documented business model. 

For everyday users it’s mostly background noise. 
But for organisations handling sensitive data,  

it can be seen as something closer 
to a live intelligence breach. 

Their working assumption is that any data leaving 
the device is no longer under government control.  

Even if content never leaves the box, the 
metadata trail and the activity patterns do. 

That can be enough.
It can reveal operational frequency,  

investigative priorities and the details of 
sensitive operations. Pull all of that together  

across 103,000 endpoints and you end up with a 
continuously updating picture of how a G7 nation  

polices itself. All refreshing in real time.
Once the data leaves national infrastructure,  

recall is effectively impossible.
It gets worse. A US law  

quietly overrides France's own.
The CLOUD Act passed in 2018 gives  

American authorities legal mechanisms to force 
US-based cloud providers to hand over server data  

on demand. The physical location of those servers, 
anywhere in the world, doesn't change that.  

For French intelligence services, that isn't a 
hypothetical concern. It is written law in another  

country that overrides their own. No vendor 
relationship or contract clause can amend it. 

Plugging that corporate data leak was 
the second motive behind the migration.  

The custom Linux build used by the Gendarmerie 
doesn’t automatically send usage or diagnostic  

information out to outside companies. Any 
diagnostics, logs, or crash information  

are kept inside national infrastructure run 
by French government engineers. The idea is  

simple. No operational data leaves the country, 
and audit checks are used to confirm that. 

That kind of enforcement has shifted 
how these systems are viewed. 

Suspicion of your own workstation is no longer 
a fringe concern held by privacy specialists.  

It is becoming a working assumption 
behind European digital policy in 2026.  

Public-sector procurement contracts across 
the continent are starting to reflect that. 

CH 3 — THE HARDWARE RESCUE
Microsoft's most profitable  

trick has a fairly clinical name…
Forced hardware obsolescence. 

Windows 11 ships with a hardware compatibility 
wall built directly into its installation  

requirements. Machines need a Trusted 
Platform Module 2.0 chip. They need a  

specific generation of approved CPU, a minimum 
amount of RAM, and Secure Boot capability. 

Computers that fail any single 
check get blocked from upgrading. 

The deadline that turned this into a global issue 
was Windows 10 reaching end of support in October  

2025. At that point, hundreds of millions of 
fully functioning machines around the world  

were effectively pushed toward replacement or 
upgrade. Not because the hardware had failed,  

but because one vendor’s product 
roadmap had moved on according  

to a schedule set in Redmond, Washington.
The bill ran into hundreds of billions of dollars  

and thousands of tons of electronic waste.
Working desktops and fully capable laptops  

were suddenly destined for the landfill or 
fire-sale liquidation. Corporate IT departments,  

school districts, hospital networks, and city 
governments all faced the same uncomfortable  

choice. Keep running unsupported software 
with mounting security risk or write huge  

capital expenditure checks for new hardware. 
The refresh cycle stopped being a technical  

question and turned into a budget event.
But France bypassed the upgrade cycle entirely. 

The custom Linux build doesn’t impose strict 
hardware cutoffs. That’s why older equipment  

is still treated as a fully usable police 
terminal. Under a different software roadmap,  

the same machine might already be marked 
for replacement and written off. Over time,  

that decision reduces replacement costs, 
and the savings build up each year. 

Existing machines stay in use longer instead 
of being replaced on a fixed schedule every 5  

to 7 years. The money that would have gone 
into new equipment is instead retained in  

the public budget and redirected toward things 
like staffing, training, and field operations. 

And that brings the 40% saving back into focus.
It was never just about licensing. That  

cost is only the most visible part.
The less obvious expenses come from the  

cycle around it: constant hardware refreshes, 
procurement contracts, retraining users,  

managing disposal and depreciation schedules. 
Over time, those pieces lock governments into  

upgrade timelines whether they need them or not.
The environmental impact follows from that,  

even if it wasn’t the primary goal. Fewer 
replacements mean fewer transport emissions  

and less electronic waste. France didn’t adopt the 
approach for environmental messaging, but reduced  

turnover naturally lowers the footprint.
Look closely and the e-waste cycle  

starts to look like a sales tactic.
The compatibility rules are a big part of  

what keeps the cycle going. In practice, they tend 
to encourage regular upgrades to newer hardware on  

Microsoft’s schedule. The way around that isn’t 
theoretical or confined to planning documents.  

It’s already being done inside a major G7 
country’s police force using systems that run on  

equipment Microsoft no longer officially supports.
Now, other countries are noticing. 

CH 4 — THE SOVEREIGN CONTAGION
Schleswig-Holstein is the most  

northern state in Germany. On paper, it's 
a pretty quiet place to kick off a software  

revolt. That's part of the reason most 
people outside the tech press missed it. 

The state just kicked off the biggest 
Microsoft eviction Germany has ever  

seen. They're going after 30,000 government 
computers used by civil servants, judges,  

and state police. The plan is to rip out the whole 
Microsoft setup. What goes in its place is a stack  

of open Linux tools. LibreOffice handles the 
office work, open-source email and calendar  

systems take the rest. Their Nextcloud platforms 
run on servers that sit inside Germany itself. 

The state's digital affairs ministry 
called the project a long-term safety  

net. A way to dodge future licensing 
fights and head off future data leaks. 

A way to stay ahead of 
European data protection law. 

Migrating tens of thousands of users is 
not cheap. The state parliament allocated  

roughly €30 million to cover the grueling 
multi-year transition. But once it’s complete,  

the state expects to save €16.5 million a year on 
licensing. That’s money that’s going back into the  

budget rather than Microsoft's European books.
The rollout is structured in waves. The first  

covers general administrative workstations, 
the second focuses on specialised regional  

departments, and the third extends to more complex 
municipal systems. Each phase includes a 90-day  

stabilisation period, during which issues are 
logged and engineers refine the deployment  

image before moving on to the next group.
The political durability is what makes the  

project strategically important. In places like 
Schleswig-Holstein, the shift has been framed less  

as a short-term initiative and more as long-term 
digital independence. Future governments could,  

in theory, change direction, but doing so 
would mean reintroducing significant recurring  

costs and rebuilding systems already 
integrated into day-to-day operations. 

The pattern these two cases set up 
is getting harder to argue with. 

A nuclear-armed G7 country runs its top police 
force without Windows. A German state with 30,000  

government computers follows the exact same path.
The corporate excuse used to be that no  

real alternative existed. That 
excuse has just about collapsed. 

But the lost license money 
isn’t what scares Big Tech. 

It's the idea every Silicon Valley sales pitch 
is built on. That there’s no real alternative. 

Once that crack shows up in public, backed by 
hard numbers, the power dynamic flips. Every  

American software company feels it. A French 
ministry sitting across from a Microsoft rep  

in 2026 already has internal numbers in hand. 
Those numbers show exactly what life looks like  

without the contract. They have proof they can 
run the whole operation without the product. 

Negotiation dynamics flip overnight. The vendor 
used to dictate terms while the customer asked for  

discounts. Now the customer arrives with a roadmap 
to leave. Pricing power moves across the table. 

The knock-on effect on European 
software is already showing up. 

European companies that sell office software 
say their government calls have jumped sharply.  

Same goes for the teams behind chat tools and 
login systems. Open-source groups that build  

free office software are getting real funding 
now. That money comes from ministries that used  

to give them nothing. European cloud companies 
are winning deals across the board. Those same  

deals would have gone straight to American 
giants 2 years ago. The money France and  

Schleswig-Holstein pulled out of Microsoft 
isn't just sitting in a bank. It's getting  

plowed back into European tech firms who until 
recently, couldn't even get a seat at the table. 

Now, other European capitals are watching closely.
The Netherlands. Italy. Spain. They are  

all looking to review their current 
system. The pattern keeps spreading.  

Each one adds another data point to the curve.
This eventually matters for your laptop too. 

The personal-scale lesson hidden inside the 
operation is straightforward enough. The same  

open-source software that runs a national police 
force is freely available. Anyone with a laptop  

and an afternoon can install it. The same 
telemetry resistance that protects French  

intelligence operations protects an ordinary 
household. The hardware lifecycle extension  

that saved France hundreds of millions saves a 
single user the cost of a brand-new computer. 

The blueprint scales down 
as cleanly as it scales up. 

Pull all of this together and the full shape 
of what France pulled off becomes visible. 

A financial play. A security operation. A 
hardware policy. All executed at once through  

a single procurement decision. The 40% cost 
reduction is the headline number. Underneath  

it sit other gains. Intelligence integrity. 
Extended hardware life. Budget redirection. 

The whole move keeps reinforcing itself 
across multiple political cycles. 

The era of unavoidable software monopolies is 
cracking. France broke the corporate lock-in  

on a national scale. Every other European 
capital can copy it. The excuse for paying  

the Microsoft tax is gone.
It shows that Silicon  

Valley's nightmare isn't a competitor.
It's you, realizing you don't need them at all. 

But the real story isn’t in the announcements, 
it’s in the systems quietly being rewritten  

underneath them. And once you start following 
that thread, you end up asking different  

questions about what Windows has become.
