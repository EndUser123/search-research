At a Glance

Software engineering has transformed rapidly while hardware modeling remains a time-intensive process.

AI agents can construct physically consistent models from engineering intent specifications.

The Dyad platform treats models as structured objects for agent collaboration.

In the past few years, software engineering has undergone a rapid transformation. Artificial intelligence has moved from novelty to infrastructure. Tools like 

GitHub Copilot

https://github.com/features/copilot

, 

Cursor

https://cursor.com/

, and 

Claude Code

https://claude.ai/login

 have moved AI from autocomplete into something closer to a collaborator. Millions of software engineers now rely on AI systems to write boilerplate, refactor code, explore APIs, debug unfamiliar codebases, and even reason about architectural tradeoffs.

This shift was not driven by perfect automation. AI systems still make mistakes. They still require oversight. Yet the productivity gains are real, and more importantly, they have changed how software engineers allocate their time. Less effort is spent on boilerplate and syntax. More attention is directed toward system design, performance tradeoffs, and long-term maintainability.

What is striking is not that software engineering has changed, but how quickly it did.

In hardware engineering, across domains such as automotive systems, aerospace, HVAC, energy and battery systems, robotics, and industrial equipment, the contrast is stark. Despite rapid advances in computational power, simulation tools, and digital workflows, the core experience of building and validating physical system models has remained largely unchanged for decades. Engineers still spend weeks constructing models, coupling subsystems, debugging numerical issues, and verifying that results are physically meaningful. Exploration is constrained not by a lack of ideas, but by the cost of setup and validation.

The question is not whether AI can help. It is why the same agentic capabilities that transformed software have not yet taken hold in hardware.

At first glance, the explanation seems obvious. Software is abstract. Hardware is physical. Code can be compiled and tested instantly. Physical systems are governed by conservation laws, nonlinear dynamics, and coupled phenomena that do not tolerate approximation or guesswork.

This explanation is incomplete.

The deeper difference lies in how work is structured. Software engineering operates on artifacts that are symbolic, modular, and inherently executable. Code is both the specification and the implementation. Feedback loops are tight. Errors are often localized and reversible. Modern software development is explicitly organized around rapid iteration, fail fast cycles in which mistakes are expected, inexpensive, and informative.

Hardware engineering, by contrast, operates through layers of interpretation. Requirements must be translated into models. Models must be parameterized. Physics must be discretized. Assumptions must be made explicit or remain implicit. Validation is often indirect, relying on experience and judgment as much as formal checks. Crucially, many validation tasks require physical prototypes, bench testing, and system identification, introducing cost, time, and irreversibility that fundamentally shape engineering behavior.

hardware development

 is rarely optimized for throughput. It is structured to manage risk and complexity. The system engineering V reflects this reality: design decisions are front-loaded, verification is deliberate, and iteration is constrained. While elements of software-style iteration are slowly emerging, hardware engineering remains inherently conservative, not by choice, but by necessity.

Arguably, the most important work in hardware engineering happens before a single simulation is run. Deciding which physics matter, how subsystems should be coupled, and what level of fidelity is appropriate are all acts of reasoning that resist traditional automation. This is why most existing tools focus on execution rather than formulation. They assume the problem has already been correctly posed. In practice, problem formulation is iterative, uncertain, and deeply human.

Agentic AI

https://juliahub.com/videos/meet-the-dyad-agent-an-agentic-ai-for-model-based-engineering

 in software thrives because agents can act, observe outcomes, and revise their approach rapidly. In hardware, acting without understanding the structure of the system can produce results that look plausible but are physically wrong. Speed without rigor is worse than useless.

Any meaningful application of AI in hardware engineering must therefore operate at the level of models, constraints, and physical laws, not just at the level of numerical execution.

A familiar engineering reality

Consider the task of modeling a friction brake system for a new electric vehicle. The system involves mechanical torque generation, frictional contact, thermal energy generation, heat dissipation, and interaction with regenerative braking strategies.

In a traditional workflow, an engineer begins by defining geometry and parameters. Mechanical subsystems are constructed first, followed by 

thermal subsystems

https://www.designnews.com/materials/master-bond-launches-high-performance-thermal-epoxy-for-heat-sink-applications

. Equations governing torque, friction, heat generation, and heat transfer are implemented and discretized. The subsystems are then coupled, often manually, with careful attention to energy conservation, unit consistency, and boundary conditions.

Each step requires expertise and vigilance. A single unit mismatch or sign error can invalidate results. Worse, some errors do not cause obvious failures. They produce outputs that appear reasonable while violating basic physical principles. Engineers learn to distrust early results and to invest significant time in validation.

This setup phase can take weeks. This is because of what is often described as the “data safari.” Even when the governing physics are well understood and a complete model structure has been defined, engineering progress often stalls on the search for parameter values. Material properties, empirical coefficients, operating envelopes, legacy test results, and vendor specifications are scattered across datasheets, standards, reports, and institutional memory. Locating, validating, and reconciling these inputs can consume as much effort as building the model itself. An AI agent capable of systematically researching, cross-referencing, and documenting parameter values represents a substantial efficiency gain, not by replacing engineering judgment, but by accelerating one of the most time-consuming and error-prone stages of the modeling workflow.

Generating 

validation scenarios

https://www.designnews.com/medical/five-ways-cdmos-oem-engineers-can-work-together-to-deliver-better-medical-devices

, tracing assumptions, and making modeling choices explicit are essential to trustworthy engineering, yet they are among the most time-consuming and least rewarding parts of the workflow. Engineers know this work matters, but it is tedious, repetitive, and often deferred under schedule pressure. Agentic systems are well suited to this task. They can systematically generate validation cases, enumerate assumptions embedded in model structure and parameter choices, and document these decisions in a form that is inspectable and auditable. By offloading this work to agents, engineers gain confidence in their models while reclaiming time for interpretation, decision-making, and design judgment.

An engineer specifies intent rather than implementation. The requirement is expressed in terms of system behavior, operating conditions, and performance goals. An AI system constructs a physically consistent model, selecting appropriate components, defining governing equations, coupling subsystems, and enforcing conservation laws by construction. It generates validation scenarios and documents the assumptions embedded in the model.

 

Image courtesy of JuliaHub.

The engineer's role shifts. Instead of spending weeks assembling and debugging the model, they spend their time interrogating results, comparing design choices, and reasoning about tradeoffs. The AI handles the mechanics of model construction, simulation execution, post-processing, and structured exploration. The engineer handles engineering judgment.

This difference is not incremental. It is structural.

Why this requires agentic AI

It is tempting to describe this as faster modeling or better automation. That framing understates what is required.

The challenge in hardware engineering is not running simulations. It is deciding what to simulate, where to find the data needed to parameterize and validate models, how to simulate the system, and how to interpret the results. These decisions depend on context, constraints, and physical understanding. They are not easily reduced to static scripts.

Agentic AI offers a way forward because it can reason over structured representations of systems. Rather than treating models as opaque code, an agent can understand components, equations, parameters, constraints, and the relationships between them as explicit objects. It can modify them, check consistency, and explore alternatives while remaining grounded in physical law.

This requires more than an LLM. It requires formal representations of physics that are both expressive and machine-interpretable. Conservation laws must be enforced, not just encouraged or assumed to be enforced. Assumptions must be explicit, not hidden. Results must be traceable to equations and parameters that engineers recognize.

Without this foundation, AI becomes a liability. With it, AI becomes a collaborator, one that operates under engineer-provided guidance in the form of skills, resources, and prompts. These inputs are reusable, refinable, and naturally support a process of continuous improvement as engineering knowledge is captured, reused, and evolved over time.

Dyad's approach

Dyad

https://juliahub.com/products/dyad

 is built around this premise. Models are treated as first-class objects, not as side effects of code. Physical structure is explicit. Components know how they connect. Equations know what they conserve. Parameters know their units and roles.

This structure allows AI agents to operate meaningfully within the engineering workflow. The tooling understands these structures explicitly and provides continuous feedback to the agent, helping ensure physical consistency, valid coupling, and interpretable results. Within this environment, agents can construct models from intent, validate physical consistency, run structured explorations, and explain outcomes in terms that align with engineering reasoning. Because Dyad was developed in the age of AI, agent interaction is considered a first-class concern in product design, with workflows and interfaces shaped to maximize agent effectiveness alongside human engineers.

The goal is not to replace engineers or to obscure complexity. It is to shift complexity to where machines are strongest and to preserve human attention for decisions that require judgment.

In this sense, Dyad's agents are not copilots in the software sense. They are closer to colleagues who handle preparation and analysis so that engineers can focus on design.

A pattern across industries

The friction brake example is illustrative, but not unique. In aerospace engineering, early design stages are dominated by trade studies across aerodynamics, structures, and thermal constraints. Building coupled models is expensive, which limits exploration. Agentic modeling can compress these cycles without sacrificing rigor, while also assisting with practical modeling challenges such as initialization, numerical stability, and debugging issues that often consume significant engineering time.

In 

multi-domain systems

https://www.designnews.com/industry/catch-these-new-ai-products-and-systems

, engineers must reason about interactions between electrical, thermal, and chemical processes. Errors propagate easily. Structured, physics-aware agents can help manage complexity while maintaining trustworthiness.

In robotics and manufacturing, control systems interact with mechanical dynamics and sensing in nonlinear ways. Iteration is slow because moving from concept to hardware prototype is a lengthy and tedious cycle as engineers work across multiple tools and formulations. Agentic systems can reduce the friction across these boundaries.

Why this moment matters

The convergence that makes this possible is recent. Advances in symbolic modeling, differentiable programming, and solver technology have made it feasible to represent complex physical systems in structured ways. At the same time, AI systems have become capable of reasoning over such structures, not just over text.

This combination enables a new class of tools that respect the realities of physical engineering rather than abstracting them away.

Trust will determine adoption. Engineers are right to be skeptical of tools that promise speed without accountability. Any AI system in this space must be transparent, physically grounded, auditable, and reusable.

Dyad's approach reflects this constraint. It does not attempt to bypass physics. It makes physics legible to machines.

 

From assistance to partnership

The future of engineering is often framed in terms of automation versus human expertise. This framing misses what is actually changing.

The more important shift is from assistance to partnership. AI agents act as a force multiplier: they do not replace the engineer; they make the engineer more effective. By taking on mechanical, repetitive, and preparatory work, agents amplify human capability. Importantly, the impact grows with experience, the deeper the engineer's knowledge and judgment, the greater the leverage these agents provide.

When this balance is achieved, engineering work changes. Fewer weeks are spent building and debugging models. More time is spent asking better questions and evaluating meaningful alternatives. Exploration expands, decisions improve, and the downstream result is not just faster development, but better hardware systems that are more efficient, more robust, and safer because they have been explored, validated, and optimized more thoroughly before anything is built.

Software engineering has already crossed this threshold. Hardware engineering has not, but the path is now visible.

Agentic AI that respects physical law and engineering practice is not a convenience. It is a prerequisite for scaling human insight in a world where physical systems are growing more complex.

That is the opportunity in front of hardware engineering, and it is only beginning to be realized.
