Abstract Investigating NLP through a philosophical lens has recently caught researchers’ eyes, as it bridges computational methods with classical schools of philosophy. This paper introduces a philosophi-cal framework inspired by the Hegelian Dialectic to enable LLMs’ self-reflection, utilizing a self-dialectical approach to emulate internal critiques and synthesize new scientific ideas (spanning do-mains such as mathematics, physics, and more). Additionally, we explore the effect of generation temperature in LLMs by introducing a dynamic annealing approach, which encourages creativ-ity in the early stages and gradually focuses on refinement and nuance, as well as a constant-temperature strategy. Furthermore, we implement a Multi-Agent Majority Voting (MAMV) strategy to assess the validity and novelty of the gener-ated ideas, which proves useful in the absence of domain experts. We also evaluate the effective-ness of our method in generating novel scientific ideas and improving LLMs’ reasoning capabil-ities. Our experiments demonstrate promising results in ideation, along with significant improve-ments in mathematical and symbolic reasoning.

Hegel conceptualizes the dialectical process as consisting of three distinct “moments” or stages. The initial stage, known as the moment of “understanding,” represents a period of stability in which a concept or idea appears to have a fixed definition. The second stage, known as the “dialectical” moment, emerges when inherent flaws or limitations within the first stage become evident, prompting the evolution of the original idea into one that stands in opposition to it in a specific manner (i.e., in response to its particular limi-tations). Hegel characterizes this transition as a process of “self -sublation” because it is the defects of the first mo-ment or original stage itself that lead to the emergence of the second moment. This second stage “sublates” the first moment because it simultaneously negates and preserves it: it supersedes the first, but it is still shaped by the very limitations it seeks to address, thereby remaining inherently connected to the initial concept. The interaction between these two stages culminates in the third “speculative” mo-ment, which reconciles and unifies the first two stages. This third moment is not arbitrary, but rather a result of the spe-cific opposition between the earlier stages, and as such, it possesses a distinct character derived from that opposition. This speculative moment is more advanced and comprehen-sive, as it integrates and transcends the preceding stages, thereby entailing a more universal perspective. This final stage can be understood to be a secondary-level process, as it sublates both previous moments: it negates the earlier stages by presenting a new idea (concept) while still depend-ing on them for its own definition, effectively building upon and transforming the earlier moments (Maybee, 2020). 

A characteristic of Hegel’s unifying step (speculative mo-ment) is that the oppositions do not need to be resolved, allowing for the expression of ambiguity given competing perspectives (Hegel, 1951; 1807). To keep the result of the unifying step consistent, we choose to resolve the introduced oppositions via qualifications and negations, all the while accumulating the premises that results in oppositions as part of the unifications, so as to have a list of all individually self-opposing points considered. 4. Proposed Hegelian Self-reflection Inspired by the Hegelian Dialectic, we employ an iterative process, allowing an LLM to evaluate an initial response or “Proposition” through a generated critique, “Opposition”, and subsequently generate a new response, henceforth re-ferred to as “Unified response”, incorporating the best ele-ments of the two. This method aims to improve the model’s ability to integrate complex and opposing information, lead-ing to more coherent and sound responses. 

Additionally, we explore two temperature configurations and their effect on the generated responses: 1) a dynamic approach by introducing an annealing scheduler for the LLM’s temperature. This ensures high creativity in the early stages of speculation (unifying step), gradually reducing it and emphasizing the addition of subtle nuances to the unified responses and, 2) a static approach that maintains a constant temperature throughout all iterations. 

As the quality of new ideas is difficult to measure, our goal in generating novel ideas is to meet two main criteria: (1) validity, ensuring that the generated response adheres to all instructions of the speculation, and (2) novelty, assessing whether the new idea introduces original content beyond the initial proposition. To do so, we draw inspiration from Marvin Minsky’s concept (Minsky, 1988) a.k.a. “Society of Mind” (SoM), where intelligence is attained through computational modules that interact and collaborate with each other to achieve objectives that cannot be accomplished by a single module alone (Zhuge et al., 2023).

4.2. Measuring Validity and Novelty of a New Ideas 

Inspired by “Society of Mind” concept and multi-agent voting, we employ multiple LLMs to assess validity and novelty of the unified ideas. In particular, we separately prompt three LLMs and ask if the generated response has followed the Speculation i.e., canceling and unifying instruc-tions (validity test), and if the information in the unified idea is more novel than the proposition (novelty test). We then take the majority vote of the LLMs’ opinions. If there is consensus on both validity and novelty, we proceed with the dialectic process; otherwise, we stop at that point. With this in mind, we define the following score to measure novelty: 

Novelty Score = Iterations Voted as Novel by MAMV Total Dialectical Iterations 

In fact, we adopt a greedy strategy as a heuristic for novelty. This approach is easy to implement, but we acknowledge that it has its pros. and cons. For instance, if a step does not meet our novelty criteria, we do not proceed. In reality, some debates may go back and forth without producing sig-nificant novelty until later stages. This stringent condition might limit creativity. Thus, for a system employing it to create diverse self-reflective processes, adjusting the nov-elty prompt’s strength or the voting condition could yield different results. To simulate the sublation process, we employ an iterative self-debating, Solo Performance Prompting (SPP) strategy. Dialectical Iterations and MAMV: For simplicity, we set a maximum constant for iterations as indicated in Appendix A, Table 2. For idea generation, we evaluate the validity and novelty of the unified idea produced through self-reflection using an MAMV module external to the self-reflection pro-cess. If the MAMV determines that the generated idea lacks sufficient novelty or that the speculation was not properly followed, the process is halted. This approach prevents re-dundant iterations over existing knowledge and establishes a clear stopping point. Aside from evaluation, the MAMV does not interfere with the model’s self-reflection and idea generation. For reasoning task, we do not use MAMV or ma-jority voting, as our goal is to evaluate the effectiveness of dialectical self-reflection isolated from a multi-agent frame-work or voting, which could be applied to any technique.

7. Future Directions In this paper, for simplicity, we used a constant 

τA. 

How-ever, as we discussed earlier, it directly affects novelty, with lower 

τA 

producing arguments directly addressing the initial proposition and higher 

τA 

results in orthogonal perspectives that lead to epistemological inquiries. Investigating multi-ple oppositions with varying 

τA 

simultaneously, generating unified ideas, and backtracking from undesired outcomes is worthwhile. We reserve this exploration for future research. 

Currently, with the novelty score stop condition, our frame-work strives to resolve dialectical contradictions every cycle. In future implementations, the statements from opposing view points might be explicitly considered as ambiguous and not integrated into the unified idea until enough itera-tions have passed to resolve them. 

Meanwhile, in the MAD setting, the Multi-Persona frame-work addresses the degeneracy-of-thought issue in naive self-reflection by pre-assigning one agent to express view-points and another to oppose them (Liang et al., 2024). By adjusting the likelihood of agreement in debate proto-cols, the Multi-Persona MAD framework surpasses other MAD frameworks in the Q&A setting by tuning the agree-ability of debating agents. (Liang et al., 2024; Smit et al., 2024). Similarly, the decisiveness of the speculation step can be tuned and compared with the performance of agree-able/disagreeable MAD frameworks. For further commen-tary on the limitations, refer to Appendix B. 

8. Concluding Remarks This paper introduces a novel technique for LLM self-reflection by framing it as a self-dialectical process grounded in Hegel’s dialectics. It follows a periodic cycle of understanding, sublation, and speculation, in which an initial proposition is critically evaluated through generated opposing views and then refined into a unified idea that inte-grates the strongest elements of both views. Meanwhile, we investigate the impact of speculation temperature on the nov-elty of generated ideas by establishing two configurations: (1) a constant and (2) a dynamic annealing temperature. To assess the novelty of generated idea, we employ MAMV, a multi-agent system designed to collaborate and vote based on proposed validity and novelty metrics. Our experiments on math and symbolic reasoning tasks, show significant improvement. In addition, we applied our method to a qual-itative task namely, novel idea generation, which resulted in promising outcomes. We observed that high constant tem-peratures occasionally produced off-topic or highly novel ideas, while the most innovative ideas typically emerged early in the annealing setting, becoming more nuanced and stable over time.

B. Limitations In this section, we briefly discuss some limitations of our current framework and experimental setup. 

Deliberate adaptation and interpretation choices in Hegelian dialectic We formulate Hegel’s dialectic as a 2-step progress, where the flow from proposition to opposition and then unified idea does not follow any sort of logical necessity as proposed by (Maybee, 2020; Kaufmann, 1965; Inoue, 2014). This also means that the perspective from which the opposition introduces its opposition is arbitrary, i.e. delegated to the LLM agent. (2) The unified idea always maintains a self-consistent position. In Hegel’s dialectic, statements can remain ambiguous, where they are true from one perspective and false from the other, without requiring a resolution (Hegel, 1807; Maybee, 2020). Interested readers can refer to (Maybee, 2020; Inoue, 2014) for more details. 

Measuring novelty of a statement is challenging as it involves subjective assessment and context-awareness. Novelty is not just about adding new information, it also requires understanding existing literature to ensure that the idea has not been previously explored. Comparing an idea solely based on an initial proposition does not account for depth of existing research. Our novelty prompt has inherent limitations. It does not explicitly define whether altering the strength of an argument is considered novel, even if all points have been previously mentioned. Additionally, it remains unclear whether the model should regard the negation of existing premises as novel, as it involves manipulating existing information rather than introducing new information. However, we view such negation as novel. 

Difficulties in distinguishing creativity from LLM remembering in baseline evaluation Evaluating new ideas against pre-proposed unified idea is extremely challenging, as distinguishing whether a model is merely recalling training information or generating creative ideas is challenging. 

Measuring the effect of annealing due to randomness of generation: We often observed that, a constant 

τ 

either fails to produce novel content when set too low or leads to irrelevant information when set too high. However, due to inherent randomness in the generation, it is not straightforward to generalize this observation. Conducting statistical significance tests over multiple rounds of prompting can help to evaluate this observation more effectively. 

Reproducibility of results: The inherent randomness in the generation process, coupled with constantly evolving nature of LLMs and the lack of control, especially when using black-box models (e.g. GPT family), makes it difficult to reproduce the results which is essential in scientific settings. While white-box models offer better control over outputs, they may not be as powerful as black-box competitors. 

Repetition and randomness in API calls We have occasionally observed identical opposition and unification paragraphs, as well as main points, raising concerns about the extent of randomness and the possible presence of hidden caching mechanisms during these experiments. 

Need for domain expert LLMs Utilizing the extensive knowledge of domain-specific LLM experts can help identify unique contributions and ensure comprehensive coverage of the field, making it more feasible to measure novelty.
