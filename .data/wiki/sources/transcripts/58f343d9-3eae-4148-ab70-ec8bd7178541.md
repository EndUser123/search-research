# Former U.S. National Security Advisor Jake Sullivan on Canada and the New World Order
From tariffs and trade uncertainty to an ascendant 
China, war in the Middle East and Europe,  

and growing uncertainty about the future 
of American leadership, Canada is having  

to rethink some of its oldest assumptions about 
its place in the world and its relationship with  

its neighbor and closest ally. Few people have 
been closer to the seismic shifts playing out  

internationally than Jake Sullivan who served as 
national security adviser to former US President  

Joe Biden. Jake Sullivan is in Ottawa today for 
the annual CANSEC defense and security trade show  

and he's been kind enough to make time for us. 
Jake Sullivan, thanks so much for being here.  

Really appreciate it. Thank you for having me. 
So you've argued that the post cold war era is  

definitively over. Prime Minister Mark Carney has 
described this moment in similarly stark terms,  

not as a transition but as a rupture in the world 
order. as he calls it. There has been some debate  

in this country about whether he is overstating 
the degree to which Canada's relationship with  

the United States has changed. Do you think he 
has the diagnosis and the prescription right  

or is Canada at risk of maybe overcorrecting for 
what might still prove to be a temporary political  

cycle in Washington? Well, I do believe that the 
prime minister is right that the international  

order is under undergoing a fundamental shift. 
uh and that as I have said that the post cold war  

era is behind us and a competition is underway 
for what comes next and what comes next will  

look quite different from what came before. So I 
think he is right about that. Where I would inject  

a note of caution though is that I think that 
the current friction between the US and Canada  

is not fundamentally structural. It is about 
the current occupant of the Oval Office and his  

particular view on Canada, his particular way of 
engaging on questions of sovereignty and hurling  

insults and doing Truth Social posts. And so yes, 
I do think that there's room for the US - Canada  

relationship to evolve, to grow, to change, but 
fundamentally I believe in a deep and enduring  

partnership between our two countries because 
I think there is no other alternative for the  

national interest of both of us. But many allies, 
not just Canada, are now building resilience not  

just towards their traditional rivals, but also 
against volatility from Washington. It does seem  

that American unpredictability has maybe become 
a fact of geopolitics, something that now allies  

are actively hedging against. I know you've said 
that you think Canada and the US are bound by this  

triple destiny through geography, our shared 
democracies, uh shared technological future,  

but many Canadians are frankly rather worried 
about the state of American democracy today. Do  

you think Americans understand just how rattled 
its traditional allies are by the second Trump  

presidency? I think some do, some probably 
don't have a full picture. I certainly have  

come to understand this quite deeply traveling to 
places like Ottawa, traveling to Europe, traveling  

to our traditional treaty partners in Asia as 
well. So, it's very clear that this has beyond  

rattled. I think it's shaken the foundations of 
these countries view of the United States and  

I get that. I understand why these questions are 
being asked. I guess I would make two points. The  

first is Canada diversifying its relationships, 
increasing its own native capabilities in defense  

and technology, uh, building closer ties to Europe 
and Asia and other countries. I think that's good  

not just for Canada, it's good for the US-Canada 
partnership as well, for our partnership to be  

embedded in this broader network of relationships. 
And second, my view is that at the end of the day,  

Democrats and Republicans, elected officials and 
private citizens almost across the board, have  

a fundamentally positive view of the US Canada 
relationship and want it to succeed. I think that  

the outlier is the current president. And so, 
while we're not going to go back to how things  

were before, I do think there is a degree of 
overstating the extent to which the relationship  

is so deeply broken it cannot be retrieved. I 
don't think that's inevitable. and I want to  

fight for it because I think it's in our interest 
to do so. There still is a strong sense in Canada  

that even after Trump, things might not go back 
to anything like they were before. And I think  

you're saying that that the relationship will have 
to, you know, evolve. But there is this sense that  

that trust that previously existed just can't be 
restored by a change in administration, even a  

democratic one. Do you think that's right? And if 
you think the relationship does need to evolve,  

how do you see it evolving concretely what would 
that look like? You know, it's an interesting  

thing about trust. trust can be eroded, uh, 
degraded, even destroyed very rapidly, and it  

can only be rebuilt and cultivated more slowly. 
So, I don't think the day a new president walks  

into office, he or she can snap their fingers and 
just say, "Okay, everything's back. Everything's  

good." No, I I don't believe that's viable. It's 
going to require hard work together and an agenda  

that is about delivering practical solutions that 
we both share. For example, we have to defend our  

continental perimeter against evolving threats 
from the Arctic, from long range drones, from  

missile proliferation. We've got to do that. We've 
got to find a way forward that does that. For  

example, we have to deal with the fact that China 
is engaged in massive industrial overcapacity and  

that its sheer scale threatens to undermine and 
hollow out the industrial bases of both Canada  

and the United States. We're going to have to 
figure out how to work together to reinforce  

and strengthen our industrial bases. These are 
just realities in my view. And so the question  

about building trust is one that has to come 
over time, but it particularly has to come not  

just through soothing words from a new president, 
but through actions and policy and follow through.  

And that's what I hope to see from the United 
States in the years ahead. You mentioned China.  

Chinese foreign minister Wang Yi is in Ottawa 
this week. The first visit to Canada by Chinese  

foreign minister in a decade. It follows Mr. 
Carney’s trip to uh Beijing earlier this year  

and what appears to be a deliberate effort to 
to thaw that relationship. How difficult do you  

think this balancing act is for Canada? How far 
can this country expand or deepen its engagement  

with China while still seeking to remain more 
fundamentally aligned with the United States?  

You know, I think that we had a pretty good 
formula going on this uh during the Biden  

administration and it is a formula that I believe 
for all the Toing and froing in American politics  

will ultimately stand the test of time and that 
is the concept of derisking not decoupling. Canada  

has and should have an economic relationship 
with China just like the United States does.  

That's totally natural. So I don't believe in 
decoupling our economies but de-risking that  

is making sure that we are taking account of the 
national security threats of for example Chinese  

telecoms or Chinese software in Canadian critical 
infrastructure and making sure that we are  

guarding against the risk of supply chain coercion 
where China could cut off Canada or the US from  

vital inputs to our economy or squeeze in other 
ways. This I think is something that we are going  

to have to do and so too is Europe and so too 
are our friends in uh Korea, Japan and and other  

countries in Asia. So de-risking not decoupling 
for me is the order of the day. I just want to  

step back a little bit again and talk about how 
Canada is trying to successfully navigate this new  

reality. Mr. Carney argued in his speech in Davos 
that middle powers are not powerless in this new  

era of great power rivalry that they need to band 
together and he argues that they can still shape  

outcomes by cobbling together these coalitions 
issue by issue. I wonder whether you buy that  

argument. Do you think middle powers have more 
room to maneuver in this environment or less?  

I think middle powers have quite a bit of leverage 
and quite a bit of capacity and you're seeing that  

in the way that Canada is extending its innovation 
and industrial partnerships with other countries  

whether it's India or it's you know new trade 
arrangements in aluminum with the Netherlands  

and you know the list is pretty long so I think 
there are opportunities here but I would offer  

one word of caution which is I do not accept the 
idea that that it makes sense for democracies  

like Canada to equate the United States and China 
to say basically they're the same and we need to  

deal with both of them similarly. I think the US 
and China present incredibly different pictures  

for Canada. Whatever our current travails at the 
end of the day, our geography, our democracy,  

our shared view of the future of technology, these 
bind us together. And I think it's important for  

Canada to see these distinctions between the US 
and China and not paint them with the same brush.  

And then just finally, I'd be remiss if I didn't 
ask you about Iran as well. Uh despite months of  

fighting, the regime has clearly shown its staying 
power, its ability to threaten the region, and of  

course its willingness to choke off global energy 
flows in the street of Hormuz. When you look at  

where things stand now, I understand obviously 
things are, you know, obviously still in flux and  

it's it's unclear in which direction they're going 
to go, but how do you see this conflict ending?  

Well, as someone who helped negotiate the original 
Iran nuclear deal back in 2015, then watched it  

get torn up and warned that that could eventually 
lead us into a misbegotten war. It has been sad  

for me to see this war play out and I think it 
has put the United States and the world in a  

terrible position where Iran is holding the world 
hostage by closing the straight of Hormuz. Iran is  

holding the Gulf hostage by threatening civilian 
and energy infrastructure and the United States  

is not capable of exerting its will to produce the 
outcomes it wants. It leaves me basically with the  

same conclusion that I've drawn for many years. 
The only way to deal with the threat Iran poses  

through its nuclear program is through a deal, 
a diplomatic deal. I hope that is what ends up  

happening in the coming days and weeks. But we 
never should have had to go through this war in  

the first place and all the costs it's imposed 
on everyone. We could have had that deal. We did  

have that deal and President Trump pulled out of 
it and we're all dealing with the consequences.  

Jake Sullivan, former national security adviser 
to US President Joe Biden. We'll have to leave it  

there. Thanks again very much for your time. 
I appreciate it. Thank you for having me.
