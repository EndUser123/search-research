# Why Post-War Russia Will Fall into the Same Historical Trap That Created Hitler
What happens to a country after it loses 
a war it was told it could not lose? 

That is the question Russia will face 
when the war in Ukraine ends — or freezes,  

or becomes impossible to sell as victory.
And to understand the danger, we need  

to look at another European country a 
little more than a hundred years ago. 

Germany after the First World War.
History never repeats itself perfectly. Russia  

is not Weimar Germany. Putin is not the Kaiser, 
and the twenty-first century is not the 1920s. 

But societies do have patterns.
When a country modernizes quickly,  

becomes proud of its new strength, then 
enters a war expecting triumph — and  

instead gets humiliation, economic stress, dead 
soldiers, returning veterans, and no satisfying  

victory — the public often does not become wiser.
It looks for a story that protects its dignity. 

In Germany, that story became the betrayal myth: 
we did not really lose, we were stabbed in the  

back. That lie helped open the road to Hitler.
Post-war Russia may be heading toward a similar  

trap, because the ingredients are frighteningly 
familiar: wounded pride, imperial nostalgia,  

censorship, veterans returning from a brutal war, 
a public trained to worship past glory, and a  

regime that can never honestly say: we were wrong.
I’m Elvira Bary, a writer born in the Soviet  

Union. And today I want to show you why post-war 
Russia could fall into the same emotional and  

political trap that once helped create Hitler — 
and what the West must understand if it wants to  

avoid the next revenge cycle.

“we were betrayed” to “we were wrong”

Now let’s begin with the first ingredient 
in the trap: the hunger for respect.

Hunger for Respect

Before the First World War, Germany 
was a rising power with fast-growing  

industry. The edge it had in science, engineering,  

education, and manufacturing gave 
Germans real reasons to feel proud. 

But at the same time, Germany had become strong 
enough to compare itself with Britain and France.  

And comparison is a dangerous sport. Britain had 
the biggest empire. France had old prestige. Both  

had colonies, sea routes, global reach, and 
a place at the table that Germany wanted. 

So German nationalism grew out of a strange 
emotional mix: achievement and grievance. 

“We have done so much. Why are 
we still treated as second rank?” 

This is exactly the kind of feeling 
that later appeared in Putin’s Russia. 

After the chaos of the 1990s, many Russians 
really did feel that life had improved.  

Cities looked better. Shopping malls opened. 
People traveled more. Oil and gas money filled  

the budget. Moscow became rich and shiny. The 
state paid salaries on time. The humiliations  

of the Soviet Union collapse seemed to fade.
But again, that improvement produced a new  

question: if we have recovered, why are 
we not feared and respected like before? 

That is the key word: respected.
In Russia, it still has the old imperial  

meaning and translates as: obeyed and feared.
This is where the early-twentieth-century Germany  

and today’s Russia begin to resemble each other.
Both countries had real progress, but their  

political cultures turned their real achievements 
into craving for more. The more they gained,  

the more they felt deprived. The state did not 
say: “Look, life is getting better, let’s build a  

normal future.” It said: “Look how strong we are 
becoming. Now the world must make room for us.” 

And when the world did not move 
aside politely, resentment deepened. 

In Germany, Kaiser Wilhelm II wanted 
what was called Weltpolitik — world  

policy. His plan for Germany was: build its 
own fleet, seek overseas colonies, and show  

that it belonged among the great imperial players.
In Putin’s Russia, the language was different, but  

the hunger was familiar. Russia wanted a sphere 
of influence. It wanted neighbors – Ukraine,  

Georgia, Moldova, or the Baltic states – to 
obey its gravity and the West to stay away. 

The tragedy is that both societies 
confused dignity with domination. 

A person with dignity does not need to 
force everyone at the table to admire  

him. But imperial resentment treats 
someone else’s freedom as an insult. 

That is why Ukraine became so unbearable for 
Putin’s Russia. Ukraine was not only a neighboring  

state but a living alternative. It showed that 
people who speak Russian, share Soviet history,  

and know Russian culture could still choose a 
different road. I have a dedicated video about  

how and why Ukraine was able to break free from 
the imperial dream while Russia stayed trapped

The Greatness Machine

How does the state turn ordinary 
people into fans of empire? 

It gives them a story where their boring 
life is secretly part of something enormous. 

This happened in Germany before the First 
World War. Generations grew up with the memory  

of victory over France in 1870 and 1871. That 
victory helped unite the German Empire. It became  

emotional property passed down to children.
The next generations did not defeat France  

themselves. But they could feel 
as if they owned the glory. 

That is the trick: the state takes an old victory, 
polishes it, and hands it to people as identity. 

Putin’s Russia did the same with the Soviet 
victory in the Second World War. Victory Day  

stopped being a day of mourning and memory 
and became a lavish public celebration with  

parades and school rituals, all of which 
taught the same lesson: we are the heirs  

of the great victorious people.
And if we are heirs of victory,  

then anyone who resists us must be a new fascist.
The same mechanism worked through culture. In  

imperial Germany, patriotic art, theater, music, 
monuments, and newspapers fed a young nation with  

images of heroic ancestors and national destiny.
In Russia, the state sponsored its own version:  

films about heroic security officers, 
imperial maps, Orthodox-patriotic ceremonies,  

endless documentaries about Western plots, 
and school lessons where war became the  

center of civic identity.
The message was: your life  

may be small, but your country is great.
And for many people, this message is addictive. 

Imagine a man whose life has no visible 
victory. He works a dull job in a gray  

town and has very limited options to make his 
future better. Then the television tells him:  

you belong to a civilization that defeated 
Nazism, conquered space, frightens NATO,  

and carries a sacred mission.
He sits on the couch,  

but emotionally he stands on a parade square.
This is why patriotic propaganda works even  

when it gives people nothing practical. It offers 
them something more valuable: status and purpose.

The Short War Illusion

Germany entered the First World War with 
confidence. Its military planning was built  

around speed: strike fast, defeat France, 
then turn east and solve the problem before  

the country’s weak points became fatal.
Because Germany did have weak points. 

It had brilliant industry, but fewer global 
resources than the empires it challenged. Germany  

could win only if the war stayed short.
That is always the trap. 

A country that needs a short war starts 
telling itself the war will be short. 

Russia did the same in Ukraine. The Kremlin looked 
at its size, its army, its missiles. Then it  

looked at Ukraine and saw a much smaller country, 
divided by language, tied to Russia by family  

history, and supposedly ruled by weak politicians.
So the plan was built on contempt. On the  

assumption that Kyiv would fall in a 
few days and the government would run. 

This is how propaganda becomes strategy. For 
years, Russian television said Ukraine was not  

a real state. Ukrainian identity was artificial. 
The government was controlled from outside. So  

when the invasion began, the Kremlin was attacking 
the cartoon of Ukraine it had drawn for itself. 

Germany had its own cartoon. It imagined a war 
where discipline, planning, and national energy  

would defeat the messiness of its enemies.
But the enemy did not behave as expected. 

Belgium resisted. France did not collapse 
fast enough. Britain entered the war. The  

German plan began to crack – just 
as Putin’s plan in Ukraine did. 

In an authoritarian system, the leader can 
never admit a mistake. If they ever do,  

that’ll be the end of them. So instead 
of backing off, they double down. 

Germany’s short war became a war of 
exhaustion. Russia’s quick operation  

became a long grinding conflict. What was 
supposed to prove greatness began to consume it.

The Victory Bubble

Censorship does not make a country stronger.
What it does is make bad news arrive late. 

That is one of the most dangerous similarities 
between Germany in the First World War and Russia  

in the war against Ukraine. Both societies were 
fed the version of war they wanted to believe. 

In Germany, patriotic newspapers, military 
communiqués, school culture, and public rituals  

kept the nation inside the idea of endurance and 
eventual victory. People knew there was suffering.  

They saw wounded men. They felt shortages. But 
the official frame told them the sacrifice had  

meaning and the army remained strong.
This is how a society can be exhausted  

and still shocked by defeat.
Russia’s version is more modern,  

but the mechanism is the same. Television says 
the army advances. Officials report captured  

villages. The goal is to make the public 
feel that Russia is still moving forward. 

The real battlefield becomes almost irrelevant 
for the average viewer. They want not the truth  

but whatever makes them feel good and right. 
And the state TV gives them exactly that,  

reinforcing the same emotional message: 
we are under attack, but we are winning. 

This is why Z-bloggers are so important in 
the Russian case. They are strongly pro-war,  

but some of them see enough from the front to 
understand that the official picture is false.  

They complain about fake reports, 
useless assaults, failed logistics,  

and commanders who hide reality from Moscow. 

They are still trapped inside 
imperial thinking. But they  

are also poking holes in the victory bubble.
When that bubble breaks, people feel robbed. 

They remember years of “we are advancing,” 
“the enemy is collapsing,” “the West is tired,”  

“victory is near.” Then suddenly they face a 
poorer country, dead sons, returning veterans,  

and no grand triumph.
That moment creates rage.

The Betrayal Myth

After the First World War, millions of 
Germans could not accept that their army  

had been beaten by exhaustion, blockade, 
bad strategy, and stronger opponents.  

They had been told for years that 
Germany was brave, disciplined,  

and destined to prevail. Then the empire 
collapsed, the Kaiser disappeared, and the  

new republic had to sign a humiliating treaty.
For many, the mind refused the obvious conclusion. 

If we were so strong, how did we lose?
The answer came quickly: traitors. 

The “stab-in-the-back” myth claimed that Germany 
had not truly lost on the battlefield. It had been  

undermined from within by socialists, 
liberals, republicans, Jews, cowards,  

profiteers, and anyone else useful as an enemy.
It was false. But it was emotionally perfect. 

It told defeated people they did not have to feel 
stupid. Their sacrifice had not been wasted by  

bad leadership and impossible war aims. Their 
country had not made a catastrophic mistake.  

No. They had done everything right and almost won 
but someone dirty had stolen victory from them. 

This is the story post-war 
Russia may be heading toward. 

A rational explanation of the war will be 
unbearable for many Russians. It would require  

them to say: we invaded a neighboring country, 
destroyed cities, killed people, believed lies,  

and ended up poorer, weaker, and hated.
That is too much truth. So another story  

will be received better: we were betrayed.
The generals lied. The corrupt officials  

stole supplies and failed the soldiers. 
The cowards ran to Georgia and Armenia. 

Some of these accusations will contain pieces 
of truth. That is what makes the myth dangerous  

as it uses real rot to hide the central fact: 
the war itself was the crime and the mistake.

The Veteran Problem

A failed war always comes home.
This is one of the most dangerous  

lessons from Germany after the First 
World War. Millions of men came back  

from the front with habits that no civilian 
society could easily absorb. They had lived  

for years in a world where violence was normal.
Then they returned to a country that was poorer,  

angrier, and politically broken.
For many of them, peace felt like  

abandonment. They had risked their lives 
and lost friends, while being told their  

sacrifice had meaning. And then they came home 
to unemployment, humiliation, and politicians  

explaining why the empire was gone.
In Germany, some of these men entered  

paramilitary groups that fought in the streets 
and attacked leftists. They treated politics  

as another battlefield. The war had trained 
them to solve conflict through force, and  

the post-war state was too weak to contain them.
Russia may face its own version of this problem. 

A large number of men will return from a brutal 
war. Some will have injuries. Some will have money  

for a short time and no future after that. And 
many will have learned cruelty as the new normal. 

And they will need a story.
But a rational story would be unbearable.  

It would say: you were used. You were sent 
to destroy another country for lies. Your  

suffering did not save Russia but only served 
a corrupt regime and its imperial fantasy. 

Most people cannot live with that, so 
they will look for another explanation.  

One that lets them keep their dignity.
They will listen to someone who tells  

them: you were brave, but betrayed.
That kind of story is very attractive  

to a man who comes home from war and finds 
that nobody wants him, nobody understands him,  

and nobody can give him back what he lost. It 
tells him that his failure was not his fault. 

And a future nationalist leader will 
know exactly how to speak to him.  

He will only need one sentence: They stole 
your victory, and I will return your honor. 

That’ll be enough to build a movement 
that pulls in veterans, angry patriots,  

young men with no future, officers disappointed 
by the old command, and civilians who want  

someone to explain why their lives got worse.
This is why post-war Russia may be more dangerous  

than wartime Russia in one specific way. During 
the war, the state tries to control violence by  

directing it outward. After the war, that violence 
returns home and starts looking for a new target.

Dignity Without War

So what can the West do to prevent a new Hitler 
from coming to power in the post-war Russia? 

First of all: it should allow 
Russia to keep its dignity. 

After the First World War, Germany was 
blamed for the catastrophe. And yes,  

Germany bore enormous responsibility. But 
the political handling of defeat gave German  

nationalists exactly the story they needed. 
They could point to the treaty, the reparations,  

the lost territories, the foreign pressure, and 
say: look, they want to destroy us. They don’t  

want peace. They want us on our knees forever.
That argument worked because it protected dignity. 

This is the warning for the 
West after the war in Ukraine. 

If Russia comes out poorer, isolated, angry, 
and flooded with returning veterans, the next  

political question will be: who gives Russians 
a way to feel like human beings without empire? 

If nobody does, the revenge 
sellers will come first. 

They will say Russia was cheated, 
betrayed, and denied its rightful  

place. They will say the West wants Russia 
weak forever and Ukraine was only a tool. 

We have heard this music before.
The answer is not appeasement. Appeasement only  

teaches aggressive states that violence works.
The real answer is harder. Russia must lose  

the right to dominate its neighbors. But 
Russians must not be told that dignity  

itself is available only through domination.
There must be a visible path where Russia can  

be poor but not humiliated. Responsible for its 
wrongdoings but not eternally cursed. A country  

can be forced to pay for crimes and still be 
offered a future that does not require revenge. 

This will require serious political imagination.
Russian society needs an alternative story:  

you can be Russian without being imperial. 
You can have dignity without making neighbors  

afraid. You can belong to Europe not as 
a conqueror, but as a normal country. 

Will Russians accept that?
I don’t know. 

But if no such path exists, the old path will 
return. The path of worshipping past glory,  

feeling betrayed, seeking revenge, and 
another leader promising to make the nation  

formidable again.
