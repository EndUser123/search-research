# Why the ADHD Brain Sees the World Differently
Take a look at this image. What do you see? 
For some people, it looks like scattered  

black-and-white patches. For others, the dog 
appears almost immediately. Can you see it? Once  

you see it, something interesting happens. You 
can't really go back. You've seen the pattern.  

Next time, your brain picks it up much faster. 
I'm Dr Sanil Rege, consultant psychiatrist and  

educator, and I've trained 1000s of clinicians 
in brain, body, neuroscience and psychiatric  

practice. In this video, I want to use this simple 
illusion to explain something important about  

ADHD. ADHD is not just about attention. It's about 
how the brain detects significance in a world full  

of competing signals, and once you understand 
that, treatment starts to make much more  

sense. You see, the brain is not just passively 
receiving the world like a camera. It is actively  

trying to make sense of the world for you. It 
predicts. It compares. It matches the prediction  

with the outcome. It checks whether what is coming 
in matches something it already knows. So when  

you look at this image, your brain asks, "What is 
this?" Does this mean anything? Have I seen this  

pattern before? At first, there may be no match, 
just noise, but if you stay with it long enough,  

the pattern emerges, the Dalmatian appears. And 
once that happens, the image is no longer random.  

Your brain has formed a pattern. It now has a 
template, so the next time you see it, recognition  

is quicker, easier, and more automatic. That 
is the key idea the brain evolved for pattern  

recognition because in many ways, treatment in 
ADHD is trying to help the brain do exactly that,  

see the pattern more clearly, stay with it for 
longer and return to it more reliably. But what  

ADHD does is it creates a lot of noise, lots of 
dots, making it difficult to discern the image. It  

also makes persistence hard. Let me explain this 
in a bit more detail. Think about what is required  

to see the Dalmatian in this image. You have to 
stay with the image. You have to tolerate a few  

moments where nothing obvious is coming together. 
You have to remain engaged long enough for the  

brain to organise the noise into meaning. This 
is where ADHD becomes relevant, because in ADHD,  

other stimuli often compete too effectively: 
a sound, a thought, a feeling, a memory,  

a notification, a slightly more interesting 
possibility, a small frustration, a drop in  

reward, boredom, and as a result, the system 
shifts. We interpret that as meaning the person  

can't pay attention, but the difficulty is that 
the task may not hold enough significance long  

enough against everything else competing with it. 
This is why task persistence becomes so important.  

It's also why people with ADHD can focus extremely 
well in some situations and very poorly in others.  

The issue is not the total absence of attention. 
The issue is what gets tagged as important enough  

to hold attention in place. The reason why I 
like this illusion is that it captures something  

patients often describe before the pattern is 
clear. Effort feels hard. It feels unrewarding. It  

can feel almost impossible to stay with the task. 
This is what patients describe as, I'm bored,  

but once the pattern is seen, everything changes. 
Now the brain says, I know what this is. I've  

seen this before. This makes sense, and it's this 
shift that we're trying to achieve in treatment,  

because ADHD treatment is often about helping the 
person build those patterns more reliably in daily  

life, patterns around getting started, patterns 
around changes and transitions, patterns around  

procrastination. This is why good treatment is not 
just about symptom reduction. It's about helping  

the person recognise how their brain works. So 
let's come to medication, specifically stimulants,  

and I'll explain what stimulants actually do. 
The common way stimulants are explained is that  

stimulants will improve attention. Now, that's not 
completely wrong, but I don't think it's the most  

useful way to understand how stimulants work, 
what they do and what we're using them for. A  

better way to think about stimulants is that they 
often increase the significance of the task. They  

increase what's known as task salience. Salience 
is what grabs the brain's attention. Stimulants  

also increase reward relevance. Reward doesn't 
mean pleasure. It simply means the ability to stay  

with the task, to match prediction and outcome, in 
other words, to make sense of what we're engaging  

in. Stimulants also increase arousal, and through 
this combination, they. Improve task persistence.  

The key is that they do not automatically decode 
the world for you. They do not magically reveal  

the Dalmatian. They do not magically create 
meaning out of nowhere. But they may help  

the brain stay engaged with the image or task 
long enough for the pattern to emerge. That's  

a very different model, and in clinical practice, 
I think it's much closer to what we actually see.  

So rather than saying stimulants simply improve 
attention, it may be more accurate to say they  

help the brain hold on to the signal long enough 
for useful patterns to form. But there's a catch,  

and this is where treatment needs nuance. If 
stimulants increase the significance of stimuli,  

they do not do that only for the right things. 
They increase significance more globally. So what  

becomes more important after treatment depends on 
the person, and not everything that becomes more  

significant will be adaptive across the board. 
For example, for some individuals, past shame  

or social threats or anxiety, perfectionism 
and high standards of phone gaming, conflict,  

fear of failure, and the need to stay busy. There 
are so many stimuli that can magnify here. This  

is why prescribing is not just about increasing 
activation. It's about understanding the person,  

what has mattered for this person in the past, 
what is dominating the system now, and what  

needs to become more significant in the 
future. This is where formulation matters:  

medication may strengthen the signal, but we still 
need to know which signal will be strengthened. So  

if we bring all of this together, what are the 
key aims of treatment? Number one, provide or  

develop a coherent model. What I mean by this is 
to help the person see the pattern in their life.  

For many people, diagnosis is powerful because 
it's the first time the noise begins to organise  

into something understandable. I understand why 
I've been the way I've been. It's the first time  

the Dalmatian becomes visible. But simply seeing 
the Dalmatian in this scattered image doesn't mean  

that the individual can see the patterns in 
other images. So that takes us to number two:  

improve the signal-to-noise ratio. We try to 
make the relevant signal stand out more clearly.  

That may involve medication, but it also involves 
sleep structure, environmental changes, reducing  

interruptions, treating anxiety and changing 
how tasks are approached. It's important that  

we take a broader lens here. Number three, train 
pattern recognition. If the brain is a predictive  

organ and has evolved for pattern recognition, the 
clinician's role is to help the person recognise  

repeated sequences. The same applies to partners, 
parents, or teachers; we've got to ask, what helps  

individuals stay on task? What does under arousal 
feel like? What does over-arousal feel like?  

What are the competing stimuli? What contexts 
make their brain work better? Essentially, we  

translate the improvement in the signal into their 
functioning, and number four: offer a predictable  

relationship. The brain learns through repetition, 
and it requires predictability. I've covered this  

in the video here. The brain didn't evolve to 
make you happy. It evolved to help predict your  

world. So a consistent, reliable therapeutic 
relationship helps the person repeatedly see  

the same pattern from a safer distance. So 
creating repeated, reliable experience is  

how the brain starts to trust a new pattern, 
and this takes time. Now you'll notice here  

that what I've talked about matters even more in 
children. Why? Because children are still learning  

how to organise their internal world, they often 
cannot yet identify the patterns for themselves.  

They need help from caregivers, teachers and 
clinicians. They need someone to say, " This  

is the moment you got distracted. This is what 
frustration looks like in your body. I can see  

that you were upset when you were asked to do that 
task. In other words, children often need another  

mind or person to help make the pattern visible. 
This is a really crucial part of treatment because  

it's not just medication; it also helps the child 
repeatedly experience structure, predictability,  

and recognition until those patterns consolidate. 
So let me summarise this for you. ADHD is not  

simply a disorder of not paying attention. 
It is a disorder in which the brain does not  

always reliably assign significance to the right 
thing at the right time in a stable enough way to  

support persistence. That's why novelty. Works. 
Urgency works. Interest, emotion. Deadlines work.  

Sometimes people need panic to move; these states 
force significance. So when a task is important  

but not immediately stimulating, the system just 
doesn't activate, and that's when people start  

blaming themselves, or others call them lazy, 
undisciplined, inconsistent, or unreliable.  

But the key issue here is that the brain 
has not tagged the task as important enough,  

long enough to remain with it. So I hope this
