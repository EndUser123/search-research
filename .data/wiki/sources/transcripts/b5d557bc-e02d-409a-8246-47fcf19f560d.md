# New Climate Research Warns of Faster Warming, AMOC Instability & Rising Tipping Point Risks
Twenty-twenty-six has so far proven to 
be a pretty busy year for new climate  

publications. There have been several major bits 
of research and data released in recent months,  

all pointing in a similar, slightly disturbing 
direction. Faster warming. Growing instability  

in major Earth systems. And potentially 
larger impacts than previously estimated. 

All of which could be, and in some 
sections of the media IS being,  

rolled into a single, dramatic 
apocalyptic narrative. 

Now I like a nice dramatic apocalyptic 
narrative as much as the next person,  

especially if it’s in a good movie. But in 
the real world, I prefer to apply a bit of  

careful thought to all the analysis. So, what 
are all these studies actually saying? Are they  

all in agreement, and what can we reasonably 
conclude from the evidence as it stands today.

Let’s start with the most fundamental of  

all the questions raised in the recent papers…
Is the Earth warming faster than we expected? 

Well, according to this analysis, published 
in March by Grant Foster and Stefan Rahmstorf  

in the online journal Geophysical 
Research Letters, the answer is yes.

So, what does the observed data tell us then?
Well it suggests that the rate of global warming  

over the past ten to fifteen years appears to 
be higher than in earlier decades. According  

to Foster and Rahmstorf, between nineteen 
seventy and about twenty fifteen, the rate  

of change was zero-point-two degrees Celsius 
per decade, but since then it’s been closer to  

zero-point-three-five degrees Celsius per decade.
Now that might sound like dancing on the head of a  

pin, but as a plot on a graph it represents 
quite a step change in the trendline,  

and that’s got scientists scrambling 
to work out exactly what’s caused it. 

Some, like James Hansen and others, point to 
a significant reduction in aerosol pollution,  

especially from sea freight.
Those aerosol particulates tend  

to reflect incoming sunlight back away from 
the earth’s surface, so ironically while they  

were caused by the combustion of fossil fuels 
which are incontrovertibly the main driver of  

additional warming, their presence in the 
atmosphere actually had a cooling effect.  

Now that they’ve been largely regulated out of 
shipping exhaust emissions, and also reduced  

in many urban conurbations across the globe, the 
so-called aerosol dimming effect is diminishing,  

revealing the true extent of human caused warming.
Others, like Professor Michael Mann Professor of  

Earth and environmental science at the University 
of Pennsylvania, point to natural variability…  

particularly in ocean cycles like the ENSO 
system that we looked at in our previous video. 

He and his colleagues highlight the fact 
that there have been two strong El Nino  

events since twenty fifteen and argue that these 
events have caused a bit of a blip in the data. 

The long-term trend since nineteen seventy 
has basically not changed argues Mann 

“The planet is warming at a roughly constant 
rate and that’s bad enough.” He said recently 

“It will continue to do so until 
carbon emissions reach zero,” 

But then there’s research suggesting that 
overall climate sensitivity may have been  

slightly underestimated and that actually 
our earth systems ARE responding more  

acutely to human induced climate forcing.
It’s enough to keep the average person’s  

head spinning, and of course it’s manna from 
heaven for the climate denial brigade because  

it enables them to completely mischaracterize 
normal scientific divergence and uncertainty as  

signs of incompetence or even deception, which 
of course is a proven and very effective way  

to sow just enough doubt in peoples minds for 
them to justify sticking with the status quo. 

But it’s important to be clear here. 
Even though we don’t have unanimous,  

unambiguous settled conclusions and certainty on 
the rate of change in these earth systems, what  

the climate scientists CAN say with confidence 
is that observational datasets continue to show  

a persistent pattern of record or near-record 
temperatures in recent years. So whether or not  

the rate itself has increased… the direction of 
travel is not in question. The system is warming…  

and it’s doing so in line with… or in some cases 
toward the upper end of… previous projections. 

And then there’s recent coverage of the 
Atlantic Meriodional Overturning Circulation  

or AMOC – something we’ve looked at several 
times over the years. The vast system of ocean  

currents that transports warm water northwards 
and cold water southwards, playing a key role in  

regulating climate, particularly in Europe. 5:08
According to a major new paper published in  

Science Advances… the AMOC may be weakening 
more quickly than previously thought. 

The study, led by Valentin Portmann and 
colleagues, combines real-world ocean  

observations with climate models… and concludes 
that the circulation could weaken by around  

fifty percent by the end of this century, which is 
significantly worse than earlier model averages. 

The authors aren’t saying collapse is 
imminent but they are suggesting that the  

system may be closer to a critical threshold… 
or tipping point… than previously believed. 

And that conclusion does seem to 
align with other recent findings. 

There IS still some disagreement in the literature 
on the specifics of timings and rate of change,  

so again, just like with all future predictions, 
it’s not possible to offer absolute certainty.  

But AGAIN the science IS showing increasing 
evidence of vulnerability in the AMOC system. So,  

there is important value in continuing to monitor 
and observe the changes as closely as possible,  

even if we occasionally get contrasting 
or conflicting results, because the  

consequences of a major weakening, or even a 
collapse of the AMOC would be pretty brutal. 

It means significantly less rainfall over West 
Africa, which is already highly drought-prone, and  

also less rainfall over the Amazon, which I think 
we can all agree is a fairly important region for  

the entire planet’s climate. Conversely, 
there would be INCREASED rainfall in parts  

of the Southern Hemisphere tropics which already 
experience extreme flooding and could do without  

any additional stress. Those changes will in turn 
influence agricultural productivity, particularly  

in those regions already vulnerable to drought.
A collapsed AMOC would also bring far colder  

conditions to northern and western Europe 6:48 
and cause significant sea level rises along the  

Eastern seaboard of the USA, potentially impacting 
major coastal cities, societies and economies. 

Which brings us to a further set of discussions in 
recent publications talking about how individual  

tipping points are not moving in isolation 
but are all interconnected with each other. 

This paper, published in February 
twenty-twenty-six on the One Earth website  

by climate experts including Johan Rockstrom, 
highlights the risk of tipping cascades rather  

than individual tipping points. Essentially 
the earth systems are a network, the authors  

explain. Changes in one part of the network can 
increase the likelihood of changes in another. 

So, for example, ice sheet melt adds freshwater to 
the ocean. That fresh water can weaken circulation  

systems like the AMOC, which in turn can affect 
global heat distribution, which then feeds back  

into further warming. We’re already heading for 
increases of two-point-seven degrees Celsius by  

the end of the century, with current policies.
And literally as I was about to start editing  

this video, an early draft report was published 
revealing the newly calculated and calibrated  

climate warming scenarios that will be included 
in the next eagerly awaited assessment report  

from the IPPC, known as AR7. Despite the fact 
that the IPCC has now done away with their  

pretty implausible so-called RCP 8.5 scenario, 
which assumed the world would increase coal use  

seven-fold from twenty-ten onwards –something 
that patently hasn’t happened, and even if  

we shield our eyes and choose to ignore the 
recalibrated ‘high scenario’ in this new chart,  

the so-called ‘Medium Scenario’ still looks 
alarming enough. It tallies pretty well with what  

Johan Rokstrom and his colleagues are warning, 
namely that if we don’t decelerate our emissions  

and decarbonise extremely rapidly then there is 
a very real risk we could stray into the region  

of four degrees Celsius or more of warming, at 
which point, the authors warn, “the economy and  

society will cease to function as we know it”
Rockstrom and his colleagues are not saying a  

“Hothouse Earth” scenario is inevitable, but they 
do seem to be strongly warning that the RISK of  

crossing multiple interacting thresholds INCREASES 
as warming progresses. And crucially… those risks  

are not always well captured in standard climate 
or economic models, which means our organisations,  

institutions and policymakers may not be 
responding as urgently as they should be. 

According to this recent analysis by the 
Institute and Faculty of Actuaries… there’s  

growing concern that widely used economic 
models may be underestimating the true cost  

of climate change. Many models assume gradual,  

linear changes, smooth seamless adaptation by 
governments and societies around the world,  

and relatively limited disruption. But of course 
that’s not how the real world works, is it? 

Extreme weather events like floods, droughts 
and heatwaves push us towards potential tipping  

points that introduce the possibility 
of non-linear shocks. In other words,  

impacts that are larger, faster and more 
disruptive than expected. Again… this isn’t  

presented as a certainty, but it is a growing 
area of concern within the research community. 

For many people… the most immediate expression 
of climate change isn’t ocean circulation  

or economic modelling though - it’s heat.
And here, the evidence is perhaps the most  

direct. Heatwaves are becoming more frequent, 
more intense, and in some cases… pushing into  

ranges that pose serious risks to human health.
The very latest European State of the Climate  

report tells us that at least ninety-five percent 
of Europe experienced above-average annual  

temperatures in twenty-twenty-five. The annual 
sea surface temperature for the European region  

was the highest on record, and eighty-six percent 
of the region experienced at least ‘strong’ marine  

heatwaves. And European wildfires burnt well over 
a MILLION hectares, the largest area on record. 

A record three-week heatwave affected 
Scandinavian and Nordic regions,  

with temperatures near to and within the Arctic 
Circle exceeding thirty degrees Celsius. Glaciers  

in all European regions saw a net mass loss, with 
Iceland recording its second-largest glacier loss  

on record. Snow cover was thirty-one percent 
below average, and the Greenland Ice Sheet lost  

a hundred and thirty-nine billion tonnes of ice.
None of the analysis we’ve looked at so far  

amounts to a single, inevitable 
outcome. But taken together,  

they all point to increasing systemic risk.
The question I get asked most often in the  

comments section of videos like this is 
what can any of us actually do about it? 

I mean it’s all very well for someone like 
me to be blathering on about risks in global  

climate systems… but most of us are just trying to 
navigate our daily lives and don’t feel like our  

individual efforts amount to a hill of beans 
in the face of such enormous global forces. 

Right now though, the answer is arguably NOT 
just about the climate. It’s about resilience. 

For example, at the time of making 
this video… tensions in the Middle  

East and disruption around the Strait of 
Hormuz are pushing up global oil prices. 

And that’s a reminder that fossil fuel dependence 
isn’t just an environmental issue. It’s also an  

issue of economic and geopolitical vulnerability.
So maybe the most relevant conversation isn’t  

just about reducing emissions, it’s about 
reducing exposure to an ever more erratic  

and unreliable source of energy. As individuals 
we CAN switch to an electric vehicle wherever  

and whenever it’s practical and affordable to 
do so. We CAN take steps to insulate our homes  

and if possible electrify our home heating and 
cooling – most likely with heat pumps. And we CAN  

start to generate our own electricity locally 
with solar panels and battery energy storage,  

the cost of which has been plummeting in recent 
years while efficiency has continued to improve  

dramatically. It won’t solve to entire 
climate emergency in one fell swoop but  

it WILL reduce YOUR reliance on fossil fuels.
Which means lower exposure to price shocks,  

greater energy independence, and a 
lower overall demand for oil and gas. 

And, as the recent election results 
in Hungary demonstrate, those of us  

fortunate enough to still be living in ostensibly 
democratic societies can, and absolutely MUST,  

VOTE for the changes we want to see.
Individually each of our actions is  

small. But collectively… they shift the system.
So, yes there’s a lot of new research. And yes,  

most of it is pretty concerning. But 
none of it is pointing to a single,  

guaranteed point of inevitable catastrophe. 
It’s pointing to a set of planetary systems  

under increasing stress with rising risks that 
are becoming harder to ignore. The trajectories  

of those systems are not fixed though. They’re 
all still highly responsive to human decisions. 

And the same actions that reduce the risk 
of those systems breaking down also just  

happen to make our energy systems more secure, 
more stable, and ultimately more resilient.
