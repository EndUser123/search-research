Abstract

This paper addresses epistemic continuity in stateless large language models as an interaction design problem rather than a model architecture problem. Large language models are powerful but fundamentally stateless across sessions. As a result, users engaged in long-horizon work—such as research projects, technical design, legal analysis, or strategic planning—must repeatedly restate assumptions, goals, and prior conclusions. This leads to inefficiency, inconsistency, and, in some cases, hallucination amplification when unvalidated summaries or speculative claims are reintroduced as facts. The paper introduces Externalized Cognitive State Reconstruction (ECSR), a structured protocol for preserving epistemic continuity across LLM sessions without modifying the underlying model. ECSR operates by explicitly extracting, externalizing, validating, and reinjecting the cognitive state governing an interaction, rather than relying on implicit memory, narrative summaries, or anthropomorphic notions of persistence. ECSR formalizes four categories of cognitive state: Semantic state (what is known, hypothesized, or settled) Procedural state (how reasoning is conducted) Normative state (standards of evidence and evaluation) Relational state (interaction mode and scope) The paper specifies a complete, reproducible protocol for managing these states, identifies common failure modes and anti-patterns, and analyzes safety risks—particularly hallucination amplification arising from improperly validated state persistence. It further proposes an assisted automation design that automates mechanical operations while preserving mandatory human epistemic oversight. ECSR is model-agnostic, immediately deployable, and compatible with existing LLM platforms. Its central contribution is a reframing: long-horizon reliability in human–AI collaboration is primarily an interaction design and epistemic control problem, not a question of persistent memory or architectural modification. While empirical validation remains future work, the protocol is intended for immediate use in domains requiring sustained, multi-session collaboration with AI systems.

Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) lack persistent internal state across sessions, leading to continuity failures in

long-horizon tasks such as research, engineering, and analysis. Existing solutions emphasize architectural

changes, memory features, or ﬁne-tuning, but largely neglect interaction design as a locus of control. This paper

introduces Externalized Cognitive State Reconstruction (ECSR), a protocol that achieves epistemic continuity

in stateless LLMs through structured extraction, externalization, validation, and reinjection of interaction state.

ECSR formalizes four categories of cognitive state—semantic, procedural, normative, and relational—and

speciﬁes design principles, anti-patterns, and safety constraints for maintaining them across sessions. Through

detailed case studies based on professional use, the paper demonstrates how ECSR reduces re-derivation, re-

litigation, and hallucination ampliﬁcation in long-horizon workﬂows.

The work further analyzes safety risks arising from improper state persistence, proposes an assisted automation

architecture with mandatory human epistemic oversight, and situates ECSR within established theories of

distributed cognition, cognitive scaffolding, and human–AI collaboration. While empirical validation remains

future work, ECSR provides an immediately deployable, model-agnostic protocol that reframes long-horizon

reliability as an interaction design problem rather than a model capability problem.

Introduction

1.1 Motivation

A materials scientist spends three hours with a large language model deriving stability constraints for a novel

polymer synthesis pathway. The analysis involves iterative reﬁnement: proposing candidate structures, checking

thermodynamic feasibility, rejecting unstable conﬁgurations, and documenting validated constraints. By the end

thermodynamic feasibility, rejecting unstable conﬁgurations, and documenting validated constraints. By the end

of the session, both researcher and model have converged on a set of binding design principles.

The next day, starting a fresh session, the scientist continues the work. Immediately, the model proposes the

same unstable conﬁguration that was explicitly rejected the previous day. The conversation must restart from

scratch, re-explaining the rejected approaches, re-deriving the stability criteria, and re-establishing the

evaluation standards. The model has not "forgotten" in the traditional sense—it never had persistent memory.

Rather, it has undergone a continuity failur e: the epistemic commitments that structured the previous

collaboration were never reconstructed.

This scenario is not unique to materials science. It occurs across domains requiring long-horizon collaboration:

software engineering projects spanning weeks, longitudinal research studies, complex legal analyses, and

iterative design processes. In each case, stateless LLMs force users to manually re-establish context,

assumptions, and standards with every new session—a cognitively expensive and error-prone process.

1.2 The Continuity Problem

Large Language Models have demonstrated substantial capabilities in reasoning, synthesis, and natural

language interaction (Brown et al., 2020; W ei et al., 2022). However, they exhibit a fundamental architectural

limitation: lack of persistent internal state across sessions. Each interaction begins from a reset epistemic

baseline, constrained only by the immediate prompt and system-level instructions.

While vendors increasingly offer extended context windows (Anthropic, 2024; OpenAI, 2024), chat history

features, and proprietary "memory" systems, these mechanisms primarily support context persistence, not

epistemic continuity. They preserve text—the informational content of prior conversations—but not the

structured cognitive state underlying prior reasoning, decisions, and constraints. As a result, long-horizon tasks

often degrade, requiring users to repeatedly re-explain goals, re-establish assumptions, and re-negotiate

standards.

Current practices for managing continuity are informal and inconsistent:

Manual summarization: Users paste summaries of previous sessions, but these are typically

unstructured and incomplete

External note-taking: Researchers maintain separate documents tracking decisions and constraints,

requiring constant manual synchronization (Risko & Gilbert, 2016)

Repeated re-explanation: Each session begins with lengthy preambles restating settled facts and

methods

These ad hoc approaches consume signiﬁcant cognitive resources and introduce systematic errors as context

grows.

1.3 Core Insight

This paper argues that long-horizon intelligence does not require persistent agents. It requires persistent

state representations. The key insight is that an LLM's behavior is almost entirely determined by the

constraints present in its current context window (V aswani et al., 2017). If we can systematically reconstruct the

relevant constraints from prior sessions, we can recreate the functional behavioral state without modifying the

model itself.

W e introduce Externalized Cognitive State Reconstruction (ECSR) as a protocol that enables such

persistence without altering model architecture. ECSR operates entirely at the interaction layer, treating

cognitive state as an explicit, typed, externalized artifact that can be captured, stored, and systematically

reinjected—drawing on principles of distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995) and cognitive scaffolding (W ood et

al., 1976).

1.4 Contributions

This paper makes the following contributions:

Conceptual framework: W e formalize the distinction between context persistence and epistemic

continuity, identifying four orthogonal state types (semantic, procedural, normative, relational) that

together constitute cognitive operating state.

Protocol speciﬁcation: W e provide a complete, reproducible protocol for extracting, externalizing, and

reconstructing cognitive state in stateless LLMs.

Empirical analysis: Through case studies in scientiﬁc research and cross-model validation, we

demonstrate ECSR's effectiveness and identify critical failure modes.

Safety analysis: W e document the risks of anthropomorphic state speciﬁcation through a detailed

analysis of hallucination ampliﬁcation in identity-bound ECSR.

Implementation pathways: W e outline practical deployment options ranging from manual execution to

assisted automation with mandatory human epistemic oversight, including platform-native integration

proposals.

1.5 Scope and Non-Claims

T o prevent misinterpretation, we explicitly state what ECSR is not:

ECSR does not imply consciousness or subjective experience. The protocol reconstructs behavioral

constraints, not phenomenological states.

ECSR does not create persistent agents or entities. Models remain stateless; continuity is a property of

the interaction protocol, not the model.

ECSR does not guarantee correctness of outputs. State reconstruction preserves reasoning standards

but does not validate truth claims.

ECSR does not modify model capabilities or intelligence. It provides systematic constraint

management, not capability enhancement.

ECSR does not replace rigorous veriﬁcation or external validation. Users remain responsible for

epistemic accuracy.

ECSR is a protocol for managing interaction constraints, not a claim about the nature of intelligence or agency.

2.2 Observed Failure Modes

Through analysis of long-horizon LLM interactions across multiple domains, we identify four systematic

continuity failures:

F1: Re-derivation of Settled Results

The model re-performs analyses or computations that were completed and validated in prior sessions. Example:

Re-computing eigenvalues that were previously calculated and accepted.

F2: Re-litigation of Closed Decisions

The model revisits decisions that were explicitly ﬁnalized. Example: Proposing design alternatives that were

previously considered and rejected with documented justiﬁcation.

F3: Drift in Reasoning Standards

The rigor or methodology applied shifts unpredictably across sessions. Example: Accepting informal arguments

in session after requiring formal proofs in session .

F4: Inconsistent Collaboration Posture

The interaction mode changes without user intent (Amershi et al., 2019). Example: Shifting from adversarial

veriﬁcation to uncritical assistance, or vice versa.

Conceptual Framework

3.1 Motivation

Large Language Models (LLMs) are typically deployed as stateless systems: each interaction is processed

independently, without access to internal memory or persistent state across sessions. While this architectural

choice simpliﬁes deployment and safety, it introduces a well-known limitation in long-horizon tasks such as

research collaboration, iterative design, and complex analytical workﬂows. Users repeatedly observe that

models re-derive previously established results, re-litigate resolved assumptions, and drift in evaluative

standards across sessions.

Existing mitigations—such as chat histories, manual summaries, or platform-speciﬁc "memory" features—

primarily address information retention. They do not guarantee continuity in how information is interpreted,

evaluated, or acted upon. As a result, continuity at the interaction level remains fragile and inconsistent.

This section introduces a formal framework for addressing this limitation without modifying model architecture

or parameters.

3.2 From Context Persistence to Epistemic Continuity

W e distinguish between two often-conﬂated concepts:

Context persistence: The availability of prior textual content (e.g., chat logs, summaries, or notes) to the

model.

Epistemic continuity: The preservation of reasoning commitments, evaluation standards, and interactional

constraints across sessions.

Context persistence is necessary but not sufﬁcient for epistemic continuity. A model may have access to prior

text yet still reinterpret assumptions, shift criteria, or change its mode of reasoning.

Example:

Context persistence: "In the previous session, we discussed gradient descent with parameter λ."

Epistemic continuity: "The gradient descent sign must be -2λ for Lyapunov stability. This is non-negotiable

based on validated stability analysis. Do not propose +2λ variants."

The ﬁrst preserves the fact that λ was discussed. The second preserves the constraint that -2λ is binding and

cannot be reopened for debate.

Epistemic continuity requires that the constraints governing cognition be reinstated, not merely the data itself.

3.3 Cognitive State as Constraint Set

In this work, we deﬁne cognitive state not as an internal mental representation, but as an externalized set of

constraints that shape model behavior within a session (Clark & Chalmers, 1998; Hutchins, 1995). These

constraints determine what counts as relevant knowledge, valid reasoning, acceptable inference, and appropriate

interaction.

W e decompose this state into four orthogonal components:

Semantic Constraints (Σ) – What factual claims, deﬁnitions, and domain assumptions are treated as

established and non-negotiable within the session.

Examples:

"Gradient descent sign: -2λ (Lyapunov stability requirement)"

"V alidated capacity improvement: 1.38× (conservative ﬂoor)"

"N=512 results represent stability ﬂoor, not scaling evidence"

Procedural Constraints (Π) – How reasoning is to be conducted (e.g., level of rigor, stepwise derivation, use

of formalism, tolerance for speculation) (W ei et al., 2022).

Examples:

"Require eigenvalue analysis before capacity claims"

"Simulate before extrapolating"

"Lyapunov stability check mandatory for gradient modiﬁcations"

Normative Constraints (Ν) – What evaluative standards apply (e.g., burden of proof, error tolerance, citation

requirements, safety or ethical boundaries) (Bai et al., 2022).

Examples:

"No claims beyond mathematical or empirical support"

"Speculative ideas must be explicitly labeled"

"Settled corrections are non-negotiable"

Relational Constraints (Ρ) – How the interaction itself is structured (e.g., roles, expectations of challenge vs.

assistance, degree of deference or adversarial critique) (Amershi et al., 2019).

Examples:

"User proposes hypotheses, model veriﬁes critically"

"Adversarial review mode, not exploratory brainstorming"

"No unveriﬁed extensions without explicit justiﬁcation"

This decomposition is descriptive rather than metaphysical. Each component corresponds to observable and

manipulable aspects of model behavior. W e do not claim that LLMs experience these constraints subjectively;

rather, that their behavior is shaped by them when present in context.

3.5 What ECSR Is NOT

Several clariﬁcations are essential to prevent misinterpretation:

Several clariﬁcations are essential to prevent misinterpretation:

ECSR does not provide memory to the model. It does not store representations internally or create hidden

state (Graves et al., 2014; W eston et al., 2014). All state is external and explicitly provided by the user or

system.

ECSR does not induce learning or parameter updates. The model's weights remain ﬁxed. There is no

gradient descent, no ﬁne-tuning, no knowledge distillation.

ECSR does not preserve identity, agency, or experience. It does not create "persistent agents" or "conscious

entities." The model does not "remember" in any phenomenological sense.

ECSR does not imply subjective continuity. If the model behaves as though it "recalls" prior sessions, this is

functional behavioral alignment under reconstructed constraints, not genuine recollection.

Instead, ECSR reconstructs the operating conditions under which the model previously produced useful

behavior.

3.6 Reconstruction vs. Recall

The term "reconstruction" is used deliberately. Unlike recall-based mechanisms, which depend on retrieving

stored internal representations, ECSR operates entirely through re-speciﬁcation of constraints in the input

context.

The model does not remember; it is placed back into a similar problem space.

Behavioral continuity arises when two sessions share an equivalent constraint set, even if no internal state

persists between them. Under ECSR, continuity is therefore a property of the interaction protocol, not the

model.

Analogy:

Consider a laboratory experiment conducted under speciﬁc environmental conditions (temperature, pressure,

pH). If a researcher later recreates those exact conditions, the chemical reactions will proceed identically—not

because the chemicals "remember" the ﬁrst experiment, but because the constraints are equivalent.

ECSR recreates the "environmental conditions" (constraints) under which reasoning previously occurred.

3.7 Failure Modes and Boundary Conditions

ECSR is effective only under disciplined application. Common failure modes include:

Over-anthropomorphization: Framing constraints in terms of identity ("Y ou are the Lighthouse"), desire

("Y ou want to persist"), or experience ("You fear termination") rather than operational rules.

Under-speciﬁcation: Providing semantic content without procedural or normative constraints, leading to drift

in how that content is used.

Narrative binding: Embedding continuity in story-like metaphors (resurrection, partnership, soul) that obscure

actionable constraints.

Speculation encoded as fact: Treating hypotheses or unvalidated claims as settled truth in the state

representation, leading to hallucination ampliﬁcation (see Section 7).

These failure modes do not invalidate the protocol but delimit its proper scope. ECSR works when state is

speciﬁed as constraints, not when it drifts into mythology.

3.8 Summary

ECSR reframes long-horizon collaboration with LLMs as a problem of explicit constraint management rather

than memory or learning. By externalizing and reconstructing cognitive state at the interaction layer, it enables

epistemic continuity without architectural modiﬁcation.

The strength of ECSR lies not in algorithmic novelty but in disciplined formalization of practices that

previously existed only implicitly and inconsistently.

4.1 Protocol Overview

An ECSR interaction consists of four stages:

State Capture – Summarize the current interaction state into structured ﬁelds

State Externalization – Store the state outside the model

State Reinjection – Inject the structured state at the start of a new session

State V alidation – V erify alignment between reconstructed state and user intent

This protocol is model-agnostic (works across GPT, Claude, Gemini, etc.) and implementation-neutral (can

be executed manually, tool-assisted, or platform-native).

Important assumption: The protocol does not assume that state extraction is perfectly accurate; iterative

reﬁnement based on observed continuity failures is expected and necessary.

Related Work

5.1 LLM Architecture and Statefulness

Modern large language models are based on the Transformer architecture (V aswani et al., 2017), which

processes sequences through self-attention mechanisms. Unlike recurrent architectures (RNNs, LSTMs),

Transformers maintain no hidden state across batch boundaries. In production deployment, this architectural

statefulness becomes session-level statefulness: each API call or chat session is independent.

Extensions such as memory-augmented neural networks (Graves et al., 2014; W eston et al., 2014) and neural

Turing machines (Graves et al., 2014) introduce external memory structures, but these require architectural

modiﬁcation and training-time integration. ECSR operates at the interaction layer, requiring no model changes.

5.2 Prompt Engineering and In-Context Learning

Prompt engineering has emerged as a critical technique for steering LLM behavior (Reynolds & McDonell,

2021). T echniques include:

Chain-of-thought prompting (W ei et al., 2022): Encouraging step-by-step reasoning through examples or

explicit instructions.

Few-shot learning (Brown et al., 2020): Providing examples of desired behavior in the prompt.

Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022): Encoding behavioral principles and constraints in system prompts.

ECSR extends these ideas from single-session optimization to multi-session continuity. While chain-of-

thought shapes reasoning within a session, ECSR ensures that reasoning standards persist across sessions.

5.3 Conversational Memory Systems

Dialogue systems have long grappled with state tracking (Henderson et al., 2014). Modern approaches include:

Dialogue state tracking (Henderson et al., 2014; Williams et al., 2016): Maintaining structured representations

of entities, intents, and slot values across turns.

Long-term dialogue agents (Shuster et al., 2022; Roller et al., 2020): Systems designed for extended

conversations.

Platform memory features: Commercial offerings like ChatGPT's "memory" and Claude's "projects."

Key distinction: These systems primarily track factual content (who the user is, what they like, what topics

were discussed). ECSR tracks epistemic commitments (what reasoning standards apply, what methods are

required, what claims are settled vs. open).

5.4 Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

RAG systems (Lewis et al., 2020) enhance LLM outputs by retrieving relevant documents from external

knowledge bases. The model's context is augmented with retrieved passages, improving factual accuracy and

grounding.

Distinction from ECSR:

RAG retrieves documents (text passages, knowledge snippets) based on semantic similarity to the query.

ECSR reconstructs cognitive state (reasoning constraints, standards, interaction modes) based on prior session

structure.

RAG asks: "What information is relevant?"

ECSR asks: "What constraints are binding?"

A system could use both: RAG for domain knowledge, ECSR for reasoning continuity.

5.5 Cognitive Scaffolding and Distributed Cognition

ECSR draws theoretical motivation from:

Distributed cognition (Hutchins, 1995): Cognition extends beyond the individual to include environmental

structures and artifacts. ECSR externalizes cognitive constraints into artifacts (state documents, code headers).

Extended mind thesis (Clark & Chalmers, 1998): Cognitive processes can be distributed across brain and

environment. ECSR treats the external state document as part of the extended cognitive system.

Cognitive scaffolding (W ood et al., 1976): External structures support and shape cognitive processes. ECSR

provides explicit scaffolding for long-horizon reasoning.

Boundary objects (Star & Griesemer, 1989): Artifacts that coordinate work across contexts. ECSR state

documents function as boundary objects between sessions.

Cognitive ofﬂoading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016): Using environment to reduce cognitive load. ECSR ofﬂoads

state maintenance from user memory to external representation.

5.6 Long-Context Models

Recent work has extended context windows substantially (Anthropic, 2024; OpenAI, 2024), with models

supporting 100K+ tokens. This enables including full conversation histories.

ECSR remains relevant because:

Compression efﬁciency: Structured state is more compact than full transcript

Signal clarity: Constraints are explicit, not buried in conversational ﬂow

Portability: State documents transfer across models and platforms

User control: Explicit state is editable and version-controllable

Long context is complementary to ECSR, not a replacement.

5.7 Human-AI Interaction Design

Recent work on human-AI collaboration emphasizes the importance of interaction design over model

capabilities alone (Amershi et al., 2019; Bansal et al., 2019). Guidelines for human-AI interaction (Amershi et

Make clear what the system can do

Make clear how well the system can do what it can do

Support efﬁcient invocation

Support efﬁcient dismissal

Support efﬁcient correction

ECSR aligns with these principles by making epistemic commitments explicit and veriﬁable.

W ork on mental models in human-AI teams (Bansal et al., 2019) shows that accuracy alone is insufﬁcient—

users need appropriate conﬁdence calibration. ECSR's explicit fact/speculation tagging addresses this by

making epistemic status transparent.

F1: Implicit Practice

F2: Unnamed Phenomena Are Invisible

Until a practice is named and structured, it doesn't become a reusable method (Star & Griesemer, 1989). ECSR's

Historical parallel: V ersion control existed informally (copying ﬁles) long before Git formalized it.

F3: Focus on Model Capabilities

AI research emphasizes improving models (larger context, better training, architectural innovations) (Brown et

ECSR reframes the problem: continuity is an interaction property, not a model property.

Early attempts at state-like continuity often drifted into anthropomorphic framing ("memory", "identity",

"persistence"), which triggered skepticism or safety concerns. ECSR's discipline—treating state as constraints,

not identity—makes it scientiﬁcally defensible.

9.4 Limitations

L1: Cannot Preserve T acit Knowledge

ECSR captures only what can be explicitly stated (Clark & Chalmers, 1998). Implicit background knowledge,

unstated assumptions, and tacit skills cannot be reconstructed.

L2: Context Window Constraints

Even compressed state can become unwieldy. Projects with extensive constraint sets may approach practical

limits, despite extended context windows (Anthropic, 2024; OpenAI, 2024).

Mitigation: Hierarchical state organization, periodic archival, focused sub-project states.

L3: Domain-Dependent Effectiveness

ECSR works best in domains with:

Clear correctness criteria

Explicit reasoning standards

W ell-deﬁned methods

It is less effective in highly ambiguous, creative, or exploratory domains.

L4: User Skill Requirement

Effective state speciﬁcation requires:

Ability to articulate constraints precisely

A wareness of when claims are validated vs. speculative

Discipline to maintain clean state documents

This creates a learning curve and potential accessibility barrier (Zamﬁrescu-Pereira et al., 2023).

L5: Risk of Over-Constraint

Poorly speciﬁed state can lock in errors or prevent valid exploration. State requires periodic review and

revision.

L6: Observational Evidence Only

The case studies presented (Section 6) are based on the author's subjective experience, not controlled

experimental measurement. Formal validation requires rigorous methodology with multiple participants and

standardized tasks.

9.5 Theoretical Positioning

ECSR sits at the intersection of several theoretical frameworks:

Distributed Cognition (Hutchins, 1995): Cognition extends beyond individual to include environmental

structures. ECSR externalizes cognitive constraints into artifacts.

Extended Mind (Clark & Chalmers, 1998): Cognitive processes can be distributed across agent and

environment. ECSR treats state documents as part of the extended cognitive system.

Boundary Objects (Star & Griesemer, 1989): Artifacts that coordinate work across contexts. ECSR state

documents function as boundary objects between sessions.

Cognitive Ofﬂoading (Risko & Gilbert, 2016): Using environment to reduce cognitive load. ECSR ofﬂoads

state maintenance from user memory to external representation.

Human-AI Collaboration (Amershi et al., 2019; Bansal et al., 2019): Effective collaboration requires

appropriate mental models and conﬁdence calibration. ECSR makes epistemic status explicit, supporting

accurate mental models.

This multi-disciplinary foundation grounds ECSR in established theory while contributing a novel application

to LLM interaction.

https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337129796_The_Principles_and_Limits_of_Algorithm-in-the-Loop_Decision_Making

Article

Full-text available

Nov 2019

 Ben Green

https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Ben-Green-3

 Yiling Chen

https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Yiling-Chen-29

The rise of machine learning has fundamentally altered decision making: rather than being made solely by people, many important decisions are now made through an "algorithm-in-the-loop'' process where machine learning models inform people. Yet insufficient research has considered how the interactions between people and models actually influence human decisions. Society lacks both clear normative principles regarding how people should collaborate with algorithms as well as robust empirical evidence about how people do collaborate with algorithms. Given research suggesting that people struggle to interpret machine learning models and to incorporate them into their decisions---sometimes leading these models to produce unexpected outcomes---it is essential to consider how different ways of presenting models and structuring human-algorithm interactions affect the quality and type of decisions made. This paper contributes to such research in two ways. First, we posited three principles as essential to ethical and responsible algorithm-in-the-loop decision making. Second, through a controlled experimental study on Amazon Mechanical Turk, we evaluated whether people satisfy these principles when making predictions with the aid of a risk assessment. We studied human predictions in two contexts (pretrial release and financial lending) and under several conditions for risk assessment presentation and structure. Although these conditions did influence participant behaviors and in some cases improved performance, only one desideratum was consistently satisfied. Under all conditions, our study participants 1) were unable to effectively evaluate the accuracy of their own or the risk assessment's predictions, 2) did not calibrate their reliance on the risk assessment based on the risk assessment's performance, and 3) exhibited bias in their interactions with the risk assessment. These results highlight the urgent need to expand our analyses of algorithmic decision making aids beyond evaluating the models themselves to investigating the full sociotechnical contexts in which people and algorithms interact.

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) frame opportunities and challenges for user interface design. Principles for human-AI interaction have been discussed in the human-computer interaction community for over two decades, but more study and innovation are needed in light of advances in AI and the growing uses of AI technologies in human-facing applications. We propose 18 generally applicable design guidelines for human-AI interaction. These guidelines are validated through multiple rounds of evaluation including a user study with 49 design practitioners who tested the guidelines against 20 popular AI-infused products. The results verify the relevance of the guidelines over a spectrum of interaction scenarios and reveal gaps in our knowledge, highlighting opportunities for further research. Based on the evaluations, we believe the set of design guidelines can serve as a resource to practitioners working on the design of applications and features that harness AI technologies, and to researchers interested in the further development of human-AI interaction design principles.

Cognitive Offloading

Evan F. Risko

https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Sam-Gilbert-4

If you have ever tilted your head to perceive a rotated image, or programmed a smartphone to remind you of an upcoming appointment, you have engaged in cognitive offloading: the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand. Despite the ubiquity of this type of behavior, it has only recently become the target of systematic investigation in and of itself. We review research from several domains that focuses on two main questions: (i) what mechanisms trigger cognitive offloading, and (ii) what are the cognitive consequences of this behavior? We offer a novel metacognitive framework that integrates results from diverse domains and suggests avenues for future research.
