indices like the S&P 500 for US large cap stocks or the S&P TSX composite for Canadian stocks are groups of stocks weighted by the market value of the companies that are included in the index They're designed to represent a stock market or a part of a stock market To track an index's performance over time each index has a base level For example the S&P 500 index has a base level of 10 starting in 1941 to43 Each day the index's return is calculated based on the performance of the stocks in the index and then a new index level is recorded Over time that number gets bigger when returns are positive and smaller when they're negative Today the level of the S&P 500 is over 7,000 Each time the level of a major index surpasses its previous all-time high the media tends to report on it The funny thing though is that the level of the index is kind of meaningless There are a few issues One is that index levels are typically calculated based on stock prices only not dividends They're priceonly indices rather than total return indices We would actually see more all-time highs if dividends were included in the index level calculations that are commonly reported on Academic research on this shows that the price only presentation of index data affects how people think about the stock market For example for a given level of returns newspaper coverage is more negative when dividends are higher because the price drops when a dividend is paid But economically it's not a bad thing because you received the dividend The price went down but you received the dividend It's kind of a well recently it often continues to do well for a bit We do see this in the data All-time highs are more frequently followed by all-time highs than by market drops or crashes The opposite is also true highs has historically resulted in positive returns on average The other issue that often coincides with all-time highs in the level of the stock market index is high stock market valuations The Schiller cyclically adjusted price earnings ratio is a common metric for measuring how expensive a stock market is When you invest in the stock market you're buying the discounted future cash flows of real businesses Basically you're buying the future earnings of companies at a discount today and your expected return is the discount rate The way that math works you have a lower expected return when
