# Sleep Apnea Non-CPAP Interventions

## Decision context

**Definition:** Sleep apnea is a disorder characterized by repeated breathing interruptions during sleep, linked to heart disease, diabetes, and reduced quality of life. Multiple non-CPAP approaches have been developed to address underlying causes such as airway collapse, breathing pattern dysregulation, and upper airway muscle tone.

## Operational details

- Breathing retraining exercises during daytime, including box breathing and techniques with exhale twice as long as inhale, aim to calm reactionary fast breathing that contributes to airway collapse at night.
- The geniohyoid muscle (distinct from the tongue) has been identified at Stanford as a target for improving airway patency; surgical studies demonstrated that tightening this muscle can reduce breathing disturbances by 68%.
- Myofunctional exercises (tongue and upper airway exercises) have been proposed as a non-invasive approach to improve muscle tone without surgery.
- Sleeping position modification, particularly incline bed therapy using bricks to elevate the head of the bed, has been shown in research to reduce apnea events by approximately 50% (from 16 to 8 events per hour).
- Oral appliances (mouth guards) that maintain jaw position are cited as alternatives to CPAP for keeping the airway open.
- Nasal breathing improvement through mouth tape or nasal dilators represents another non-CPAP option.
- Magnesium supplementation for sleep apnea requires caution due to electrolyte interactions; recommendations include kidney function testing, limiting elemental magnesium intake to a single source, and preferring topical forms.
- Peptide protocols targeting metabolic and recovery systems have shown individual case results reducing AHI from 35 to 2 (severe to normal range) over two months of weekly administration.
- Health Canada recently approved the first injection treatment for sleep apnea.
- Research involving 3,500 CPAP patients conducted by Harvard-affiliated researchers has examined how cardiovascular risk varies based on sleep apnea severity and CPAP usage, raising questions about optimal treatment thresholds.
